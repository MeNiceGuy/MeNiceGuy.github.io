How to Start a Home Care Agency in North Carolina (The $510 Fee and the Two License Tracks)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 31, 2026

The North Carolina story usually starts with a founder in Charlotte, Raleigh, or Greensboro who has already picked a name, priced out an office, and found the license application. The fee looks simple: $510, one check to the state. Then the first real question lands, and it is not about money. Will your caregivers touch the client? Help with a bath, a transfer, getting dressed? In North Carolina that single answer decides which of two licenses you need, which rules your staff work under, and whether a registered nurse has to stand behind your aides. File the wrong track and the $510 is gone, because the fee is nonrefundable either way.

That fork is the moment this video was made for, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against a North Carolina primary source today. Three facts have moved since the video was recorded, and the article follows the verified facts. The senior wave arrives sooner than the video said: the state demographer now puts one in five residents at age 65 or older by 2030, not 2031. The price of care is higher than the video said: the CareScout median for a non-medical caregiver is now $68,640 a year, about $30 an hour, not $23. And the state publishes no 90 to 180 day timeline for this license at all. The only clock DHSR guarantees is the one that can end your application: twelve months to finish everything, or start over.

The demand: about 2.12 million seniors, and the fastest growth in the Southeast

Start with why North Carolina draws new owners. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 11,197,968 as of July 1, 2025, up 7.2 percent from the 2020 estimates base, and 18.9 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 2.12 million seniors today. The same Census QuickFacts page for North Carolina puts median household income at $72,388 for 2020 to 2024. Private pay home care is bought out of household income by families who need help at home, and North Carolina keeps adding both households and seniors faster than almost any state in the region.

The forward numbers are the real draw, and they come from the state demographer, not a marketing deck. The NC Office of State Budget and Management Demographic Outlook projects that between 2025 and 2030 the number of adults age 65 and older will grow at three times the rate of the total population, reaching 2.4 million older adults by 2030, an increase of about 282,000 people, or 14 percent. At that point one in five North Carolinians will be 65 or older, and older adults will outnumber children. The NC Division of Aging 2024 State Aging Profile, built on OSBM projections, carries the line further: from 2,016,408 residents age 65 and older in 2024 to a projected 2,850,072 by 2044, a 41 percent increase, with the age 85 and older population more than doubling. The wave in the video is real. On current state numbers it peaks later and lasts longer than the video described.

The price families face explains the business case. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for North Carolina, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $68,640, flat against 2024 and ranked 43rd among states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $30 an hour. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows a state where care costs less than the national median while the senior population grows at three times the overall rate, which is exactly the gap new agencies form to fill.

Two licenses, and the dividing line is touch

North Carolina licenses this business through the Division of Health Service Regulation, called DHSR, inside the Department of Health and Human Services. The mandate sits in G.S. 131E-138(a): no person or governmental unit shall operate a home care agency or a home assistance services provider without a license obtained from the Department. Two license types in one sentence, because since Session Law 2023-150 there are two tracks, and the UNC School of Government summary of that law draws the line the same way the statute does: home assistance services are services that do not require the provider to touch the client.

Track one is the home care agency license. It covers home care services under G.S. 131E-136, and for a non-medical startup the load-bearing service is in-home aide services. The rules define that term at 10A NCAC 13J .0901(13): hands-on services that assist individuals with home management tasks, personal care tasks, or supervision of the client activities. Bathing, dressing, toileting, transfers, help with walking: if your aides do any of it, you are on this track, and companion, sitter, and respite care you provide alongside hands-on care rides on the same license. This track carries the full rulebook in 10A NCAC 13J, including health care practitioner supervision and the competency rules below.

Track two is the home assistance services provider license. It covers in-home companion, sitter, or respite care, plus homemaker services such as basic home management, shopping, meal preparation, transportation, socialization, and medication reminders, provided in combination with companion care and with no hands-on care at all. The statute states plainly that home assistance services are not home care services. A companion only business is not exempt in North Carolina. It is licensed on the lighter track, and the moment one aide helps one client out of a chair, the business has crossed into home care agency territory and needs the other license.

The lighter track is licensed, not unregulated

Home assistance providers carry their own statutory duties in G.S. 131E-138(h). A provider must maintain liability insurance; inform all new clients that it does not provide health care or hands-on care; run a criminal background check in each state where any contractor or employee was a resident during the past seven years; document that each employee or contractor holds a valid driver license, vehicle registration, and proof of insurance, and has provided an official driving record from the NC Division of Motor Vehicles; make sure services are provided in a safe and appropriate manner that complies with the law; and attest every year that the provider is in compliance with those requirements.

The prohibitions in G.S. 131E-138(i) matter just as much to the business model. A home assistance services provider may not submit claims to any private or government funded health insurer, managed care organization, or health plan for services to a North Carolina resident. It may not employ anyone convicted of a relevant offense, and it may not employ anyone listed on the National Sex Offender Public Website. Plan a companion business around insurance billing and the statute blocks the model itself, not just the paperwork. Private pay, with the no-touch line held hard, is what this license is built for.

The money and the clock: $510, nonrefundable, twelve months to finish

The fee is set in statute, not by a fee schedule a clerk can change. Under G.S. 131E-138(c), the Department charges a nonrefundable annual license fee of $510. The same amount comes due at renewal every year. There is no Certificate of Need step in the home care path: the DHSR procedure for establishing a home care agency runs entirely through licensure, application, document review, and approval. The Certificate of Need process belongs to the separate home health side of the industry, where a new certified home health agency is a different business with a different gate. A non-medical home care founder budgets for the license process itself, not a need fight.

The process is paper based and sequenced. Per the DHSR procedure page, the applicant downloads and completes the licensure application, includes the fee as a check, money order, or certified check payable to the N.C. Division of Health Service Regulation, and includes proof of previously owning or operating a home care agency, or proof of completion of home care provider training, then mails the packet to DHSR in Raleigh. The section reviews the packet for accuracy and completeness, emails confirmation of receipt, then emails a request for the policies, procedures, and personnel documents. The initial survey is a review of those documents for compliance with the licensure rules. If the survey completes with no need for additional information, the applicant gets an approval email with instructions to access and print the license through the Enterprise system.

Two timing facts replace the timeline in the video. First, DHSR publishes no promised processing window for this license, so any specific day count you hear is an estimate from someone else experience, not a state commitment. Second, the hard deadline is real and it is yours: an applicant must be able to complete all necessary requirements within one year, twelve months, from the date DHSR receives the initial application and fee. Build a plan that assumes the document rounds take months, keep your training proof, policies, and personnel files moving inside that year, and never let the application sit waiting on a document you control.

The first-time owner gate: training before the license can issue

North Carolina adds a gate most states do not have. Under 10A NCAC 13J .0903(a), the application goes in before any initial licensure survey is scheduled, and if the applicant cannot demonstrate to DHSR that he or she has ever owned or operated a home care agency before applying, the Division shall not issue a license until the applicant has received training approved by the Division. The rule names the curriculum: the requirements for licensure, the licensure process, and the rules pertaining to the operation of a home care agency. That is why the application packet asks for proof of prior ownership or operation, or proof of training completion, up front. A first-time founder who skips the training has not saved time. He has built a stop into his own application that only surfaces after the fee is paid.

The license itself is narrow in ways that shape the business plan. Under 10A NCAC 13J .0902 and .0903(b), each agency premises must be separately licensed, licensure runs for one year, and each license expires at midnight on the expiration date printed on the license and is renewable on application. Under .0903(d), the license is issued for the premises and persons named in the application and is not transferable, and the services the agency provides are printed on it. Budget per site, and understand that adding a service category later runs through advance notice to the Department and a compliance determination under .0903(g), not through simply starting the work.

The survey is a document review, and DHSR tells you how applicants fail it

Most founders picture an inspector walking their office. The North Carolina initial survey is a review of the policies, procedures, and personnel documents the applicant submits, measured against 10A NCAC 13J. DHSR states the failure pattern on its own procedure page: the primary reason home care licensure applicants are not approved for licensure is because they do not understand or comply with the initial survey checklist or the NC licensure rules. The department tells applicants to review the Initial Home Care Survey Checklist and use it as the guide, and warns that the checklist states minimal requirements and that failure to comply may delay licensure.

The policy load is concrete. Under 10A NCAC 13J .1001(a), the agency governing body must establish and implement policies covering the scope of services offered; admission and discharge; supervision of personnel; development of and updates to the plan of care; management of emergency care situations in the home; the time frame for completing and returning service records; personnel qualifications; an organizational chart; program evaluation; employee and client confidentiality; and coordination of and referral to and from other community agencies and resources. Eleven named areas, each one a document a surveyor can score. The article version of the warning is simple: in North Carolina the license is won or lost on paper before the first client is ever visited.

The people rules: director, supervisor, and the practitioner behind the aides

Leadership has a rulebook too. Under 10A NCAC 13J .1001(b), the agency designates an agency director with authority and responsibility for administrative direction, and the director must meet at least one of three qualifications: be a health care practitioner as defined in G.S. 90-640(a); hold at least two years of supervisory or management experience in home care or another provider licensed under G.S. 131E or G.S. 122C; or hold a bachelor degree in health, business, or public administration science plus at least one year of supervisory or management experience in home care or another licensed health care program. Under .1001(c), the agency also designates a person responsible for supervising each type of home care service it provides, and that supervisor may also serve as director. A founder who meets none of the three director paths has a hiring decision to make before the application, not after.

For in-home aide services, the clinical spine is the health care practitioner. Under 10A NCAC 13J .1107(d) and (e), the initial assessment is conducted in the client home by the health care practitioner before the plan of care is developed, and the plan of care is signed and dated by the practitioner and the client or the client responsible party, with a copy accessible in the home. Under .1107(f), the agency must provide availability of the health care practitioner for supervision and consultation. Under Rule .1110, every in-home caregiver is assigned only tasks the agency has documented them competent to perform, competency includes a demonstration of tasks to the health care practitioner, and for caregivers not covered by an occupational license the practitioner makes a supervisory visit to each client residence every 90 days, and annually while the caregiver is providing care, with documented findings. Personnel files carry their own proof: a baseline tuberculosis skin test for hands-on care employees under .1003(b), no hiring of anyone with a substantiated finding on the NC Health Care Personnel Registry under .1003(c), and, for in-home aides not listed on the Nurse Aide Registry who were hired after April 1, 2009, registered nurse verification of the six core personal care skills under .1003(g): assisting with mobility, bathing and showering, toileting, dressing, eating, and continence needs.

The rule that decides who can take which client: extensive versus limited assistance

Here is the North Carolina rule that gets misstated most often, because the general version sounds simpler than the actual one. Under 10A NCAC 13J .1107(a) and (b), in-home aide services are provided under the client plan of care, and the plan of care must state the level of assistance the client requires for each activity of daily living. Activities of daily living in these rules are mobility, eating, bathing, dressing, and toileting, per .0901(1). The split is this. If the plan of care requires the in-home aide to provide extensive assistance, the aide shall be listed on the Nurse Aide Registry under G.S. 131E-255. If the plan of care requires the aide to provide only limited assistance, the aide is not required to be listed on the Registry.

The definitions decide real assignments. Extensive assistance, under .0901(7), means the client is totally dependent or requires hands-on assistance more than half the time while performing part of an activity, and meets one of three criteria: extensive assistance in more than two ADLs; a need for the aide to perform at least one task at the nurse aide II level; or extensive assistance in more than one ADL combined with a medical or cognitive impairment. Limited assistance, under .0901(18), means hands-on care involving guided maneuvering of limbs, with help needed half the time or less during the activity, and the client does not meet the extensive definition. So the staffing question is never just whether an aide is good. It is what the plan of care says for each ADL, client by client. Assign a non-registry aide to an extensive assistance plan and the agency is out of compliance on a rule the surveyor can read straight off the file. One more boundary belongs in the plan: under .1107(h), the service area for in-home aide supervision is limited to the county where the agency is located, the counties touching it, or within 90 minutes driving time from the agency site, whichever is greater. Growth past that line means a new licensed site, not a longer drive.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every North Carolina owner. The expensive mistakes in this state happen at the fork and on the calendar, not in the client home. Choose the home assistance track and then let one aide help one client stand up, and you are operating a home care agency without its license. Choose the agency track as a first-time owner and skip the approved training, and the license cannot issue at all under .0903(a). File either application and drift, and the twelve month window closes with the $510 spent and nothing transferable to show for it, because the fee is nonrefundable and the license, once issued, names the premises and persons on the application. And staff to the general idea of aide competency instead of the plan of care test in .1107(b), and a single extensive assistance client can put your whole aide roster on the wrong side of the Nurse Aide Registry rule. Decide the track by the tasks your aides will actually perform, finish the training first if you are new, and build every plan of care to state its assistance level ADL by ADL.

Your next step

Start with the task list, not the application. Write down every task your caregivers will perform in a client home in your first ninety days, and mark each one hands-on or no-touch. That one list chooses your license track, your director and supervisor plan, and your training path. If you have never owned or operated a home care agency, complete the DHSR approved training and keep the proof for the packet. Then download the Initial Home Care Survey Checklist from DHSR and build your eleven policy areas, personnel files, and competency documentation to that checklist before you mail anything, with the $510 fee in the form DHSR accepts. An owner who files in that order uses the twelve months for approval, not for avoidable corrections.
