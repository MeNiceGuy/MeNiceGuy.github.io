How to Start a Home Care Agency in Iowa (No License for Home Care or Home Health, One Voluntary Hospice License)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Iowa story starts with a founder in Des Moines who found a license that does not cover her business and nearly paid for it anyway. A guide had pointed her at chapter 135J, and the chapter is real, and it does license a kind of home-based care. It licenses hospice programs only. The first thing I tell owners looking at Iowa is that this state splits home-based care the opposite way from most states: there is no Iowa license for non-medical home care, and no Iowa license for a home health agency either. The second thing I tell them is where the real gates sit, in Medicaid enrollment and HCBS provider certification for in-home work, in Medicare certification for skilled care, and in the one voluntary state license in the family, the hospice license. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

Iowa is therefore a payer-door state. The founder who builds the enrollment, certification, and screening systems first is ready when the first referral calls. The founder hunting a license application is hunting a document Iowa does not print. The sections below follow the Iowa packet, which is built from Iowa primary sources checked on November 17, 2026.

The demand: about 651,000 seniors, one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Iowa. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 3,238,387 as of July 1, 2025, and 20.1 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 651,000 seniors, one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Iowa puts median household income at $75,059 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into. An older age profile spread across small cities and farm counties means demand arrives through hospitals, clinics, churches, and word of mouth as much as through search, and an agency built to be found by referral sources holds an edge here.

The license question, answered directly: Iowa licenses neither home care nor home health agencies

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Iowa? No Iowa license exists for it. No Iowa Code chapter licenses an agency for sending personal care workers into client homes, and no chapter licenses a home health agency either: the Medicaid rules at 441 IAC 77.37 and 78.9 speak only of Medicare-certified home health agencies, and the Department of Inspections, Appeals, and Licensing, called DIAL, acts through its Health Facilities Division as the state survey agency for federal certification rather than as a licensor of home-based agencies. A private pay personal care agency forms as a business and operates without a state home care license, because Iowa prints none.

The regulated door for the same work is Medicaid. Homemaker, home health aide, and supported community living services are covered lines of the HCBS waivers at 441 IAC chapters 78 and 83, delivered by providers enrolled with Iowa Medicaid and certified as HCBS providers under 441 IAC chapter 77, inside the Department of Health and Human Services. The one state license in this family is the hospice program license under chapter 135J, and section 135J.2 prints that nothing in that chapter prohibits a person from establishing, conducting, or maintaining a hospice program without a license, so the hospice license is a voluntary state credential. No certificate of need attaches to any home-based track either: section 135.63 gates new institutional health services, nursing facilities above all, and home-based agencies are not institutional health facilities.

The non-medical lane: business formation, then Medicaid enrollment and HCBS certification

The non-medical sequence starts with the business itself. Form the entity, hold the EIN, put insurance and the operating accounts in place, and write the service agreements that carry private pay work, with the service boundary stated plainly: homemaker and personal care help, no skilled tasks. For Medicaid work, enroll with Iowa Medicaid and certify as an HCBS provider under 441 IAC chapters 77, 78, and 79. Iowa Medicaid prints one enrollment clock worth planning around: the provider is notified of the decision within 30 calendar days of receipt of a complete and correct application, under 441 IAC 79.15(5). Complete and correct is the operative phrase; an incomplete application does not start that clock in any way a founder can bank on.

No license fee exists on this door, so the packet prints NO FEE PUBLISHED for it. Iowa Medicaid provider enrollment carries the federal application fee frame at 441 IAC 79.15(2), and no separate Iowa dollar figure for HCBS personal care enrollment was located in the sources checked, so none is printed here. Two nearby figures are traps. The health care employment agency registration under chapter 135Q charges $500 for each separate location per year, and it covers an agency that contracts with a health care entity to provide agency workers; it authorizes no home care services, and budgeting it as a home care fee buys a staffing credential for a care business. Secondary guides also print a home health agency license and a homemaker-home health aide agency license under 641 IAC chapters 24 and 25; neither is a DIAL licensure door in the current structure, and no fee attaches to either name. Iowa Health Link plan lists go stale the same way: the current health plans are Iowa Total Care, Molina Healthcare of Iowa, and Wellpoint Iowa, and a contracting plan built on an older roster needs a fresh check.

The skilled lane: Medicare certification is the credential, DIAL is the survey agency

Skilled care in Iowa runs on the federal credential. The operative sequence is business formation, Medicare certification where the payer mix requires it, and Iowa Medicaid provider enrollment under 441 IAC 79.15. There is no state application, no state survey for licensure, and no state fee for the skilled door as such. Homemaker providers under the waivers qualify as Medicare-certified home health agencies or through a public health local-services contract, which tells a founder exactly how the state expects the skilled and waiver worlds to connect. Medicare enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS, with the CY2026 federal institutional enrollment fee of $750. The Home Health and Hospice MAC for Iowa is CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, and the A/B MAC is WPS Government Health Administrators, Jurisdiction 5. Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction D, is the DME MAC, a different door.

Initial Medicare enrollment on the certified tracks is gated by the federal enrollment moratoria, CMS-6101-N2 for home health and CMS-6102-N2 for hospice, extended and modified effective November 12, 2026 (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). No Iowa-initiated Medicaid provider enrollment moratorium was located in the primary sources checked on November 17, 2026, and the packet prints no Iowa Medicaid pause. Iowa Code section 249A.48 and 441 IAC 79.15(15) require Iowa Medicaid to impose a moratorium CMS identifies, and that federal mirror is printed on the certified tracks, not as a separate state pause. Do not import another state pause into an Iowa plan.

The hospice lane: the one state license in the family, and it is voluntary

Hospice is where Iowa does print a license, and the details reward a careful read. Under chapter 135J and 481 IAC chapter 53, a person may establish, conduct, or maintain a hospice program and receive a license from DIAL after meeting the chapter requirements, and section 135J.2 states the voluntary frame on its face. The license runs two years, under section 135J.6 and rule 53.2(9). The nonrefundable biennial license fee is $500 under rule 53.2(2), with an additional 25 percent fee where the license lapses for failure to make timely renewal application. The licensure survey runs before the initial license issues and at least every 36 months after, so survey scheduling, not a printed processing figure, is the operative timeline fact on this track.

The trap on this door is reading the voluntary license as a payer door. Medicare certification under 42 CFR Part 418 and Iowa Medicaid hospice enrollment decide payment, and each stands on its own terms; a licensed hospice that never certified or enrolled holds a credential and no revenue door, and a certified hospice that never took the state license operates inside the frame section 135J.2 allows. The name trap runs with it: chapter 135J is the hospice chapter, and it says nothing about a home health agency license, because Iowa prints none.

The residential doors: certified programs and licensed facilities

Founders who want a home meet a certified Iowa map. Assisted living programs are certified under chapter 231C at $750 for a two-year initial certification. Elder group homes, three through five elders, are certified under chapter 231B at the two-year certification fees the chapter prints. Adult day services programs are certified under chapter 231D at $750 for a three-year initial certification. Residential care facilities are licensed under 481 IAC chapters 57 and 60 at the tiered facility fee under section 135C.7, an annual fee by bed capacity from twenty dollars to one hundred dollars, and nursing facilities sit behind the certificate of need at section 135.63, at an application fee of three-tenths of one percent of anticipated project cost, minimum $600, maximum $21,000. HCBS residential based supported community living group homes run on HCBS certification under 441 IAC 78.41, printed as no fee published. Every one of those figures belongs to its own door and is never borrowed for a home-based track.

Background checks and the personnel file

Iowa screening runs through the record check frame at Iowa Code section 135C.33: a criminal history record check and dependent adult and child abuse record checks before hire and on the cycle the applicable standards set. The personnel file the packet builds for each worker holds the application, references, and the signed job description for the role held, license or credential verification for every licensed role from the primary source where the board provides it, the record check results, OIG and SAM exclusion checks before hire and monthly, orientation, competency evaluation, and the annual performance record, plus the health screening and immunization record the applicable standards require. In a state with no licensure survey for home-based care, that file is the standard a hospital discharge planner or a Medicaid reviewer will judge, and it should read like it was built before the first client, because it has to be.

What you still do today: form the business the Iowa way

Formation is the ordinary employer load, done in the right order: form the entity, get an EIN from the IRS, put liability insurance in place before the first client, adopt written policies and procedures covering the services you will actually sell, set up caregiver training and the section 135C.33 record check rhythm as a system rather than a scramble, and use a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do. No state formation fee figure is printed in the sources checked for this build, so none is quoted here. The Iowa difference is sequencing: the business record comes first, the Medicaid enrollment and HCBS certification come second for payer work, and Medicare certification carries the skilled lane, with no license filing anywhere in the order.

The practical warning

Three Iowa traps cost new owners money. The first is the chapter 135J name trap, paying hospice attention to a home care plan, or worse, telling referral sources a home health license exists because a hospice chapter does. The second is the borrowed figure trap: the $500 chapter 135Q staffing registration and the phantom 641 IAC license fees both spend real money on doors that authorize no home care services. The third is the mirror-image mistake, reading no license as no gate at all, then meeting the real gates late: a 30 day enrollment clock that only runs on a complete application, HCBS certification standards surveyed like any other credential, and EVV and record check duties that attach to the payer work. In Iowa the paperwork is the license, and the founders who treat it that way start clean.
