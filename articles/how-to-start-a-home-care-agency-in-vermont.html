How to Start a Home Care Agency in Vermont (No State License for Non-Medical Care, and a Skilled Door Closed to New Agencies Until 2030)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Vermont story starts with a founder who did everything in the wrong order for a good reason. She had caregivers ready in the Burlington area, families asking for help at home, and a folder of notes from consultant pages that all quoted her a Vermont home care license fee. She paid for advice, she saved the fee money, and she waited for an application that does not exist. When owners bring me a Vermont plan, this is the first thing I sort out, because Vermont runs two completely different systems side by side and the internet merges them into one. For private pay non-medical care, Vermont has no license at all: no application, no licensing agency, and no fee. For skilled home health, Vermont has one of the hardest doors in this whole series: designation, a four year franchise for a service area, sitting behind a certificate of need that the state will not grant to a new home health agency until 2030. A founder who mixes those two doors wastes either months or real money. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

The demand: about 155,000 seniors, nearly one in four residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Vermont despite its size. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 644,663 as of July 1, 2025, and 24.1 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 155,000 seniors, nearly one in four people in the state, one of the oldest population shares in the country. The same Census QuickFacts page for Vermont puts median household income at $81,203 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into. A small state with an old population and solid household income is a relationship market: the work is there, it is spread across small towns, and the agencies I work with win it on reliability and word of mouth rather than on advertising volume.

The license question, answered directly: no license for non-medical care, designation for skilled care

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Vermont? No, because Vermont does not issue one. The Regulations for the Designation and Operation of Home Health Agencies, effective July 1, 2026, define personal care, homemaker, respite, and companionship as unskilled services, and state plainly that unskilled services are not designated services. No Vermont statute or rule attaches a license, a licensing agency, or a license fee to opening a private pay non-medical agency, and that is why the Vermont packet prints NO FEE PUBLISHED on this door: there is no state fee to publish.

The regulated doors are specific, and naming every one of them is how you stay out of trouble. Skilled home health is designated, not licensed: the Department of Disabilities, Aging and Independent Living, through its Division of Licensing and Protection, designates home health agencies under 33 V.S.A. section 6304, and designation follows a certificate of need from the Green Mountain Care Board and federal Medicare certification, in that order. Hospice has no separate Vermont license either: it is a designated service delivered by designated home health and hospice agencies. The Medicaid door for non-medical agencies is different again: certification as a Choices for Care home care provider through DAIL, opened to non-medical agencies on October 25, 2024. No credential from one door covers another door's services, and a residential care home or assisted living residence license under 33 V.S.A. chapter 71 is a facility credential that authorizes no in-home work at all.

The open lane: non-medical home care, and the Choices for Care door for Medicaid work

The private pay sequence is short, and the packet prints it in five steps. Step one is to confirm the service menu is non-skilled: hands-on personal care, homemaker, respite, and companion work only, with no nursing, no therapy, and no skilled tasks, and to document that determination in the startup file. Step two is formation: register the business with the Vermont Secretary of State, hold the EIN and the Vermont business tax account, and put workers compensation and insurance in place, with workers compensation required for employees under 21 V.S.A. section 687. No state application and no state fee sit on this door. Step three is to build the screening and training systems before the first visit, covered in the background check section below.

Step four is the Medicaid door, for founders who want it. Choices for Care is Vermont's long-term services and supports program under the Global Commitment to Health section 1115 demonstration, administered by the Department of Vermont Health Access, which itself acts as the managed care entity, so there is no roster of private Medicaid plans to contract with. Since October 25, 2024, a non-medical home care agency may apply to DAIL, Adult Services Division, to be certified as a Choices for Care home care provider for personal care, respite, and companionship. Certification requires an active Vermont Medicaid provider enrollment through DVHA first, and enrollment and claims run through the Vermont Medicaid Portal operated by Gainwell Technologies. Before that 2024 opening, only designated home health agencies billed for these services, which is why older guides still tell non-medical founders the Medicaid door is closed to them. It is not. Step five is to confirm the current DVHA electronic visit verification instructions for Choices for Care personal care at enrollment and build the visit record system to those instructions before the first Medicaid visit. Private pay work on this door carries no state visit verification rule located in the sources checked.

The closed lane: designated home health, and a certificate of need moratorium through January 1, 2030

The moment your plan includes skilled nursing or therapy, you have left the open lane. Under 33 V.S.A. section 6304, designation gives each agency a franchise to provide home health services in one or more geographic service areas for four years, with the obligation to serve eligible patients in that area, and no agency may render home health services to patients in a designated service area without being designated for that area. The sequence is fixed by the designation rules: first, a certificate of need from the Green Mountain Care Board under 18 V.S.A. section 9434, because offering home health services is a new health care project; second, federal CMS certification as a home health agency; and only then designation by DAIL.

The gate that decides this track is a moratorium, and it is verified fact, not a rumor. A session law moratorium, most recently extended by Act 87 of 2024, section 93, provides that no certificate of need shall be granted for the offering of home health services, which includes hospice, or for a new home health agency, through January 1, 2030, or until the General Assembly lifts the moratorium, whichever occurs first. The only exceptions are narrow: a continuing care retirement community that has been issued a certificate of authority, and a licensed home for persons who are terminally ill under 33 V.S.A. section 7102. Without a certificate of need there is no new designation, so this moratorium gates the home health, Medicare certified, and hospice tracks in the Vermont packet. No designation fee figure is published in section 6304 or in the designation rules, so the packet prints NO FEE PUBLISHED for that door too, and no Vermont source checked prints a processing timeline for designation. The federal layer carries the only hard fee on this lane: Medicare enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS with a CY2026 federal enrollment fee of $750, to Wellpoint Federal, Jurisdiction K, which holds both the A/B and the home health and hospice contracts for Vermont. Initial Medicare enrollment is also gated by the federal moratoria, CMS-6101-N2 for home health and CMS-6102-N2 for hospice, extended and modified effective November 12, 2026 with no end date printed (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). Those federal moratoria gate the Medicare components only; they are not the reason the Vermont door is closed. The state moratorium is.

Background checks and the personnel file: no state license does not mean no standard

Vermont sets no licensure screening file for a door it does not license, so the screening system on the non-medical track is the agency's own adopted standard, and I tell owners to build it like a surveyor will read it. The packet's personnel file for this door carries the application, references, and the signed position description; license or credential verification for every licensed role; the background check record, with the Vermont Criminal Information Center check as the state record private pay agencies use; OIG and SAM exclusion checks before hire and monthly; the orientation, competency, and annual training record; and the health screening record the door requires. The deficiencies the packet flags on this lane are the predictable ones: skilled tasks creeping into a non-medical service plan, an agency holding itself out as designated or licensed when it holds neither credential, Choices for Care visits billed before DAIL certification and DVHA enrollment were both active, and background and exclusion checks run at hire and never again. In a state with no licensure gate, your screening file, your training records, and your policies are the proof of seriousness that a license provides automatically in other states.

What you still do today: form the business the Vermont way

The formation step itself is ordinary, and the packet prices it honestly: no Vermont formation fee figure is printed in the sources the packet is built from, so none is printed here either. Register the business with the Vermont Secretary of State, get the EIN, open the Vermont business tax account, and carry workers compensation for employees as 21 V.S.A. section 687 requires. Then do the employer load in the right order: liability insurance in place before the first client, policies and procedures matched to the services you will actually sell, caregiver screening and training set up as a system rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do, with skilled and medical tasks on the does not do side of the line. Formation is the whole state filing on the non-medical door. Everything after it is preparation and, if you choose it, the Choices for Care certification sequence.

The practical warning

Two Vermont traps cost new owners money, and both come from misreading one of the two doors. The first is paying for the license that does not exist. Consultant and directory pages quote Vermont home care license fees, and the figures that circulate most, $115 and $275, are real numbers in the wrong place: they are the nursing home administrator license fees at 18 V.S.A. section 2058, charged to a person, not to an agency or a facility. The second trap is reading the certificate of need moratorium as a paperwork delay on the skilled door. It is not a queue. Through January 1, 2030, no certificate of need may be granted for a new home health agency or for home health services, hospice included, apart from the two narrow exceptions, and without the certificate there is no designation to wait for. A founder who wants skilled revenue in Vermont before 2030 is not looking at a filing problem; she is looking at a different business model, usually the non-medical door done well, or work inside an existing designated agency's service area. Budget against the door that is actually open.
