How to Start a Home Care Agency in New Jersey (Non-Medical Care Registers at $500, Home Health Waits on a Certificate of Need Call)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The New Jersey story starts with a founder who used the word license for everything, and it cost her a season. She shopped for a home care license, found the Department of Health fee page, and built her plan around the $2,000 home health license, when the business she actually described, non-medical personal care with aides she would employ, never files with the Department of Health at all. It registers with the Division of Consumer Affairs, for $500, under a different statute, with a bond, an accreditation clock, and a filing calendar that changed in 2026. Meanwhile the license she was budgeting for sits behind a certificate of need call that is not open. In the agency records I review, New Jersey is the state where the vocabulary does the damage: registration read as licensure, two different certificates of need sharing a name, a suspended audit read as an eliminated one, and plan names that changed while the paperwork did not. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 1.77 million seniors, the largest market in this series

Start with why owners keep looking at New Jersey. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 9,548,215 as of July 1, 2025, and 18.5 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.77 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for New Jersey puts median household income at $103,556 for 2020 to 2024, the strongest private pay income base in this series. Scale is not the constraint in New Jersey. The constraint is sequencing: the right door, in the right department, on the right calendar.

The license question, answered directly: non-medical care registers, home health is call-gated, hospice is exempt from the call

Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in New Jersey? You need a registration. Non-medical home care is registered, not licensed, by the Division of Consumer Affairs in the Department of Law and Public Safety, Regulated Business Section, under the Employment Agency Act framework at N.J.S.A. 34:8-45.1 and N.J.A.C. 13:45B-13 and 13:45B-14. The registration is the health care service firm. It is not a Department of Health license and not a Board of Nursing license; the Board of Nursing enters the model because hands-on personal care is delivered by Certified Homemaker-Home Health Aides it certifies under N.J.A.C. 13:37-14.

The two Department of Health doors run opposite gates. Skilled home health is licensed under N.J.A.C. 8:42, and under N.J.A.C. 8:42-2.1(a) a home health agency may not be instituted, expanded, licensed, or closed except on a certificate of need issued under a CN Call under N.J.A.C. 8:33. There is no open CN Call for new home health agencies: the call posture was postponed on July 21, 2025, and no open call was verified in the sources checked on November 16, 2026, so entry is call-gated, not open filing, and the call status belongs on the recheck list for the day any plan is made. Hospice is the mirror image: hospice is not subject to certificate of need under N.J.S.A. 26:2H-80(e), and it is licensed under N.J.A.C. 8:42C, but the Department issues the license only to a program with documentation of Medicare certification under N.J.S.A. 26:2H-80, so the federal certification posture comes first in practical effect. One more door is closed rather than gated: comprehensive personal care homes sit under the closure text at N.J.A.C. 8:33H-1.10(c), and that track is not an open new-build path. New Jersey maintains no Medicaid enrollment moratorium of its own for home health or hospice; the operative restrictions are the federal Medicare moratoria on the certified tracks and the DDD application calendar covered below.

The non-medical lane: health care service firm registration at $500, on a September 30 calendar

A health care service firm provides personal care, companionship, and homemaker services for persons with disabilities and persons age 60 or older, and a firm that employs, places, arranges, or refers individuals for those services registers before operating. Companion-only firms are inside the registration; companion services entered the statute effective February 1, 2016. The filing facts: the annual registration fee is $500 per primary location under N.J.A.C. 13:45B-7.1, and the firm posts a $10,000 surety bond, waivable on a showing of $100,000 net worth, under N.J.A.C. 13:45B-13.3. The term is one year, and the calendar changed in 2026: under P.L. 2026, c.45 and the Division notice, registrations now expire September 30 instead of June 30, renewal applications are submitted before October 1 each year, and firms that already renewed for 2026-27 are automatically extended to September 30, 2027. The older July 1 renewal date in the code text is superseded and must not drive a filing calendar.

Two duties decide whether the registration survives its first year. Accreditation runs on two separate layers: the statute conditions registration on accreditation within 12 months from a body recognized for homemaker agencies in the Medicaid program under N.J.S.A. 34:8-45.1(c), and a second layer at N.J.A.C. 10:60-1.3(d) requires a firm participating in Medicaid home care to be accredited by an organization approved by the Department of Human Services. The layers are not interchangeable, and the recognized body list located for the packet build is dated March 2019, so confirm current recognition before paying an accreditor. Financial reporting runs on the same suspended-versus-eliminated line: the annual financial statement remains due each September 30 under N.J.A.C. 13:45B-13.5A(a), while the audit and report duties at 13:45B-13.5A(b) to (e) are suspended pending new regulations under P.L. 2026, c.45. Suspended is the word. A firm that deletes its audit file in 2026 rebuilds it under deadline when the regulations publish.

The skilled and hospice lanes: $2,000 licenses with opposite entry gates

The home health agency license under N.J.A.C. 8:42 covers an agency providing, at minimum, nursing services, homemaker-home health aide services, and physical therapy on a visiting basis in the home. Read the gate before the fees: the certificate of need comes first, on the CN Call route under the 8:33 framework, and the license application follows. Keep the two certificates of need apart, because they share words and nothing else: N.J.A.C. 8:42-2.1(a) requires a facility certificate of need before an agency may be licensed, while N.J.A.C. 10:60-2.2 requires a certification of need for services, a physician certification for an individual patient. Treating the patient certification as satisfying the facility gate does not open the gate. The fees, from N.J.A.C. 8:42-2.2 as amended December 18, 2023: application and each annual renewal $2,000, biennial inspection fee $500, transfer of ownership $1,000, and relocation $250. Fee figures from pre-December 2023 sources are superseded.

The hospice license under N.J.A.C. 8:42C carries application and each annual renewal at $2,000, a biennial inspection fee of $1,000, an inpatient unit inspection at $1,500 per site, adding an inpatient unit at $1,500 plus $15 per bed, a branch office at $150, and transfer of ownership at $1,500. Inpatient hospice units are licensed inside this license at Subchapter 11. Because the state license requires documentation of Medicare certification, the federal moratorium window sits inside the licensure calendar: initial Medicare enrollment for hospices is gated by CMS-6102-N2, and for home health agencies by CMS-6101-N2, extended effective November 12, 2026 with no end date printed in the notices (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). Medicare enrollment for either track runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS, with the CY2026 federal enrollment fee of $750, to National Government Services, Jurisdiction 6, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for New Jersey. Novitas Solutions, Jurisdiction L, is the A/B MAC; a home health or hospice 855A sent to that desk goes to the wrong desk.

Enrollment routes, plan names, and EVV: the requirements behind the doors

Medicaid enrollment runs through NJMMIS, operated by Gainwell Technologies for the Division of Medical Assistance and Health Services, and the license or registration comes first. Two routes must not be merged: full fee-for-service enrollment, which permits billing NJ FamilyCare directly, and the 21st Century Cures Act MCO-only enrollment, for providers who will only be paid through managed care plans. The MCO-only route does not permit fee-for-service billing, and the wrong route is corrected by re-enrollment, not by billing through it. New Jersey publishes no state Medicaid enrollment fee. State enrollment places a provider in no plan network; each of the five NJ FamilyCare MLTSS plans, Aetna Better Health of New Jersey, Fidelis Care, Horizon NJ Health, UnitedHealthcare Community Plan, and Wellpoint, credentials and contracts separately. Use the current names: Wellpoint is the plan formerly operating as Amerigroup New Jersey, and Fidelis Care is the plan formerly operating as WellCare, and credentialing or billing records carrying the old names as current are built on superseded sources.

Electronic visit verification runs on an open, aggregator model. HHAeXchange is the state contractor and aggregator, every NJ FamilyCare visit record must end in the aggregator regardless of the front end, HHAeXchange fronts Aetna Better Health of New Jersey, UnitedHealthcare Community Plan, Fidelis Care, and fee-for-service, and CareBridge fronts Horizon NJ Health and Wellpoint, relaying into the same aggregator. Personal care visits have required capture since January 1, 2021 and home health care services since January 1, 2023. Two workforce facts complete the readiness picture: aides on a health care service firm registration are employees, and engaging them as independent contractors is a named deficiency, and supervisory visits are documented on the cycle the plan of care sets, not batched at month end.

The developmental disabilities doors carry their own calendar, and it is a future stop, not a current one. DDD provider approval, through the Combined Application to Become a Medicaid/DDD Approved Provider, gates the residential tracks, including the community residence license under N.J.A.C. 10:44A for group homes and supervised apartments of up to 16 individuals, licensed by the Department of Human Services, Office of Licensing. DDD accepts new provider applications through December 31, 2026; the stop is effective January 1, 2027, the first Open Application Period is anticipated about September 2027, and two windows per year, tentatively March and September, follow. That is a payer approval calendar, not a licensure moratorium, and it does not touch the registration or the Department of Health licenses. An owner planning a DDD group home starts with the approval calendar and the 24 month experience record DDD requires, not with the building.

What you still do today: form the business the New Jersey way

Form the entity with the New Jersey Division of Revenue and Enterprise Services, get the EIN and the tax registrations, and put liability insurance in place before the first client. Then build the record for your door: for a health care service firm, the registration file, the bond, the accreditation plan inside its 12 month clock, and the financial reporting calendar rebuilt around September 30; for a licensed track, the license file behind its entry gate. Written policies and procedures, aide certification and screening files, and a service agreement that states what the agency does and does not do complete the load. Sequence enrollment after the credential: NJMMIS on the correct route, then plan contracting, then EVV configuration, and no services billed to a door before that door's effective date, because no step in this sequence backdates an enrollment.

The practical warning

Four New Jersey traps recur. The first is the wrong-department build: a non-medical plan budgeted and sequenced as a Department of Health license filing, when the door is a $500 Division of Consumer Affairs registration with its own duties. The second is the certificate of need confusion, in both directions: a home health filing sequenced as if licensure were open filing with no CN Call behind it, and a hospice plan delayed by a certificate of need that N.J.S.A. 26:2H-80(e) says does not apply. The third is the suspended audit read as eliminated, with the September 30 financial statement missed because the filing calendar still runs on the superseded June 30 dates. The fourth is stale names and desks: EVV visits fronted through the wrong route for the member's plan, credentialing records carrying Amerigroup where the plan is now Wellpoint, and a CMS-855A mailed to Novitas Jurisdiction L or to Jurisdiction K instead of National Government Services Jurisdiction 6. New Jersey prints all of this. The traps are what happens when a plan is built from summaries instead of the code.

Skilled home health licenses with the Department of Health under N.J.A.C. 8:42, behind a certificate of need call, and hospice licenses under N.J.A.C. 8:42C. The registration sequence runs form the business, file the registration with the $500 fee and the surety bond, and complete accreditation within 12 months. How much does a home care license cost in New Jersey?

The home health agency license costs $2,000 on application and each annual renewal, plus a $500 biennial inspection fee, under N.J.A.C. 8:42-2.2. Your next step

Work in door order. If non-medical care is the plan, register as a health care service firm at $500 per primary location, post the bond, start the 12 month accreditation clock, and set the calendar to September 30 expirations and renewals filed before October 1. If skilled home health is the plan, the license is $2,000 a year under N.J.A.C. 8:42 and the first step is the CN Call posture, rechecked with the Department of Health on the day you act, because no open call was verified in November 2026. If hospice is the plan, the license is $2,000 under N.J.A.C. 8:42C with no certificate of need, and the Medicare certification documentation comes first in practical effect. Then run the payer sequence in order: NJMMIS enrollment on the route that matches the business, separate credentialing with each of the five MLTSS plans, and EVV into the HHAeXchange aggregator from the first Medicaid visit, in a state with about 1.77 million seniors.
