How to Start a Home Care Agency in Illinois (The $1,500 License and the 90-Day Clock)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 3, 2026

The Illinois story usually starts with a search result that is years out of date. A new owner in Chicago or Springfield finds an old page that lists the home care license fee at $25, budgets almost nothing for licensing, and starts planning around clients. Then the owner reaches the Illinois Department of Public Health application and finds the current picture. The fee is $1,500 and non-refundable. The license is a Home Services Agency license under the Home Health, Home Services, and Home Nursing Agency Licensing Act. The application is a full package of policies, staff records, insurance proof, and registry access, not a one page form. And you cannot open, operate, or even advertise home services before the license issues. The owner who budgeted $25 is now rebuilding the whole launch plan around a license that has to come first.

That gap between the old pages and the current rule is the reason this video exists, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against an Illinois primary source today. One correction matters enough to state up front, because the video and many summaries flatten it. A new agency in Illinois gets a provisional license that runs 240 days, with one possible renewal of up to 90 days. The shorter 120 day provisional term belongs to a different situation, an operating agency placed on a provisional license. New owners should plan on the 240 day runway, and on the inspection that comes near the end of it.

The demand: about 2.38 million seniors in a big, established market

Start with why Illinois draws new owners. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 12,719,141 as of July 1, 2025, and 18.7 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 2.38 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Illinois puts median household income at $83,390 for 2020 to 2024. That pairing is the business case. Illinois holds one of the largest senior populations in the country, anchored by the Chicago metro, and household incomes that can carry private pay care in its major markets.

The price families face explains the revenue side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Illinois, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $82,368, up 3 percent, ranked 23rd among states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $36 an hour. That supersedes the 2024 survey figure used in the video, $80,080 a year at $35 an hour. The median is a market benchmark, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows a large market where one full time client represents a substantial annual contract, which is why getting the license right matters more than getting it fast.

The license: Home Services Agency, non-medical only

Illinois licenses this business through the Illinois Department of Public Health, usually shortened to IDPH, under the Home Health, Home Services, and Home Nursing Agency Licensing Act, 210 ILCS 55, and the rules at Title 77, Part 245 of the Illinois Administrative Code. The license you need for a non-medical agency that employs its own caregivers is the Home Services Agency license. It is a different license from a home services placement agency, which places workers it does not employ, and a different license again from home health and home nursing, which cover skilled care.

The boundary is in the definitions. Under 210 ILCS 55/2.09 and 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.210, home services are non-medical services that assist clients with activities of daily living: personal care, medication reminding, housekeeping, personal laundry, cooking, shopping, help getting to and from appointments, household records, and companionship. The same rules state the hard edge plainly. A home services agency shall not provide medical services that belong to a home health or home nursing license, and home services do not include any service that would be required to be performed by an individual licensed under the Nurse Practice Act. The IDPH application form says it even more bluntly: this non-medical license does not allow skilled services regardless of whether an individual worker is licensed or certified in another discipline. A nurse on your staff does not expand your license. The license defines the work, not the worker.

The money: $1,500 a year, non-refundable, and no surety bond on this application

The fee rule is at 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.95(b): the home services agency fee is not to exceed $1,500 annually, and the fee is not refundable. IDPH charges the flat $1,500 on its Home Services Application, Form 445104, payable by check or money order to IDPH, and the form states that electronic payments are not accepted. Fees go to the Home Care Services Agency Licensure Fund under 210 ILCS 55/10.05. The old $25 figure still circulating on older pages comes from before a 2024 amendment raised the fee. Budget $1,500, and expect it again at renewal, because the fee is annual.

Two money points on this application surprise owners, in opposite directions. First, the application does not list a surety bond for this license. Do not buy a bond product because a generic checklist told you to. Second, the application does require insurance proof, and the amounts are specific. Under 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.90(a)(2), the initial application must be accompanied by a certificate of insurance documenting minimum liability coverage of $1 million per occurrence and $3 million in the aggregate, and the agency must keep showing evidence of that coverage under 245.30(b)(2). The insurance certificate is an application cost in the same line as the fee, because the package is incomplete without it.

The package: policies, people, and registry access, all before you file

The Illinois application is a full package, not a form and a check. From 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.90 and the IDPH application, expect to submit your policies, including complaint resolution, infection control, employee training, and supervisory visits, a staff list, your fee schedule, your client service contract, and the Agency Manager Qualification Review Form for the manager you designate. The geographic service area matters too: it is an area of contiguous counties approved by the Department, and IDPH must approve new counties before you serve clients there, through the Geographic Service Area Request Form described on the IDPH Home Services Agencies FAQ page. An agency also has to maintain an office with a working telephone staffed during business hours, with records protected for confidentiality, under 245.40(a)(12), and keep records at least two years past the last service date under 245.210.

The manager rule is good news for non-medical founders, stated carefully. A home services agency designates a person to supervise the provision of services under 245.30(f)(1), and that agency manager has overall responsibility for the organization and its day to day operation. No registered nurse is required to hold that role for a home services agency. The RN supervisor mandate in 245.30(f)(2) applies to home nursing agencies, where skilled services are being supervised. Illinois asks a non-medical agency for a qualified, accountable manager with a qualification form on file. It does not ask it to hire a nurse to supervise companionship and personal care.

Background checks and training: the worker file the state will ask for

Every home services worker goes through the Health Care Worker Background Check Act, 225 ILCS 46. Under 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.71(a)(1), a worker must not have a disqualifying background check under that Act without a waiver, and 245.72 requires the agency to comply with the Act and the Health Care Worker Background Check Code at Part 955. Section 33 of the Act provides for a fingerprint based criminal history records check. At application, the agency must also show it is registered with the IDPH web portal and granted access to the Health Care Worker Registry, under 245.90(a)(3)(J). Build the registry access and the fingerprint process into your hiring system before the first hire, not after a surveyor asks to see the file.

Training has a split clock that owners miss. Under 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.71(c), as rewritten effective March 25, 2025, a home services worker completes a minimum of 10 hours of initial training: 5 hours before the first assignment, and the remaining 5 hours within the first 30 days after the start of employment. The worker may not take an assignment until passing a competency evaluation on the first 5 hours, and competency is tested again after the remaining 5. On top of that, 245.71(f) requires a minimum of 10 hours of training during each year of employment, and the initial 10 hours count for the first year. A training calendar with dates, topics, and competency results for every worker is not paperwork for its own sake in Illinois. It is the record the rule is built around.

The clock: 90 days to provisional, 240 days to prove the agency

Illinois publishes its clock, which makes it unusual in this series. The IDPH Home Services FAQ states that the time from application submission to approval or denial of provisional licensure should take no more than 90 days, and 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.100(a)(1) states that the Department will issue a provisional license to a new agency within 90 days after receipt of the application, provided the application complies with Section 245.90. That condition is the whole game. The 90 day clock runs on a compliant package. An incomplete package can be denied under 245.130(b), which is why the work in the sections above happens before filing, not during review.

What follows is the part the video compressed, stated in full here. Under 245.100(a)(2), a provisional license for a new agency is valid for 240 days unless sooner suspended or revoked. Within 30 days before the provisional license ends, the Department inspects the agency, and if the agency substantially meets the requirements, it issues the full license. For home services agencies, that license expires one year from the end of the month in which the provisional license was first issued, under 245.100(a)(3)(A). If a provisional holder does not substantially meet the requirements but has made significant progress, the Department may renew the provisional license once, for a period not to exceed 90 days, under 245.100(a)(4). Renewal of the full license has its own window: file not less than 60 days and not more than 90 days before expiration, under 245.90(b)(1), and filing later than 60 days before expiration can trigger a fine under Part 245.140. Put the renewal window on the calendar the day the license arrives.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Illinois owner. The statute bars the shortcut directly. Under 210 ILCS 55/3.3 and 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.80(b), no person shall open, manage, conduct, or maintain a home services agency, or advertise as one, without a license issued by the Department. Advertising counts. A website, a listing, and a referral pitch that present you as an operating agency before the license issues are not marketing head starts. They are unlicensed operation on the face of the rule. The enforcement tools behind it include a fine of up to $100 per day under 210 ILCS 55/9.04(b), licensure action, and an injunction, because operating in violation of the Act is declared a public nuisance under 210 ILCS 55/14. Add the sequence errors that cost owners quietly: budgeting off the old $25 fee, sending a caregiver out before the first 5 training hours and the competency check are done, and assuming a nurse on staff lets the agency take skilled tasks. Illinois is not a hard state because its rules are hidden. Part 245 is public, the FAQ is plain, and the application lists its own contents. It is a hard state for owners who treat the package as a formality and meet the rules for the first time at the provisional inspection.

Build the package before you file anything, in the order the rule checks it.
