Home Care Profit Margin: What a $27 an Hour Rate Really Pays

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 18, 2026

She had not signed anything yet, and that was the good news. A new owner I was advising had a rate in front of her, $27 an hour, and a simple piece of math on a notebook page: $27 minus the $17 an hour she expected to pay a caregiver leaves $10, and $10 an hour sounded like a business. It is the math almost every new owner does first, and it stops one step too early. That $10 is not profit. It is a spread, and three layers of cost have a claim on it before a single dollar belongs to the agency: the payroll burden on the wage, the direct costs that ride on every hour worked, and the overhead that arrives every month whether you bill an hour or not. Run all three honestly and the same $27 rate tells two completely different stories depending on one thing most new owners never put in the first calculation: how many billable hours a month the agency actually runs. This article takes that rate apart cost by cost, in the order the costs claim the spread, and lands on the three calculations to run for any rate, from any payer, before you sign, set a price, or spend a dollar.

Why the $17 pay figure is realistic, not generous

Start with the pay side, because owners who want the spread to work tend to shave it first. The $17 an hour in this example is not a generous wage picked to make the math look hard. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the May 2025 national median for home health and personal care aides at $35,800 a year, $17.21 an hour, in its Occupational Outlook Handbook. Pay far under that in a tight labor market and the problem stops being margin and becomes staffing: the hours you sold do not get covered. And the rate side deserves the same honesty. The CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey puts the national median bill rate for a non-medical in-home caregiver at $35 an hour, up 3 percent, $80,080 a year on the survey 44 hour week basis, from more than 25,000 rates gathered July to November 2025. A $27 rate sits well under that national median, which is exactly why it deserves this scrutiny: rates at this level usually arrive from a payer schedule or a competitive local market, not from an owner choosing a comfortable number.

The first claim on the spread: payroll burden

The wage is not the cost of the wage. On top of the $17, the agency owes the employer share of Social Security and Medicare, 7.65 percent, 6.2 percent for Social Security plus 1.45 percent for Medicare, as the IRS sets out in Publication 15, the Employer Tax Guide, and in IRS Topic No. 751. Then come federal and state unemployment insurance, and workers compensation. Workers compensation for home care work typically adds about $0.80 to $1.20 per caregiver hour in my professional judgment, because the injury risk in this work is real: transfers, slips, and driving between clients. Your state and your carrier quote your actual figure. Added together, the payroll burden typically lands at 15 to 20 percent of wages as a planning estimate. At 18 percent on $17 an hour, that is $3.06. The $10 spread is now about $7, and not one direct cost has been counted yet.

The second claim: the costs that ride on every hour

Some costs attach to the hour itself. A background check has to be spread across the hours that caregiver actually works for you. Say a check costs $75 and the caregiver works 500 hours before leaving, which is optimistic in a high turnover field: that is $0.15 an hour. Paid training time you cannot bill works the same way: 8 hours of paid onboarding spread over those same 500 hours comes to about $0.27 an hour. Add travel between clients, supplies, and your EVV system, and a conservative planning figure for direct costs is about $1 an hour, every hour. The spread is now about $6. Everything so far moves with the work. The next layer does not, and it is the layer that decides whether the rate works.

The third claim: overhead, and why volume decides the answer

Overhead is the rent on the business itself: liability insurance, scheduling software, billing, phone, marketing, and the administrative hours that keep the agency running. In my professional judgment, liability insurance alone typically runs $500 to $1,000 a month for a small agency, and your quotes will vary. Say total overhead is $3,000 a month, which is modest. Overhead does not care how much you bill, so the same $3,000 lands very differently depending on volume. At 400 billable hours a month, roughly 100 hours a week, that overhead sits on every hour at $7.50. Now run the full line: $17 in pay, plus $3.06 in burden, plus $1 in direct costs, plus $7.50 in overhead, gives a true cost of $28.56 an hour against a $27 rate. The agency loses $1.56 on every hour it bills, about $624 a month at that volume, with full schedules and happy clients. That is how a busy agency goes broke: the work was never the problem, the math was.

Watch what volume does to the identical rate. At 800 billable hours a month, the same $3,000 of overhead is $3.75 an hour. The full line is $27 minus $17, minus $3.06, minus $1, minus $3.75, which leaves $2.19 an hour, or $1,752 a month. Not riches, but a real business. At 1,200 billable hours a month, overhead falls to $2.50 an hour, the margin rises to $3.44 an hour, and the month is worth $4,128. For most small agencies at mid-range rates like this one, the real margin lands around $2 to $3.50 per billable hour once everything is counted. There is also a break-even point hiding in the same numbers: each billable hour contributes $5.94 after pay, burden, and direct costs ($27 minus $17, minus $3.06, minus $1), so $3,000 of overhead needs about 505 billable hours a month before the rate stops losing money. The $27 rate was never good or bad by itself. It is bad at 400 hours and workable at 1,200. The rate did not change. The volume did.

The warning at the low end: when the spread cannot carry the burden

Here is the practical warning I give owners, and it matters most before you start, while a payer contract or a price sheet is still unsigned. Run the burden test on any rate before anything else. Some owners are staring at a far thinner version of this problem: a $17 an hour rate against caregivers who want $15. That leaves a $2 spread, and the payroll burden on $15 of pay alone runs $2.25 to $3 an hour at the same 15 to 20 percent planning range. The mandatory burden is bigger than the whole spread, before insurance, before software, before one background check. No amount of hustle fixes that, and volume makes it worse, not better, because every added hour loses more. The agencies that survive at that rate level do specific things: they mix payers, using steadier Medicaid hours as a base and layering private pay clients on top, because the private pay hours are where the margin lives; they keep overhead brutally low until volume earns the spending; and they treat keeping a caregiver as a financial strategy, because at a thin margin every quit is a cost the margin cannot carry. If your burden test fails, the answer is a different payer mix, a different rate, or a different overhead plan, never more hours of a losing hour.

Your next step: run the three calculations this afternoon

Everything in this article reduces to three calculations you can run for your own market before you sign or spend. One, true cost per hour: what you pay the caregiver, plus the payroll burden, plus your direct costs per hour, plus your monthly fixed costs divided by your billable hours. Two, margin per hour: your rate minus that true cost. Three, break-even hours: your monthly fixed costs divided by what each hour contributes after direct costs. Run all three for every payer you are considering, at low volume and at the volume you honestly expect in year one, not the volume you hope for in year three. Do it after the schedules fill up, and the same math chooses for you.
