How to Start a Home Care Agency in Kansas (Non-Medical Care Is Licensed Too, and There Is No Certificate of Need)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Kansas story starts with a founder near Wichita who read that Kansas does not license non-medical home care, and believed it. She signed clients, sent caregivers, and built a schedule on that reading, and the reading was wrong. The first thing I tell owners looking at Kansas is the correction this state keeps teaching: Kansas licenses every in-home door under one act, and the license duty reaches the non-medical agency that only helps with bathing, meals, and daily living. The second thing I tell them is the fact that surprises founders coming from other states: Kansas has no certificate of need for home health, so the gate that slows skilled filings elsewhere simply is not in the Kansas sequence. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

Kansas rewards the founder who picks the right scope the first time, because the two scopes carry different fees and the wrong one either underfunds the filing or overpays it. The sections below follow the Kansas packet, which is built from Kansas primary sources checked on November 17, 2026.

The demand: about 563,000 seniors across a wide state

Start with why owners keep looking at Kansas. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 2,977,220 as of July 1, 2025, and 18.9 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 563,000 seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Kansas puts median household income at $74,275 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into. Demand concentrates in the eastern metros and spreads thin across the western counties, which makes service area choice a real business decision in this state: the license is statewide law, and the drive time is local math.

The license question, answered directly: supportive care is licensed under K.S.A. 65-5102

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Kansas? Yes. The Kansas Department of Health and Environment, called KDHE, through its Bureau of Facilities and Licensing, licenses home health agencies under the Home Health Agency Act, K.S.A. 65-5101 and following, and K.A.R. 28-51. Supportive care services, defined at K.S.A. 65-5101, are non-medical help with activities of daily living, and the license duty at K.S.A. 65-5102 applies to an agency providing supportive care services for a fee, private pay agencies as well as Medicaid providers. An agency that provides only supportive care services holds the same license restricted to that scope. An agency that provides home health services or HCBS attendant care holds the license at the higher scope, and the Kansas Department for Aging and Disability Services, called KDADS, requires that scope for HCBS waiver programs.

K.A.R. 28-51-101(a)(2) bars any person from providing home health services, HCBS, or supportive care services before the license issues, so the sequence is license first, clients second, on every in-home door. The license term is one year. Hospice sits on the federal door: under K.S.A. 65-6202, only a Medicare certified hospice may hold itself out as a hospice in Kansas, Kansas does not license hospices under that statute, and a Medicare certified hospice serving only hospice patients is excepted from the home health agency act at K.S.A. 65-5112(d). Checked negatives, printed because owners budget against them: no Kansas certificate of need requirement for home health agencies or hospices was located in the sources checked, and no KanCare or KDADS payer enrollment moratorium or pause was located. The similarly named adult care home license at K.S.A. 39-923 and following is a residential facility license issued by KDADS; it authorizes no in-home agency work, and no in-home agency needs it.

The non-medical lane: the supportive care scope at $250 initial

The sequence for the supportive care scope runs in a plain order. Confirm the service menu is supportive care only: non-medical help with activities of daily living, with no skilled services and no HCBS attendant care, because the scope on the application is the scope the license restricts. Form the business with the Kansas Secretary of State, hold the EIN, and put the insurance and operating accounts in place. File the KDHE application at the supportive care scope and pay the tiered fee at K.A.R. 28-51-101: the initial license fee is $250 for an agency expecting fewer than 100 total unique clients, and $500 for 100 or more. The annual renewal fee is $100 for fewer than 100 unique clients served, and $350 for 100 or more, under K.A.R. 28-51-101(b)(2), with renewal filed at least thirty days before expiration. Fees are nonrefundable and, since July 1, 2025, are paid online through PayIt. Hold services until the license issues, then operate inside the scope the license prints.

The Medicaid door on this track needs a scope warning. KanCare is administered by KDHE, Division of Health Care Finance, with the HCBS waivers operated by KDADS, and provider enrollment runs through KMAP with revalidation at least once every five years. Managed care runs through three KanCare organizations: Healthy Blue, Sunflower Health Plan, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan; Aetna Better Health of Kansas exited KanCare on January 1, 2025, and plan lists still naming it are stale. The scope point is the one founders miss: HCBS attendant care sits in the higher license scope, so a supportive-care-only agency that wants waiver work files the higher scope, it does not bill waiver services on the restricted license. Electronic visit verification runs through AuthentiCare, the Fiserv system Kansas provides, on the services the state EVV frame covers.

The skilled lane: the full home health scope at $500 initial, no certificate of need

The skilled sequence uses the same act at the higher tier. The definitions at K.A.R. 28-51-100 set the scope line for skilled services, the licensing procedure and tiered fees sit at K.A.R. 28-51-101, organization and administration at 28-51-103, skilled home health services at 28-51-104, supportive care services at 28-51-117, and HCBS at 28-51-118. The initial license fee is $500 for fewer than 100 total unique patients and clients, and $750 for 100 or more. The annual renewal fee is $350 for fewer than 100, and $600 for 100 or more. The license term is one year, and no processing timeline for the license is printed in K.A.R. 28-51-101, so none is promised here; the printed clocks are the one year term, the renewal filing at least thirty days before expiration, and the plan of care review cycles the service rules print.

There is no certificate of need gate in front of this license, and that negative is a planning asset: the Kansas skilled sequence is formation, application at the correct tier, license, then payer enrollment, with no need determination in the middle. Medicare certification layers on the license through enrollment on Form CMS-855A through PECOS, with the CY2026 federal institutional enrollment fee of $750. The Home Health and Hospice MAC for Kansas is CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, and the A/B MAC is Wisconsin Physicians Service Government Health Administrators, Jurisdiction 5. Noridian Jurisdiction D is the DME MAC, not the enrollment desk. Initial Medicare enrollment on the certified tracks is gated by the federal enrollment moratoria, CMS-6101-N2 for home health and CMS-6102-N2 for hospice, extended and modified effective November 12, 2026 (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). The federal screening frame at 42 CFR 455.470 is a condition of enrollment, not a Kansas pause notice, and no state pause is printed for Kansas.

The hospice lane: Medicare certification is the whole door

Hospice in Kansas prints no separate state license and no state hospice license fee. Under K.S.A. 65-6202, only a Medicare certified hospice may hold itself out as a hospice or licensed hospice in Kansas, so the build is the federal sequence: program formation, enrollment on Form CMS-855A with the CY2026 fee of $750, the file the hospice Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR Part 418 require, and the certification survey. A Medicare certified hospice serving only hospice patients is excepted from the home health agency act at K.S.A. 65-5112(d), which means a hospice-only program does not also buy the home health license. A program that adds home health services beyond hospice steps back into the act and its tiers.

The residential doors: adult care homes, adult day care, and group homes

Founders who want a home meet the KDADS map, and one Kansas naming point matters more here than anywhere else in this article. Adult day care in Kansas is a licensed adult care home classification, licensed under K.A.R. 26-43: the KDADS license is the credential, and the Frail Elderly Waiver is only the Medicaid payer door behind it, not a substitute license. Adult care homes price at thirty dollars for each resident in the stated resident capacity plus one hundred dollars, under K.A.R. 26-39-101(k), across the assisted living and residential living tiers at K.A.R. 26-41, homes plus, and nursing facilities and ICF/IID facilities at K.A.R. 26-40. I/DD residential supports group homes are certified by KDADS on the I/DD Waiver, printed as no certification fee published, and the Community Supports Waiver, approved by CMS with an effective date of October 1, 2026 and 500 first-year slots, adds a new residential door on that waiver frame. None of those figures is a home health fee, and the adult care home name never licenses in-home work.

Background checks and the personnel file

Kansas screening runs through the Kansas Nurse Aide Registry check through KDHE before hire and on the cycle the applicable standards set, with a criminal history and fingerprint record where the door requires it; the Article 51 fingerprint fee cap is fifty dollars. The personnel file the packet builds for each worker holds the application, references, and the signed job description for the role held, license or credential verification for every licensed role from the primary source where the board provides it, the registry check result, OIG and SAM exclusion checks before hire and monthly, orientation, competency evaluation, and the annual performance record, plus the health screening and immunization record the applicable standards require. The discipline point I give owners is rhythm: checks run at hire and on the printed cycle, filed where a surveyor can follow them without asking.

What you still do today: form the business the Kansas way

Formation is the ordinary employer load, done in the right order: form the entity with the Kansas Secretary of State, get an EIN from the IRS, put liability insurance in place before the first client, adopt written policies and procedures matched to the scope you filed, supportive care at 28-51-117 or the full service rules for the higher scope, set up caregiver training and the registry check rhythm as a system, and use a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do. No state formation fee figure is printed in the sources checked for this build, so none is quoted here. The Kansas difference is that formation alone opens nothing: the KDHE license at the correct scope is the door, and services wait for it.

The practical warning

Three Kansas traps cost new owners money. The first is the no-license myth itself: any source that tells a Kansas founder that non-medical care needs no state license is contradicting K.S.A. 65-5102, and an agency built on that reading provides services before the license issues, against K.A.R. 28-51-101(a)(2). The second is the swapped tier: the supportive care tier at $250 initial under 100 clients prices the restricted license only, and the skilled and HCBS tier at $500 initial under 100 prices the full license, so budgeting the supportive tier for a skilled agency underfunds the filing and budgeting the skilled tier for a supportive only agency overpays it. The third is the imported gate: founders arriving from certificate of need states sequence a need review Kansas does not run, and stall a filing that was ready. Kansas has no certificate of need in this sequence, and the calendar belongs to the license and the payer enrollments instead.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a home care agency in Kansas?

You start with the KDHE home health agency license, because Kansas licenses this work under one act. An agency providing supportive care services only files at that restricted scope, $250 initial for fewer than 100 total unique clients, and an agency providing home health services or HCBS attendant care files at the higher scope, $500 initial at the same size, under K.A.R. 28-51-101. What are the Kansas home care license requirements?

Kansas home-based care runs on one licensing act with two scopes, issued by the Kansas Department of Health and Environment under K.S.A. 65-5101 and following and K.A.R. 28-51. Supportive care services only takes the license restricted to that scope, and home health services or HCBS attendant care takes the license at the higher scope, which KDADS requires for HCBS waiver programs. Hospice has no separate Kansas license: under K.S.A. 65-6202, only a Medicare certified hospice may hold itself out as a hospice. There is no certificate of need in Kansas for these doors, and adult care homes are a separate KDADS residential license.

How much does a home care license cost in Kansas?

Supportive care only costs $250 initial for fewer than 100 total unique clients and $500 for 100 or more, with annual renewal at $100 or $350. The home health and HCBS scope costs $500 initial for fewer than 100 and $750 for 100 or more, with annual renewal at $350 or $600, all under K.A.R. 28-51-101, nonrefundable. Hospice carries no state license fee, because the door is Medicare certification. The free Kansas preview prints the full fee table from the state own schedule.

Do I need a license for non-medical home care in Kansas?

Yes. K.S.A. 65-5102 requires an agency providing supportive care services for a fee to hold a KDHE home health agency license, and the duty applies to private pay agencies as well as Medicaid providers. K.A.R. 28-51-101(a)(2) bars providing the services before the license issues. The license is restricted to the supportive care scope when the agency provides no skilled or HCBS services.

Your next step

Work in lane order. Pick the scope first, supportive care only at the $250 initial tier or the full home health and HCBS scope at the $500 initial tier, each priced under 100 clients at K.A.R. 28-51-101, because that one choice sets the fee, the policy set, and the waiver doors open to the agency.
