Caregivers Quit in the First 90 Days: What Each Departure Costs, and the Five Levers That Stop It

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 22, 2026

She did not resign. She just stopped showing up. The owner I was advising had hired her six weeks earlier, trained her for two days, and put her on a schedule that looked nothing like the one described at the interview: short shifts scattered across town, a first paycheck that came up short, and an office number that rang out the first time a client visit went wrong. By the time anyone in the office called to ask what happened, she was already working for another agency. Owners tell me this story as a hiring problem, and they reach for more job ads. In the records I review, it is almost never a hiring problem. It is a first ninety days problem, and most of it sits inside the owner's control. This article puts the industry's own numbers on what an early quit costs, walks the real reasons new caregivers leave inside ninety days, and covers the five levers that stop it.

The numbers: three out of four leave, and most leave early

Start with the benchmark, because owners feel this cost without ever seeing it added up. The Activated Insights Benchmarking Report, the home care industry's largest annual study, put median caregiver turnover at 75 percent for 2024, improved from 79.2 percent in 2023. Three out of four caregivers, gone within the year, at the median agency. The timing is the story: Activated Insights reports that 57 percent of caregiver turnover happens inside the first ninety days of employment. Most departures are not veterans leaving. They are new hires who never settled.

Now price one departure. Activated Insights puts the average cost of replacing a caregiver at about $2,600, built from recruiting, screening, hiring, and training time, using the Center for American Progress method of 16 percent of annual pay for lower wage roles. Treat that figure as a floor, not a ceiling, because it is built on an older wage base and it leaves out the overtime you pay to cover open shifts, the scheduler hours burned rebuilding the week, and the referrals you turn away because nobody is available. Run the arithmetic on the published figures anyway, because even the floor is sobering: an agency with twenty caregivers at the median turnover rate loses fifteen people a year, and fifteen replacements at about $2,600 each is about $39,000 a year spent replacing people instead of serving clients. Turnover is not a soft culture metric. At a small agency it can be the largest single line of waste in the business.

Why new caregivers actually leave inside ninety days

The reasons are less mysterious than owners expect. The job was not the job they were sold. The schedule they were promised dissolves into scattered short shifts and last minute changes, so the paycheck never matches the picture from the interview. Pay arrives on a cadence that does not match their bills, or the first check is wrong. Training was a signature on a form, so the first hard client feels like being set up to fail. And the office is unreachable: the first time a shift goes wrong, the caregiver calls, nobody answers, and the lesson lands that they are alone out there.

Notice what is missing from that list. It is rarely the work itself. People who choose caregiving know the work is hard. They leave the management of the work. That is the practical warning in this article, and it cuts both ways: if your early quits cluster in the first ninety days, posting more ads feeds the same leak. Fix the ninety days first, then hire into a system that keeps people.

The five levers an owner actually controls

Lever one: tell the truth at the interview. Show the real schedule, the real client mix, the real pay cadence, including the hard parts. A realistic preview costs you some applicants on day one and saves you the far more expensive quit on day forty. Lever two: schedule consistency. Anchor each caregiver to a stable set of clients and hours, publish schedules as far ahead as your operation allows, and treat changes as the exception that gets a phone call, not a text the night before. A caregiver builds a life around a predictable schedule, and predictable income is the benefit a small agency can offer that big ones often cannot.

Lever three: pay cadence and accuracy. Pay on the day promised, every time, and make the first paycheck flawless. Errors an office finds routine, a new caregiver reads as a warning about every future paycheck. Lever four: a real first week. Orientation that covers the actual job: the first client visit shadowed by a supervisor or a senior aide, the documentation practiced on a practice entry before a real client's record depends on it, the emergency procedure walked through out loud. A caregiver who has already succeeded at the job once, with support, walks into the second week a different risk than one who was handed keys and a login. Lever five: be reachable, and check in before there is a problem.

Make the check-ins concrete

The check-in is not a meeting and it is not a form. In the first week, one short conversation: what surprised you, what do you need that you do not have, who showed you the ropes and did it help. Around day thirty, the same three questions plus one more: is the schedule still the schedule you agreed to. Around day sixty, ask the question owners are afraid of: what would make you leave. Record the answers, one line each, and fix what is fixable while it is still small. A caregiver whose day thirty complaint about a long drive got answered with a closer case does not become a day seventy no-show. Most ninety day quits announce themselves weeks earlier to anyone who asks.

One more cost belongs in the count, because families feel it before your books do. The client who leaves because their caregiver changed three times in two months. Families buy continuity. Every early quit spends a little of the trust your agency name carries, and that trust is the asset your referral sources lend you. Retention work is not human resources decoration. It is protection of the only inventory a home care agency has, which is trusted people showing up where they promised. And when a caregiver crosses day ninety, say so. Name it to them, plainly and briefly: you made it through the hardest stretch, here is what I have seen you do well, here is what comes next for you here. It takes two minutes, it costs nothing, and it tells the caregiver that somebody in the office counts their time as something other than a schedule slot.

None of this requires a bigger wage than your market can bear, and wages do matter: a caregiver offered two dollars more an hour across town will weigh it. But the owners I watch keep their people at market wages, not top wages, by winning the ninety days: honest hiring, steady schedules, clean pay, real training, and an office that answers. Those five levers are free or cheap. They are also unglamorous, repetitive, and entirely yours. If you are still in the application stage, read this as a build list: write the first week, the check-in rhythm, and the schedule rules into your operations before your first hire, because retrofitting them onto a running schedule is much harder.

Your next step: run your own turnover math this week

Here is the one actionable next step. Count last year's departures. Count how many left inside ninety days. Multiply by what a replacement truly costs you, starting with the published $2,600 floor and adding your overtime and scheduler time. Then pick the lever your numbers point to and work it for one quarter. Retention is not a program you announce. It is a number that moves when the first ninety days stop being an accident.

Turnover benchmarks are industry medians from the Activated Insights Benchmarking Report and its published articles, and the replacement cost figure is an estimate built on the method described above, not a figure for your agency. Your own departure count and your own costs are the numbers to act on.
