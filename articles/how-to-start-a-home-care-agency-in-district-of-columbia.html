How to Start a Home Care Agency in District of Columbia (The Home Support Agency License Is the Non-Medical Door)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The District story starts with a founder who had a strong name, a clean logo, and a licensing problem inside the name itself. She planned a non-medical agency with the word health in its title, in a jurisdiction that bars that word on the non-medical license. I start District work with the frame, because the District frames this business differently from every state in this series: the non-medical door has its own license name, its own fee chapter, its own office rule, and its own naming rule. Founders who import a state map into the District file the wrong chapter at the wrong fee. Here is the full picture, door by door, in the order a founder actually meets it.

Hold the headline first. In the District, non-medical home care is a home support agency. Skilled care is a home care agency. The two names differ by one word, the fees differ by chapter, and the chapters never substitute for each other. An owner who sorts the names before spending sorts the District correctly.

The demand: about 94,000 seniors, the highest income base in this series

Start with why owners keep looking at the District. The Census Bureau puts the District population at 693,645 as of July 1, 2025, and 13.6 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 94,000 seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for District of Columbia puts median household income at $109,870 for 2020 to 2024, the highest income base in this series, which is the base a private pay agency sells into. That senior count is the population figure multiplied by the age 65 and over share, labeled as that math because the Census page publishes the two inputs, not the product.

The senior share is the lowest in this batch, and the density is the point instead: the whole market sits inside 61 square miles, which cuts travel time to a minor cost line and puts referral sources within a short trip of any office. The payer side runs mostly through managed care. DC Healthy Families plans are AmeriHealth Caritas District of Columbia and MedStar Family Choice DC, with Health Services for Children with Special Needs serving children with special needs. Wellpoint DC left the managed care program on August 1, 2026, and its enrollees moved to AmeriHealth Caritas DC, so a plan list that still names Wellpoint or Amerigroup for the District is stale.

The license question, answered directly: the home support agency track is the non-medical door

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in the District? Yes, and the license is the home support agency license under Title 22-B DCMR chapter 99, issued by DC Health through the Health Regulation and Licensing Administration (HRLA). Frame the plan as a home support agency track from the first document: a home support agency provides non-medical services only, including personal care services delivered by qualified home health aides supervised by a registered nurse under section 9917, it keeps a staffed operating office inside the District, and it may not use the word health in its title under chapter 99.

The skilled door is the home care agency license under Title 22-B DCMR chapter 39, covering skilled nursing or at least one other therapeutic service in the home, with home health aide and personal care aide services, homemaker, and chore services on the same license where the agency offers them. Hospice is a hospice license under D.C. Official Code sections 44-501 and 44-502. Residential doors are separate again, and two carry live gates: group homes for persons with intellectual disabilities, licensed as community residence facilities under chapter 35 for 4 to 8 residents, sit behind the Department of Health Care Finance (DHCF) capacity gate, and mental health community residence facilities under Title 22-A DCMR chapter 38 are closed to new certification applications under the Department of Behavioral Health notice of November 14, 2025. DC Health's licensing portal names these settings Community Residential Facilities. No one license covers another license's services.

The non-medical lane: the home support agency license, office first, census before survey

Step one is confirming the service menu is non-skilled: hands-on personal care, homemaker, and companion work only, with no nursing, no therapy, and no skilled tasks, documented in the startup file. Step two is forming the business, holding the EIN and tax registrations, and securing the District operating office chapter 99 requires: a staffed office inside the District, open at least eight hours per business day, with hours posted and the public website section 9901 requires, carrying hours, services, ownership, key personnel, and contact information. A separate license is required for each office, and a virtual-only operating model does not fit this license.

Step three is filing through the DC Health facilities licensing portal with the $1,200 initial application processing fee and the $400 license fee at section 9903.1, with the policies and procedures manual, the governing body and Director record, and the ownership disclosures section 9902.3 lists. Step four is building the screening and training systems before the first visit, covered below. Step five is the license sequence itself: an office walk-through, a 90-day provisional license, with one discretionary renewal of up to 90 more days, during which the agency builds a census of at least five clients, then the on-site survey, then a permanent license for twelve months including the provisional period. No permanent license issues to an agency that has not demonstrated the five-client census and substantial compliance during the provisional period. Renewal is filed at least 90 days before expiration, and the annual renewal processing fee follows the client census at section 9903.1: $800 for 1 to 50 clients, $1,400 for 51 to 150, $2,200 for 151 to 350, and $2,600 for 351 or more, with a duplicate license at $100 and a late fee of $100. Insurance sits at section 9905.1: blanket malpractice coverage of at least $1,000,000 per incident, and comprehensive general liability of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence and $2,000,000 aggregate. Section 9904.1 is the license fee section, not the insurance section, and merging the two is a filing error. For Medicaid work, enroll through the DC Provider Data Management System (DCPDMS) for State Plan personal care aide services or EPD Waiver personal care, and configure EVV through Sandata before the first billed visit.

The skilled lane: the home care agency license at $600, behind a certificate of need test

Run the certificate of need test before any other filing on this lane. Establishing a new home health service is establishing a new institutional health service, and a home health agency with an annual operating budget of at least $500,000 is a health care facility under D.C. Official Code section 44-401(10), as amended by D.C. Law 26-7 effective June 10, 2025, so a certificate of need under section 44-406 through the State Health Planning and Development Agency (SHPDA) sits in front of the license at that scale. The 2025 amendment raised the general capital thresholds, to amounts exceeding $15,000,000 for hospitals and $5,000,000 for other facilities; the $500,000 operating budget line is the figure that decides most home health filings, and the older $3.5 million capital figure in secondary summaries is superseded.

File the home care agency application through the facilities licensing portal with the $600 initial application fee at section 3902.1 and the policy file, and hold services until the license issues. The annual fee follows the patient census: $400 for 1 to 150 patients, $700 for 151 to 750, $1,100 for 751 to 1,250, and $1,300 for 1,251 or more, with a $100 late fee. Trap figure: the $1,200 home support agency processing fee at chapter 99 belongs to the non-medical license, and the two chapters price different licenses. The Director must be a licensed physician, a licensed registered nurse, or a person with training and experience in health services administration including at least one year of supervisory or administrative experience in home health care or a related program, under section 3904. Insurance at section 3901.1 is blanket malpractice coverage of at least $1,000,000 per incident and general liability of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence, with no $1,000,000 aggregate figure in that section. Enroll with DC Medicaid for home health services through DCPDMS after licensure, with managed care credentialing after DHCF approval, and configure EVV through Sandata.

The certified and hospice lanes: a District processing hold sits on top of the federal gate

Medicare certification layers on the District license, after the certificate of need determination. Enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS to CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for the District, with the federal enrollment fee of $750 for 2026. Novitas Solutions, Jurisdiction L, is the A/B desk only, and sending a home health enrollment to Novitas is the local form of the wrong-desk error; Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction A, is the DME desk. The federal gate: initial enrollment is blocked under CMS-6101-N2, effective November 12, 2026, with no end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). Build to the Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR Part 484 and hold the certification before billing Medicare.

Hospice carries two gates and one missing fee, and all three belong in the budget. The District fee posture is plain: NO FEE PUBLISHED for the District hospice license fee. No hospice fee schedule was located in the chapter 31 schedule at section 3114, which prices community residence facilities, ambulatory surgical centers, nursing homes, and intermediate care facilities only, in the DC Health hospice materials, or in the licensing portal checked for this build, so confirm the current filing fee in the portal at filing and never budget the $600 chapter 39 fee or the $1,200 chapter 99 fee as the hospice fee. The certificate of need test applies to hospice at the same $500,000 operating budget line. And the processing hold is District-level: DC Health will not process initial hospice Medicare certification requests until the federal moratorium is lifted, on top of the federal gate itself at CMS-6102-N2, which blocks initial Medicare hospice enrollment on the same six-month extension terms. A hospice plan that sequences the state license first and treats the federal certification as a held filing, not a dated opening, plans the District correctly.

Background checks, aide training, and the gates on the residential doors

On the home support track, build the criminal background checks the District requires for unlicensed personnel, the OIG and SAM exclusion checks, and the home health aide qualification file under 17 DCMR section 9300 and following, with registered nurse supervision of personal care aides under section 9917, before the first visit. On the skilled track, home health aides complete 75 hours of training, including 16 hours of practical training, with a competency evaluation under section 3915, and 12 hours of continuing education each year only after the first year. Stating the continuing education duty as a first-year duty misreads the rule, and survey files get cited for the mirror error, training records that skip the practical hours.

The residential gates belong in this section because founders meet them as background facts and misread them as home care rules. The ICF/IID capacity gate is verified-true: since June 1, 2018, DHCF denies all new ICF/IID Medicaid enrollment applications, the District is at capacity, and DC Health refrains from issuing community residence facility licenses for individuals with intellectual disabilities until the hold lifts, so the gate sits on the group home and ICF/IID tracks, priced at $50 to $150 by bed count at section 3114.4, and on no home-based track. The DBH certification moratorium under the amended notice of November 14, 2025 accepts applications only for ASAM Levels 3.1, 3.3, 3.5 Adult, 3.5 Adolescent, and 3.7-WM, plus one clinic door, and closes mental health community residence facilities under chapter 38 and substance use doors outside those levels. Neither gate touches the home support or home care agency licenses, and no District Medicaid enrollment moratorium for home health or hospice was located in the sources checked.

What you still do today: form the business the District way

Form the business, get the EIN from the IRS, hold the District tax registrations, and secure the staffed District office before the home support filing, because the office is part of the license, not an overhead choice after it. Then do the ordinary employer load in the right order: insurance at the chapter figures above in place before the first client, written policies and procedures covering the services you will actually sell, background screening and aide training set up as systems rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do. Choose the agency name last, after the chapter check, because a home support agency may not use the word health in its title. I print no District formation fee here because no formation fee figure sits in the primary sources this article is built from.

The practical warning

Three District traps cost new owners money. The first is the fee chapter trap: chapter 99 prices the non-medical home support agency at $1,200 plus $400, chapter 39 prices the skilled home care agency at $600 to file, and a budget that swaps them underfunds the non-medical filing by half or overstates the skilled one. The second is the office trap: a home-based or virtual-only operating model does not fit a license that requires a staffed office inside the District, open at least eight hours per business day, with a separate license for each office. The third is the hospice shortcut: budgeting a borrowed fee for a license whose District fee is not published, and publishing a Medicare opening date while DC Health holds initial hospice certification processing until the moratorium lifts. In the District, the frame is the filing, and founders who respect the frame open on schedule.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a home care agency in District of Columbia?

You start on the home support agency track for non-medical care: secure the staffed District office chapter 99 requires, then file the home support agency application through the DC Health facilities licensing portal. Skilled care runs the certificate of need test first, then files the home care agency license under chapter 39. Hospice files its own license, with Medicare certification processing held while the federal moratorium runs. What are the District of Columbia home care license requirements?

District home-based care runs on separate DC Health licenses through HRLA. A home support agency license (Title 22-B DCMR chapter 99) covers non-medical care, requires a staffed District office open at least eight hours per business day, and bars the word health in the agency title. A home care agency license (chapter 39) covers skilled nursing and therapeutic services, with a Director who meets section 3904. Hospice is licensed under D.C. Official Code sections 44-501 and 44-502. A certificate of need under section 44-406 gates a new home health agency or hospice at the $500,000 annual operating budget line.

How much does a home care license cost in District of Columbia?

The home support agency filing is a $1,200 initial application processing fee plus a $400 license fee under section 9903.1, with census-based renewal of $800 for 1 to 50 clients up to $2,600 for 351 or more. The home care agency initial fee is $600 under section 3902.1, with census-based annual fees of $400 to $1,300. For the District hospice license there is NO FEE PUBLISHED; confirm the current fee in the DC Health portal at filing. The federal CMS-855A fee is $750 for 2026.
