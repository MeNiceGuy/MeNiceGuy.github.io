4 Clients to Break Even: The Home Care Break-Even Math, Worked Step by Step

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 23, 2026

An owner I was advising had three clients, a waiting caregiver roster, and a bank balance that went down every month even though the schedule looked busy. She was not careless and she was not undercharging by much. She had simply never run the one calculation that tells a home care agency whether busy and profitable are the same thing: the break-even point, the number of clients, and underneath it the number of billable hours, at which revenue finally covers every cost. Below that number, each month is a slow loan from your savings to your agency. Above it, the agency starts paying you back. This article works the math the way I walk it in the video, with three numbers you already have, verified anchors for the two figures owners guess at most, and the worked example behind the title: four clients.

The three numbers, and the formula they feed

Break-even needs three inputs. First, your monthly fixed costs: the bills that arrive whether you serve one client or twenty, such as insurance installments, scheduling and EVV software, office costs, licenses spread across the year, phone, and basic marketing. Second, your average revenue per client per month: your bill rate times the average billable hours one client actually uses. Third, your average variable cost per client per month: the caregiver wages for those hours, the payroll burden on those wages, and the travel and supply costs that only exist because that client is on the schedule.

Subtract the variable cost from the revenue and you get the contribution per client, the amount each client leaves behind to cover your fixed costs. Then divide: fixed costs divided by contribution per client, rounded up to the next whole client, is your break-even point. You cannot serve a fraction of a client, so the rounding is not a detail. The formula is the same one the free calculator in the video runs, and it is deliberately simple, because an owner who cannot run it in five minutes will not run it at all.

Anchor the two numbers owners guess at

The math is only as honest as the rate and the wage you feed it, so anchor both to published figures before you use your own. For the bill rate, the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey puts the national median for non-medical caregiver services at $35 an hour, up 3 percent, or $80,080 a year on the survey basis of 44 hours a week, drawn from more than 25,000 rates gathered between July and November 2025. For the wage, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the May 2025 median for home health and personal care aides at $17.21 an hour, $35,800 a year. Medians are not your market, and your state and city figures will differ, sometimes by a lot. They are a sanity check: if your worked example uses a $50 rate and a $14 wage in a market sitting near both medians, the example is fiction and the break-even it produces is fiction too.

One more anchor matters, because owners forget it in the wage line: the burden. The wage is never the cost of the wage. The employer share of Social Security and Medicare alone is 7.65 percent, 6.2 percent plus 1.45 percent, per IRS Topic No. 751, before workers compensation and unemployment insurance are added. In the worked example below I use an 18 percent planning burden and label it as exactly that, a planning estimate in the 15 to 20 percent range I tell owners to test, not a quote for your agency. Your workers compensation class and your state unemployment rate will move your real figure, which is why you replace it with your own before you act on the answer.

The worked example: where four clients comes from

Take a lean new agency with $5,200 a month in fixed costs. Say the average client uses 100 billable hours a month at the $35 median rate: revenue per client is $3,500 a month. Pay the caregiver $18 an hour, a little above the BLS median, because recruiting at the median is hard in most markets: wages per client are $1,800. Add the 18 percent planning burden on those wages, $324, and $76 a month in travel and supplies for that client, and the variable cost per client is $2,200. The contribution per client is $3,500 minus $2,200, which is $1,300.

Now divide: $5,200 divided by $1,300 is exactly 4. Four average clients cover every cost in this example, and the fifth client is the first one that produces profit. Check it in hours, because hours are what you actually schedule: the contribution per billable hour is $35 minus $18 in wages, minus $3.24 in burden, minus $0.76 in travel and supplies, which is $13 an hour, and $5,200 divided by $13 is 400 billable hours a month, about 92 hours a week. Four clients at 100 hours a month and 400 billable hours a month are the same answer seen from two sides, and when your two views of the number disagree, one of your inputs is wrong.

What moves the number, and the warning that comes with it

Three levers move your break-even, and they do not move it equally. Fixed costs move it dollar for dollar: every $1,300 of monthly overhead you add in this example costs you one whole client you must find and keep just to stand still, which is why a signed office lease in month one is a break-even decision, not a branding decision. The spread moves it faster: if the wage rises to $20 an hour with the same burden and travel, the contribution per client falls to about $1,064, and the same $5,200 in fixed costs now needs five clients, not four. A two dollar raise, given without rerunning this math, quietly raised the target by a full client. Hours per client move it too: the same agency serving clients who average 60 hours a month instead of 100 needs seven clients to reach the same 400 hours.

Here is the practical warning. The formula assumes every paid hour is a billed hour, and that is never quite true. Training time, travel between visits, a covered call-off, and the gap week between one client's discharge and the next client's start are paid hours that bill nothing. If a tenth of your paid hours go unbilled, your effective contribution drops and the real break-even sits above the one your calculator printed. Track billed hours against paid hours every week; that ratio is where break-even estimates go to be corrected. And if your contribution per client comes out at zero or below, no number of clients fixes it. The calculator cannot save a rate that does not cover its own variable cost. That is a pricing problem to solve before it is a marketing problem to outrun.

Your next step: run your three numbers this week

Here is the one actionable next step. Add up last month's real fixed costs, take your average revenue and variable cost per client from the same month, and run the division. Then write the answer where you will see it: break-even is this many clients, this many billable hours a week. Rerun it every month with actuals, and rerun it before any raise, lease, or hire that changes one of the three inputs. Owners do not usually fail because the math was hard. They fail because the number was never run while there was still time to change it.

The worked example above uses illustrative figures built on the published medians and the labeled planning burden described in the text; they are an example to learn the method from, not figures for your agency, and they are not a quote of any price or rate. Your own fixed costs, rates, wages, and burden are the numbers to act on.
