168 Hours a Week: The Real Math of a Residential Care Home

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

She had found the house first, and that was the problem I had to walk her back from. An owner I was advising had a ranch house on a quiet street, a price she could carry, and a plan that read like a business: six bedrooms, six residents, care around the clock. What she did not have yet was a license category, a zoning answer, or the coverage math. In a residential care home, the house is the business. Residents live there full time and get help with daily life: meals, bathing, dressing, medication reminders, supervision. That is not a large assisted living facility with a hundred units and a dining hall, and it is not a home care agency either. An agency sends caregivers into a client home and the shift ends. In a residential home, the care never clocks out, and that one fact drives everything else: the license, the building rules, the staffing, and the math. This article walks the order I give owners in her position: category first, zoning second, license and survey third, the 168 hour math running underneath all of it, and the house only when the first three say yes.

Your state picks the category before you pick anything

Before the house, before the furniture, before anything with a price tag, there is a decision you do not get to make: your state decides what this business is called and which license category it falls in. In one state the same house is an assisted living facility. In another it is a personal care home, a residential care home, or an adult care home. The names are not decoration. Each category and tier sets the level of need, the acuity, you are allowed to serve. Texas is the clean example. Under 26 TAC section 553.5, a facility must be licensed as Type A or Type B, and the type turns on the residents themselves: in a Type A facility a resident must be physically and mentally capable of evacuating without physical assistance from staff, must not require routine attendance during nighttime sleeping hours, and must be capable of following directions under emergency conditions. In a Type B facility a resident may require staff assistance to evacuate, may require attendance during nighttime sleeping hours, and may be incapable of following directions in an emergency, but must not be permanently bedfast. Pick the category wrong and you have licensed a house for residents you never intended to serve, or you have built a house for residents your license cannot admit.

Zoning and the house set your real bed count

Once the category is clear, the house has to survive two reviews before it houses anyone. The first is local: zoning. A residential street does not automatically allow a care business, and those rules live with your city or county, not with the state licensing office. Some areas allow small care homes by right, some require a permit or a hearing, and some say no. Get that answer as a letter or an email you can keep before you sign anything. The second review is the building itself: fire and life safety requirements, accessibility, and the simple physical facts of the house, how many bedrooms, how many bathrooms, whether residents can get out safely. Those facts, not your business plan, set your real bed count. A house that looks like an eight bed business in a listing can come out of the fire review as a five bed business, and every number after that changes. This is the practical warning I give owners at this stage: never let a purchase contract run ahead of the zoning answer and the category. A house you cannot license for the residents you planned to serve is not a business asset. It is an expensive lesson with a mortgage.

Licensing, the manager question, and the survey

Then comes the licensing work itself. You apply to your state licensing agency and submit policies that describe how the home actually runs: admissions, medication handling, incidents, staffing, resident rights. The state checks backgrounds, on you and on the people you hire. Some states also qualify the person running the home. Maryland, for example, requires an assisted living manager employed by a program to complete a Department approved manager training course of at least 80 hours with an examination, plus 20 hours of Department approved continuing education every two years, under Maryland law and the Office of Health Care Quality guidance built on Health-General section 19-1807 and COMAR 10.07.14. Texas sets its own manager qualifications inside its assisted living rules. After the paperwork comes the survey, where an inspector walks the house and tests whether what you wrote matches what you do. That is why I tell owners the same thing in every state: write policies you can actually run at three in the morning, because that is the operation the survey is really checking, and the one your residents will live with every night after the inspector leaves.

The math: one post is 168 hours a week

Now the number in the title. Care in a residential home runs around the clock, and one staff post covered 24 hours a day, seven days a week, is 168 hours of labor every week, about 728 hours a month. Divide 168 by a 40 hour week and one post takes about 4.2 full time people, and that is the floor, before sick days, vacations, and turnover. In my professional judgment, a plan that survives real life staffs that post with relief built in, closer to five people sharing the post across a month. Now price the floor. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the May 2025 national median for home health and personal care aides at $35,800 a year, $17.21 an hour, in its Occupational Outlook Handbook. At that median, 728 hours of coverage costs about $12,529 a month in wages alone for a single post. Add a labeled 18 percent planning burden for payroll taxes, unemployment insurance, and workers compensation, and the same post runs about $14,784 a month, before the mortgage or lease, food for every resident every day, insurance, utilities, and licensing costs. Revenue runs the other direction: beds multiplied by your monthly per bed rate, multiplied by how full you are. Run the two sides against each other before you fall for a house. As a labeled illustration only, at a $4,500 monthly per bed rate, covering that one loaded staff post takes the full rent of about 3.3 beds, and nothing else in the home has been paid yet. In a small home, one empty bed is not a rounding error. In a six bed home it is about a sixth of your revenue, and it can be the whole margin. Price the bed from the coverage cost upward, never from what the house down the street charges.

Who pays: private pay carries the plan

So who pays the per bed rate? At this size, plan on private pay doing most of the work: families paying from savings, income, and sometimes long term care insurance. Correct one assumption before it gets into your plan. Medicare does not pay for this. The Medicare long term care page says it plainly: Medicare does not pay for long term care, and since most long term care is non-medical, Medicare and most health insurance, including Medicare Supplement Insurance, do not pay for long term care services in a nursing home or in the community. The daily help a care home provides is exactly that kind of care. Medicaid is narrower than most people expect, too. In some states a Medicaid waiver will pay for care services delivered in a residential setting. But under 42 CFR 441.310(a)(2), federal payment for home and community based waiver services is not available for the cost of room and board, apart from a narrow respite exception in a state approved facility that is not a private residence. Even where a waiver helps with services, the resident still owes the living part of the bill. Run your plan on private pay, and treat anything else as a bonus, not a foundation.

The three mistakes that kill these homes

In the records I review and the owners I advise, the same three mistakes end these businesses, and all three happen before the first resident moves in. Mistake one: buying the house first. The owner falls for a property, signs, and then learns the license category or the zoning does not fit, and now owns a house that cannot legally do the job. Mistake two: underpricing the bed. The rate gets set by looking at competitors instead of by counting the 168 hours, so the home fills up and still loses money. A full house at the wrong rate just loses money faster. Mistake three: staffing math that quietly assumes the owner never sleeps. The owner covers every night shift personally, the plan works in the spreadsheet, and the first month the owner is sick or burned out, the whole schedule collapses. Build the plan for the operator you will be in month six, not the one you are on signing day. Your next step: run the order, in order

Here is the one actionable next step, and it costs nothing this week. Before you tour another house, get two answers for the state and the street you are actually looking at: the license category and tier your planned residents fall under, from your state licensing agency, and the zoning answer for small care homes at that address, from your city or county, kept as a letter or an email. Then run the 168 hour math at your local wage, loaded, and divide your true monthly cost by the per bed rate your market actually pays. If the beds that survive the fire review cannot carry one staff post plus the house costs, the answer is a different house, a different category, or a different rate, and it is far cheaper to learn that this week than after a closing. Category first, zoning second, license and survey third, the math underneath all of it, and the house only when the first three say yes.

Licensing categories, manager qualifications, zoning, and payer rules vary by state and locality and change over time. The Texas and Maryland points here are labeled examples from those states rules, the wage figure is a national median, and the per bed rate and burden figures are labeled planning illustrations, not quotes for your market. Verify your category, your zoning, and your numbers with your state licensing agency and your local zoning office before you act.
