Surveyors Pull Five Files: Build the Client File That Holds Up

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 21, 2026

The surveyor did not start with a tour. She sat down, asked for a small sample of client files and a small sample of caregiver files, and started reading. The owner I was advising had good caregivers and clients who liked them, and none of that was in the room. What was in the room was paper: an assessment that did not match the plan of care built from it, visit notes that were signed but not dated, and a caregiver file with training records for tasks the caregiver was not assigned and no competency record for the task she actually performed every week. Nothing in those files said the care was bad. The files simply could not prove the care happened the way the agency said it did. That is the moment most owners learn the rule that runs every survey and audit I review: if it is not documented, a reviewer treats it as if it did not happen. This article walks what a surveyor actually asks for, file by file, the two habits that generate findings out of proportion to their size, and the quarterly self audit that lets you find the gaps before a stranger does.

How a surveyor reads: three sets of records, compared against each other

A survey is not a mystery inspection. A surveyor or auditor works from lists. On the client side, they pull a sample of client files and check whether each file tells one consistent story from intake to today. On the caregiver side, they pull personnel files and check whether every person who entered a client home was cleared, trained, and supervised the way your license and your own policies require. Then they compare the two sets against each other and against your billing. The findings live in the gaps between those three. They may also interview caregivers and clients, and what people say has to match what the files say. A caregiver who describes a different visit routine than the plan of care describes is a finding in conversation form. If you are still in the application stage, read this as a build list, not a warning list: the file structure you set up before your first client is the structure a surveyor will read in your first survey year.

The client file that holds up

The client file that holds up opens with the intake and admission record and the signed service agreement, so anyone can see what the client agreed to and when. It carries the initial assessment and every reassessment after it, current, dated, and signed. It carries the plan of care built from that assessment, and this is where weak files start to tear, because the plan says one thing and the visit notes say another. It carries the orders and authorizations behind the services, so every billed hour traces back to something approved. Then it carries the visit documentation itself: notes for each visit, authenticated, dated, and timed.

For Medicare certified home health agencies, that shape is not a preference. It is the Condition of Participation at 42 CFR 484.110. The clinical record must include the current comprehensive assessment, including all assessments from the most recent admission, clinical notes, plans of care, and practitioner orders. It must include all interventions and the patient response to them, the goals in the plan of care and progress toward them, and contact information for the patient, any representative, and the primary caregivers, plus the practitioner who will provide care after discharge. The discharge and transfer clocks are in the same section: a completed discharge summary sent within 5 business days of discharge, and a transfer summary sent within 2 business days of a planned transfer, or within 2 business days of becoming aware of an unplanned transfer. Every entry must be legible, clear, complete, and appropriately authenticated, dated, and timed, with authentication that includes a signature and title, or a secured computer entry by a unique identifier. Clinical records must be retained for 5 years after discharge, unless your state law requires longer, and a record must be made available to the patient, free of charge, on request at the next home visit or within 4 business days, whichever comes first. Whatever your license type, build your client file to that standard and you will satisfy almost any reviewer who sits down with it. The practical warning here is specific: the most common tear I see in the records I review is not a missing file. It is a plan of care that was never updated after a reassessment, so the visit notes, the authorization, and the plan describe three different levels of service.

The caregiver file, where findings cluster

The caregiver file is where I see the most findings per folder. It should show, in order: the application and identity documents, the background check result and the date it cleared, evidence the person was screened before working alone with clients, credentials and competency evaluations for the tasks they actually perform, orientation and training records with dates and topics, health screening records where your state or your policy requires them, and the ongoing file: annual evaluations, in-service training, and supervision records.

One item deserves its own paragraph. The HHS Office of Inspector General maintains the List of Excluded Individuals and Entities, the LEIE, and the effect of an exclusion is that no federal health care program payment may be made for items or services furnished by an excluded person. OIG guidance is that health care entities should routinely check the list to ensure that new hires and current employees are not on it, because anyone who hires an individual or entity on the LEIE may be subject to civil monetary penalties. The LEIE quick tips page adds the step owners skip: a matching name alone is not a verification. Verify identity using the Social Security number for an individual or the Employer Identification Number for an entity in the online search. File the dated result of that check, at hire and on the rescreening cycle you follow, so a reviewer can see when it was run, not just that your policy says it is run.

Two habits that generate findings: supervision records and the incident log

Two documentation habits generate findings out of proportion to their size. The first is supervisory visits. Your license and your payers set how often a supervisor must observe care in the home, and the exact interval varies by state and license type, so check your own rule instead of borrowing another state number. Every one of those visits needs a record: who visited, when, what was observed, and what was corrected. A supervision schedule that exists only on a calendar, with no visit notes behind it, reads to a surveyor as supervision that never happened.

The second is the incident log. Falls, injuries, missed visits, client complaints, and hospitalizations belong in one log with dates, what happened, what you did, and who you notified. Owners hide incidents because they fear the log looks bad. Surveyors read it the other way. A complete incident log with follow through is evidence of an agency that watches itself. An empty log at an agency that has operated for two years is the red flag.

The trace test, and the verification trail underneath it

There is one more test your files have to pass, and owners rarely prepare for it because it does not look like a document check. The surveyor traces a paid claim backward. They pick a claim you were paid for and walk it down: show me the authorization behind these units, show me the visit note, show me the caregiver file, show me that this caregiver was cleared and trained to do this task on this date. Every link in that chain is a document you either have or do not have, and the chain breaks at its weakest link, which is usually the visit note that never got authenticated or the competency evaluation that was never filed.

Underneath all of this sits the habit that makes the rest cheap to run: keep a verification trail. For every requirement your agency operates under, keep the requirement, the source it came from, and the dated evidence that you checked your operation against it. When you review a file, record the review. When you check a credential, record the check. When a policy changes, keep the old version with its dates. A reviewer trusts a trail because a trail is hard to fake after the fact, and it answers the question behind every finding, which is not did you care, but can you show me.

If a finding lands: the plan of correction is a work order

When a finding does land, because at some point one will, know how the rest of the process works so it does not push you into bad decisions. A finding is not the end of the story. The response is. Survey and audit findings normally come with a statement of what was found and a window to respond with a plan of correction: what you fixed, how you fixed it, who is responsible, how you will monitor it, and the date it was done. Agencies hurt themselves here in two ways: arguing with the finding instead of correcting the system behind it, and promising new behavior with no monitoring step behind the promise. The agencies that recover cleanly treat the plan of correction as a work order, assign every line a name and a date, and then file the evidence that the fix held. A single corrected finding, handled that way, closes. The same finding repeated at the next survey is what escalates, because repetition tells the reviewer the system never changed.

Your next step: audit yourself before they do

Here is the one actionable next step, and it costs nothing but an hour. This week, pull five client files and five caregiver files at random, not the ones you are proud of, and read them the way a surveyor reads, as a stranger who knows nothing about your agency except what the record proves. Check each client file for the assessment, plan, authorization, and authenticated visit notes telling one story. Check each caregiver file for the background check date, the exclusion screening result, and a competency record for the tasks that person actually performs. Write down every gap as a list. Fix the list in order of risk: files that touch client safety first, billing support second, formatting last. Repeat it quarterly. In the agencies I work with, the ones that pass clean are not the ones with the fanciest software. They are the ones whose owner reads their own files like a reviewer four times a year, starting before the first survey is ever scheduled.

File contents, supervision intervals, background check cycles, and retention periods vary by state, license type, and payer, and the federal clinical record standard at 42 CFR 484.110 applies directly to Medicare certified home health agencies. Verify every requirement that applies to your agency against your own state rule, your license, and your payer contracts before you act.
