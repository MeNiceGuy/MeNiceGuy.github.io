How to Start a Home Care Agency in Arizona (No State License for Private Pay Non-Medical Care)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 26, 2026

The search usually starts with a license. Someone in Phoenix, Tucson, or Mesa decides to start a non-medical home care agency, goes looking for the Arizona application, and keeps landing on home health pages that talk about skilled nursing, physicians, and clinical records. That mismatch is the moment most new Arizona owners get stuck, because they are looking for a license Arizona does not issue for the business they actually want to run.

Arizona does not require a state license for a private pay agency that provides only non-medical supportive services at home: companionship, homemaking, meal help, transportation, and hands-on help with daily living that is not skilled care. I walk that line in the video below, and this article walks it in the same order, with every number checked against a primary source. The short version is this: no license does not mean no work, and it does not mean no standards. It means your preparation, not a state application, is the gate between you and your first client.

The demand is real, and it is large

Start with why Arizona draws so many new owners. The Census Bureau puts Arizona population at 7,623,818 as of July 1, 2025, and 20.6 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.57 million seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Arizona puts median household income at $79,964 for 2020 to 2024, which matters because private pay home care is bought out of household income.

The price families face explains the business case. In the Genworth and CareScout 2024 Cost of Care Survey, the Arizona median for homemaker services is $80,080 a year, at a reported $35 an hour, based on 44 hours a week for 52 weeks. The home health aide median in the same survey is $82,368 a year. Those are medians for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. They show that Arizona families are already paying for help at home at a level that can support a real agency, if the agency is built right.

In my own 50 state market analysis, Arizona also showed the thinnest agency density I found, about 0.13 Medicare certified agencies for every 1,000 seniors. That is my analysis, labeled as such, not a state count of every non-medical agency. Treat it for what it proves and no more: the licensed medical side is thin, and a well run private pay agency is not walking into a saturated licensed market.

The line Arizona actually draws: home health versus supportive services

Arizona law licenses home health agencies. It does not create a license category for a private pay non-medical agency in the same way. Under Arizona Revised Statutes section 36-425.01, the department by rule shall license home health agencies, and the licensure period does not exceed two years. The Arizona Department of Health Services, ADHS, runs that licensing work through its Public Health Licensing pages.

The definition does the rest of the work. Under section 36-151, a home health agency is primarily engaged in providing skilled nursing services and other therapeutic services, has policies set by professional personnel including physicians and registered nurses to govern those services, provides for supervision by a physician or registered nurse, and maintains clinical records on all patients. The same section defines supportive services as services other than home health services that may reasonably be expected to help maintain an individual in his home as an alternative to institutionalization, including homemaker services, meal services, general maintenance services, and transportation services.

That is the line to build on. If your service list is supportive services only, you are describing the non-medical lane. If your service list, your website, or your brochure promises skilled nursing, therapy, wound care, infusions, or other therapeutic work, you have described a home health agency, and that agency needs the ADHS license. Do not market across that line and operate on the other side of it.

What no license does not remove

No state home care license removes the application. It does not remove the business. You still form an entity, get an employer identification number, carry liability insurance and workers compensation where required for your staff, write policies, hire and train caregivers, and keep records a family or a referral source can inspect.

On the entity step, Arizona is specific about where you file. A limited liability company files Articles of Organization with the Arizona Corporation Commission, not with a secretary of state style office. On the Commission fee schedule for limited liability companies, revised March 2026, Articles of Organization are $50 for regular processing and $85 for expedited processing. Arizona LLCs do not file an annual report with the Commission. Name a statutory agent with an Arizona street address, keep that information current, and check whether your city requires a local business license before you open. Those are ordinary business steps, but they are the steps that make you an agency instead of a person with a flyer.

Policies are not back office decoration in a no license state. They are your proof. Write down, before the first client, how you screen and train caregivers, how you build and change a care plan, how you handle a missed visit, a fall, a medication reminder that stays within the non-medical line, a complaint, and an emergency. Families vet you. Hospitals, discharge planners, and other referral sources ask for your policies and your insurance before they send anyone your way. When anyone can open, professionalism is what sets you apart, and professionalism has to be written down to be believed.

The Medicaid lane is a different lane

Private pay with no license is not the same as Medicaid work. If you want to serve Arizona Long Term Care System members through the Arizona Health Care Cost Containment System, AHCCCS, the rules change. AHCCCS has training and testing requirements for Direct Care Workers who provide attendant care, personal care, and homemaker services to ALTCS members in their own homes. On the AHCCCS Direct Care Worker Training and Testing Program page, AHCCCS states that all AHCCCS provider agencies of direct care services must use Direct Care Workers who have passed the required competency tests within 90 days of hire, unless the worker is exempt, for example a registered nurse, licensed practical nurse, or certified nurse aide. An agency can train and test its own workers only as an approved program, or it can delegate that work to an approved program by contract.

Keep the lanes separate in your plan. Getting hired by private pay families does not enroll you with AHCCCS, and enrolling with AHCCCS does not turn skilled work into non-medical work. Decide which payer you are building for first, then build the training and records that payer requires.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Arizona owner. The risk in a no license state is not that the state slows you down. The risk is that you slow yourself down by treating no license as no preparation, or that you drift across the skilled line in your marketing because a family asks for one more task. A caregiver who is hired for companionship and homemaking cannot quietly become the person managing wound care or insulin. Stop, write the service list down, and refer skilled needs to a licensed home health agency. That boundary protects the client, the caregiver, and the business you are trying to build.

The second risk is invisibility. A thin licensed market does not fill your census for you. Families cannot hire an agency they have never heard of, and referral sources cannot refer to an agency that has not shown them insurance, policies, and a clear service list. In Arizona, speed to open only helps if visibility and proof move at the same speed.

Your next step

Write your service list down first, in plain language, and check every item against the home health definition and the supportive services definition above. If the list is clean, form the entity with the Arizona Corporation Commission, get the employer identification number, bind insurance, and write the policies and caregiver training plan before you take the first client. That order is the whole game in Arizona. Owners who follow it open as a business. Owners who reverse it open as a risk.
