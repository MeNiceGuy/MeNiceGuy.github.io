Hire Off Workload, Not Census: When a Home Care Agency Actually Needs a Director

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 23, 2026

An agency owner asked a question this week that I hear in some form every month: when did you hire a director or an assistant director, and at what point in the business did you do it?

Almost every owner who asks it has a number in mind. How many clients. How many caregivers on the roster. How much monthly revenue. They want the census figure that makes the hire make sense.

Census is the wrong trigger. Census tells you what the business earns. It tells you nothing about what the business is costing you.

Workload is the trigger. Here is the test I give owners. When you are the backup scheduler, the intake person, and the one on the phone every night, the business is already paying for a director. It is just paying with your time instead of a salary. Every coverage gap routes back to you. Every family complaint waits for you. Every intake decision sits until you can get to it. You are doing the director's job on top of the owner's job, and the owner's job is the one suffering for it.

So the question is never really when can I afford a director. It is whether you keep paying for one in hours you never get back.

The mistake that makes owners swear off the role

The failure I see most is not hiring too early or too late. It is hiring the title and skipping the duties.

A director without written duties turns into an expensive assistant. She has the title and the salary, but every real decision still waits on your call, because nobody wrote down which decisions are hers. Scheduling conflict? Ask the owner. Caregiver keeps calling out? Ask the owner. Family is unhappy? Ask the owner. Six months later the owner concludes that directors do not work, and goes back to doing everything, now with a sour story about the one time he tried to delegate.

The role did not fail. The role was never defined. A person cannot own decisions that were never handed over in writing.

The four decisions your director has to own

Before you interview anybody, you should be able to list the decisions the director owns. If you cannot write the list, you are not ready to hire, no matter what your census says.

Start with these four:

  1. Scheduling and coverage. Filling call-outs, approving schedule changes, and deciding who covers an open shift without calling you first.
  2. Caregiver discipline, day to day. Late arrivals, missed documentation, dress and conduct issues, handled against the standards in your policies, with final termination decisions staying with you.
  3. Family complaints and service recovery. The first call, the fix, and the follow-up, inside limits you set in writing for what she can offer a family to make something right.
  4. Intake screening. Saying yes or no to a new client against criteria you wrote down, so referrals stop waiting in your inbox.

If a decision is not on the list, it stays with you. Pricing, contracts, payer agreements, and anything that changes what the business promises stay with the owner. The director runs the operation inside lines you draw. That is not a small job. Done right, it is the job that gives you your evenings back.

And if your honest list comes out to nothing but scheduling, you do not need a director. You need a scheduler, and that is a cheaper and simpler hire.

The two-week test that writes the job description for you

Do not write the job description from a template. Write it from your own last two weeks.

For two weeks, write down every task that only you can do today. Not tasks you prefer to do. Tasks that would stop if you did not do them. Keep the list where you will actually use it, a notes app, a legal pad on the desk, whatever survives a busy day.

At the end of the two weeks, sort the list into three piles:

  • Decisions someone else could own if the criteria were written down. This pile is your director's job description, in your agency's own language.
  • Tasks someone else could do with a checklist. This pile goes to the office help you already have, and half the time it turns out you never needed a new hire for these at all.
  • The true owner list. What is left is usually shorter than owners expect, and seeing it that short is what makes delegation feel safe instead of reckless.

Interview against the first pile. A candidate who can talk through how she would handle your actual scheduling conflicts, your actual call-out problem, and your actual difficult families is worth ten candidates who interview well against a generic posting.

Two warnings before you post the job

First, do not promote your best caregiver into an undefined director role and call it a career path. You will lose your strongest caregiver and gain an expensive assistant, and you will have damaged a good employee in the process. If she is the right person, she still needs the written duties, the decision list, and the authority that goes with them, same as an outside hire.

Second, do not hire before the list exists and then promise yourself you will define the role later. Later never comes, because the new director's first month fills up with whatever the office throws at her, and the pattern sets before you ever sit down to fix it. The two weeks of writing come first. They are the cheapest part of this entire decision.

The answer the log gives you

If your two-week log comes back nearly empty, you have your answer. You do not need a director yet, and hiring one would be buying a title to feel like a bigger business.

If it comes back full, then be honest about what you were asking. You were never really asking when. You were asking for permission to stop carrying all of it yourself.

Start the log Monday. Two weeks from now you will know exactly what you need, and you will hire against a list instead of a hope.

This is my professional judgment from consulting work with home care agency owners. It is general business guidance, not legal advice, and your state's licensing rules may set specific requirements for administrator roles, so check those before you title the position.
