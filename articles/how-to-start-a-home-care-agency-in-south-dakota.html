How to Start a Home Care Agency in South Dakota (No State License for Home Care Agencies)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The South Dakota story starts with a founder who kept hunting for a license application that the state does not publish. She had priced insurance, she had caregivers ready to talk, and she had a folder of forms for three other states, none of which matched anything a South Dakota agency sent her. When I walked the question back to the source with her, the answer was sitting on the Department of Health's own page in plain words: non-medical providers who provide in-home care such as bathing, light-duty housekeeping, or meal preparation are not regulated by the department, and South Dakota does not require state licensure for home health agencies either. There is no license to find because there is no license. That is not the same as no rules and it is not the same as no work. The doors that do exist in South Dakota are payer doors and federal doors, Medicaid enrollment with its own personal care requirements and Medicare certification, and a founder who merges those doors with a license that does not exist builds the wrong business record. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

The demand: about 181,000 seniors, nearly one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at South Dakota. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 935,094 as of July 1, 2025, and 19.4 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 181,000 seniors, nearly one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for South Dakota puts median household income at $75,081 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

South Dakota is a small market spread over a large map, and that shape is the business fact under the license fact. Drive time, referral distance, and the single point of contact for long term care inquiries, Dakota at Home, matter more here than raw senior counts. The founders who do well in a no license state are the ones who build the screening, training, insurance, and visit records a license would have forced, then aim them at the payer doors that actually exist.

The license question, answered directly: no state license exists for a home care agency

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a home care agency in South Dakota? No state license exists to get. The Department of Health states on its Home Health Agencies page that non-medical home health providers who provide in-home care such as bathing, light-duty housekeeping, or meal preparation are NOT regulated by the Department of Health, and that South Dakota does not require state licensure for certified home health agencies. The annual license fee schedule at SDCL 34-12-6 lists nursing facilities, assisted living centers, hospitals, chemical dependency treatment facilities, inpatient and residential hospice facilities, freestanding emergency medical care facilities, and community living homes. It lists no home health agency and no hospice agency, because neither license exists.

What exists instead is a set of doors that are easy to merge by mistake. Private pay non-medical care opens on the business record alone. Medicaid personal care is a payer door: enrollment through the Department of Social Services plus the added personal care agency requirements run by the Department of Human Services, Division of Long Term Services and Supports. Medicare home health and hospice are federal doors: certification on Form CMS-855A, with the Department of Health acting as the state survey agency. The licensed settings in the state, assisted living centers under ARSD 44:70, community living homes under ARSD 44:82, nursing facilities under ARSD 44:73, and the certified residential programs for people with developmental disabilities, are facility and program doors of their own, and a facility license authorizes no in-home agency work. South Dakota repealed certificate of need in 1988, so no need gate sits in front of any home-based track; the one bed gate in the state, the nursing facility bed moratorium at SDCL 34-12-35.3 and following, gates the nursing facility track only.

The private pay lane: form the business, build the record, open

The private pay sequence is short, and its shortness is the point. First, confirm the service menu is non-skilled: hands-on personal care, homemaker, and companion work only, with no nursing, no therapy, and no skilled tasks, and document the determination in the startup file. Second, form the business with the South Dakota Secretary of State, hold the EIN and tax registrations, and put the operating accounts and insurance in place. No state license application exists for this door; do not file a facility application with the Department of Health. Third, build the screening and training systems before the first visit, on the agency's own hiring standard: the OIG and SAM exclusion checks and the competency record. The department publishes no background check, training, or insurance standard for private pay non-medical agencies, because there is no licensure standard to publish. Fourth, open for private pay work on the business record alone: no license issues on this door and no state survey precedes opening. Agencies that build to the Medicaid personal care standard from day one keep the payer door open without a rebuild.

The Medicaid lane: personal care agency enrollment through DSS and LTSS

The Medicaid door is different, and it has two halves. An agency billing South Dakota Medicaid for personal care enrolls with Department of Social Services Provider Enrollment and completes the Department of Human Services LTSS requirements in the DSS Personal Care Agency Services manual: the LTSS review process, corrective action where required, a signed purchase of services agreement, and a Therap account for referrals through the LTSS case management system, all before the first billed visit. Personal care must be medically necessary, in the recipient service plan, and delivered in a home or community-based location, and agency based care is limited to 500 hours per recipient per plan year for recipients over age 20, with added units possible under a waiver where the assessed need supports them.

South Dakota Medicaid is fee-for-service, administered by the Department of Social Services, Division of Medical Services. The care management programs, the Primary Care Provider program, Care Connect, and BabyReady, coordinate care; they are not managed care organizations, and plan lists naming Medicaid MCOs for South Dakota are imported from other states. The HOPE waiver, Home and Community-Based Options and Person-Centered Excellence, at ARSD 67:44:03, serves people age 65 and older and people age 18 and older with a qualifying disability who meet nursing facility level of care, with personal care, homemaker, adult companion, adult day services, respite, assisted living, community living home services, and structured family caregiving among its services. Its renewal effective October 1, 2026 made no change to eligibility and no change to service definitions; a renewal is not a pause. Federal electronic visit verification requirements attach to Medicaid personal care services and home health services, so configure the visit record to the department requirements before the first billed visit. No South Dakota Medicaid enrollment moratorium or pause for home health, hospice, or personal care was located in the sources checked for this article.

The skilled and hospice lanes: federal certification is the door

A home health agency provides skilled nursing services and at least one other therapeutic service, physical therapy, speech-language pathology, occupational therapy, medical social services, or home health aide services, in the home, under a plan of treatment established by the attending physician. South Dakota adds no state license step to that door, certified or not. Medicaid payment runs through Department of Social Services enrollment and the DSS Home Health Agency Services manual. Medicare payment runs through federal certification: Form CMS-855A enrollment through PECOS to CGS Administrators, LLC, Jurisdiction 15, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for South Dakota, an Office of Civil Rights assurance of compliance, a health insurance benefit agreement, and a survey against 42 CFR Part 484, with OASIS reporting. The A/B MAC is Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction F, and the DME MAC is Noridian Jurisdiction D; neither Noridian desk enrolls a home health agency or a hospice. Credential the clinical team first, nurse licensure under SDCL chapter 36-9 and the therapy practice acts, competency files, and the plan of treatment workflow, and build the record to Part 484 from the first patient if certification is planned.

Hospice runs the same federal shape under 42 CFR Part 418. South Dakota licenses the buildings where hospice care is delivered around the clock, inpatient hospice under ARSD 44:79 and residential hospice under ARSD 44:80, and those facility tracks are separate doors. The agency itself holds no state license. On both certified tracks, read the moratorium gate before timing an initial enrollment: the federal Medicare enrollment moratorium (CMS-6101-N2 for home health, CMS-6102-N2 for hospice) gates new initial enrollments on those tracks only (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027), and the CY2026 institutional enrollment fee on the CMS-855A is $750.

Background checks: no state standard for private pay, a real standard at the payer doors

State the position exactly, because founders get this wrong in both directions. For a purely private pay agency, the Department of Health publishes no background check, training, or insurance standard, because no license scheme exists. My professional judgment, given to every owner I work with in this state, is to run caregiver background checks anyway, with the OIG and SAM exclusion checks, orientation and training records, and competency files, and to keep the documentation where a family or a referral partner can be shown it exists. At the Medicaid door the standard stops being judgment and becomes requirement: the personal care agency frame in the DSS manual is the build target, and an agency that opens on the private pay record and later rebuilds for Medicaid pays for the same systems twice. In a state with no licensure gate, the screening file, the training records, the insurance certificate, and the policies are the proof of seriousness that a license provides automatically in other states.

What you still do today: form the business the South Dakota way

Form the business with the South Dakota Secretary of State, obtain an EIN, carry the insurance the market and your contracts require, and follow state and federal employment law. Then do the ordinary employer load in the right order: written policies and procedures covering the services the agency will actually sell, caregiver training and background screening set up as a system rather than a scramble, a service agreement that states clearly what the agency does and does not do, with skilled and medical tasks on the does not do side of the line, and visit records kept per visit. Do not describe the agency as state licensed in South Dakota home care: enrollment, LTSS review, and Medicare certification are payer and survey doors, and an agency that claims a state license is describing a credential the state does not issue. No South Dakota formation fee is printed in the sources checked for this article, so I print none: confirm the current filing fee on the day of filing.

The practical warning

South Dakota's traps are phantom-door traps. The first is the license that does not exist: a filing, a fee, or a timeline offered for a South Dakota home care license is an offer for a door that does not exist, and House Bill 1138 (2026), which would have licensed non-medical home care agencies with aide training, criminal history background checks, and a license fee capped at $100, is not the exception. The Governor vetoed the bill in March 2026 and the override vote fell short, so the bill is not law and its $100 figure is not a fee; build to its standards if you choose, but file nothing under it. The second trap is the borrowed figure: the $200 annual fee at SDCL 34-12-6 prices inpatient and residential hospice facilities, never the hospice agency, whose only enrollment fee is the federal $750. The third trap is lane drift: skilled services marketed or delivered on the personal care door remain the most expensive scope error in this state, license or no license, because the service definitions bar a non-medical agency from advertising health services that need a licensed medical professional.
