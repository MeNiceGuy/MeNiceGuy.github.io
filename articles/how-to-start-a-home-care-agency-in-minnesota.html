How to Start a Home Care Agency in Minnesota (The $2,100 License and the Pause That Does Not Apply to You)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 7, 2026

The Minnesota story starts with a founder who almost walked away from a good market over a headline. She had her eye on the Twin Cities suburbs, she had caregivers ready to talk, and then she heard the news going around the owner groups: Minnesota has paused new home care licenses for 2026. She read it three times, could not tell which license the pause covered, and put her plan on a shelf. When I recorded my Minnesota walkthrough, I ended it on exactly that confusion, because it is the most expensive misunderstanding in this state right now. The pause is real. It also belongs to a different department and a different license. The home care license this article covers, the one issued by the Minnesota Department of Health under chapter 144A of the statutes, is still open, still taking applications, and still priced at the figure in the video title: $2,100 for the basic license most non-medical founders need. Here is the full path, in the order the state runs it.

The demand: about 1.11 million seniors in a high income state

Start with why owners keep looking at Minnesota. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 5,830,405 as of July 1, 2025, and 19.1 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.11 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Minnesota puts median household income at $89,062 for 2020 to 2024, well above the national figure, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

The price families face explains the revenue side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Minnesota, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $100,672, up 6 percent, ranked 6th among states. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $44 an hour. Minnesota is one of the most expensive care markets in the country, and that is before you count the license below, which keeps casual competition out.

One license, two tiers: basic is the non-medical lane

Minnesota licenses home care itself, not just the facilities around it. Under section 144A.471, subdivision 1, a home care provider may not open, operate, manage, conduct, maintain, or advertise itself as a home care provider, or provide home care services in Minnesota, without a temporary or current home care provider license issued by the commissioner of health. The provider definition reaches anyone regularly delivering at least one home care service directly in a client home for a fee. There is no private pay exemption and no friends and family scale carve out for a business doing this regularly. The Minnesota Department of Health home care licensing page says the same thing in plain language: home care services may not be offered or provided without a valid license unless the provider is exempted or excluded, and the Health Regulation Division handles licensure, registration, on-site surveys, and enforcement.

Subdivision 5 makes every applicant pick a tier: basic or comprehensive. The basic license in subdivision 6 is the non-medical lane, and its service list is specific. A basic provider can assist with dressing, self-feeding, oral hygiene, hair care, grooming, toileting, and bathing. It can provide standby assistance. It can give verbal or visual reminders to take regularly scheduled medication, including bringing the client previously set up medication, medication in original containers, or liquid or food to go with it, and reminders to perform regularly scheduled treatments and exercises. It can prepare modified diets ordered by a licensed health professional. And it can assist with laundry, housekeeping, meal preparation, shopping, or other household chores, but only if the provider is also providing at least one of the personal care activities in the first five clauses. Hold that last condition, because it decides real business models and it comes back in the warning below.

The comprehensive license in subdivision 7 covers everything basic covers, plus one or more of the skilled services: services of an advanced practice registered nurse, physician assistant, registered nurse, licensed practical nurse, physical therapist, respiratory therapist, occupational therapist, speech-language pathologist, dietitian or nutritionist, or social worker; tasks delegated to unlicensed personnel by a registered nurse or assigned by a licensed health professional; medication management services; hands-on assistance with transfers and mobility; treatment and therapies; help with eating when the client has complicating eating problems; and other complex or specialty health care services. If your plan includes nursing, therapy, or medication management, you are comprehensive from day one, not basic with extras. One boundary sits in subdivision 1a and catches founders who mix models: a home care licensee must not provide sleeping accommodations as part of home care services. Care with a bed attached is assisted living, licensed separately under chapter 144G, and a home care license does not cover it.

The fees: $2,100 basic, $4,200 comprehensive, and a renewal priced off your revenue

The entry fee is set by statute, not by a fee page that changes quietly. Under section 144A.472, subdivision 7(a), an initial applicant seeking temporary home care licensure pays $2,100 for a basic home care provider or $4,200 for a comprehensive home care provider. That is the doubling my Shorts flagged: the comprehensive fee is exactly twice the basic fee, because the skilled tier carries the heavier review. Subdivision 7(b) sets the same two amounts for a change of ownership, which matters more than it looks, because subdivision 5 says a home care license may not be transferred to another party at all. Buying an existing agency means a new license application and the full initial fee, not a transfer form.

Renewal is where Minnesota stops looking like the other states in this series. There is no flat renewal fee. Under subdivision 7(e), the schedule in force since July 1, 2020 prices renewal off the revenue the provider derived from home care services in the prior calendar year: $231 at the bottom for revenue of no more than $25,000, $462 above $25,000 up to $50,000, $957 above $100,000 up to $250,000, $2,391 above $450,000 up to $550,000, and $7,651 at the top for revenue greater than $1,500,000. Two details in the same subdivision belong in every owner budget. Subdivision 7(g) lets a provider elect to pay the highest renewal fee for its category instead of providing annual revenue information. Subdivision 7(h) sets a civil penalty of double the fee the provider should have paid if it knowingly reports incorrect revenue to pay less. And subdivision 7(j) is one line long in effect: all fees are nonrefundable. The tier decision and the revenue report are both places where guessing costs real money.

Your first license is temporary: the four clocks that run it

Minnesota does not hand a new agency a full license at the counter. Under section 144A.473, every new applicant starts on a temporary license, and four clocks govern the first year. Clock one is acknowledgment: within 14 calendar days after receiving an application, the commissioner acknowledges receipt and tells you whether the application appears complete or needs more. Clock two is the decision: within 90 days after receiving a complete application, the commissioner issues a temporary license, renews a license, or denies it. That 90 day figure is the current statute, and it is the number to plan around, because older guides and summaries still repeat a shorter 60 day window that the statute does not say.

Clock three is the temporary year itself. A temporary license is effective for up to one year from issuance, and during that year the agency operates under the full home care rules, sections 144A.43 to 144A.482. Clock four is the one new owners miss. Within five days of beginning the provision of services, the temporary licensee must notify the commissioner that it is serving clients, by mail or email to the address the commissioner provides. That notice starts the survey clock: the commissioner surveys the temporary licensee within 90 calendar days after being notified, or after otherwise having evidence the licensee is providing services. If the survey finds substantial compliance, the commissioner issues the full basic or comprehensive license. If it does not, the commissioner either terminates the temporary license or extends it for up to 90 days with conditions. Two safety valves sit in the same section: if you notify the commissioner that you have clients within 45 days before the temporary license expires, the commissioner may extend it up to 60 days to finish the survey, and if you never provide services during the temporary year at all, the license simply expires and you start over with a new application and a new fee. Renewal after the first year carries the matching rule in section 144A.472, subdivision 3: apply at least 30 days before expiration, and show that you provided home care services within the past 12 months.

The application packet: what the statute asks for, item by item

The application contents are listed in section 144A.472, subdivision 1, and a founder who builds the packet from that list files once. The application identifies the applicant, its principal place of business and county, its principal administrative office and any branch offices, and the names and contact details of all owners and managerial officials. It includes the initial license fee, the license level sought, the signature of the officer or managing agent, and the designation of one or more owners, managerial officials, or employees as agents. It documents compliance with the background study requirements of section 144A.476 for everyone involved in the management, operation, or control of the provider, and a background study under section 144.057 for any individual seeking employment, paid or volunteer. It shows evidence of workers compensation coverage, and documentation of liability coverage if the provider has it. One common question gets a clean answer from this list: no surety bond item appears anywhere in the subdivision 1 application requirements. Minnesota prices this license with fees and background studies, not with a bond.

The heart of the packet is clause (14), the policies verification. The applicant verifies it has policies and procedures in place, ready to implement and keep current, covering: reporting of maltreatment of minors and vulnerable adults; conducting and handling background studies on employees; orientation, training, and competency evaluations of home care staff, with a process for evaluating staff performance; handling complaints from clients, family members, or client representatives; initial evaluation of client needs and the provider’s ability to meet them; initial and ongoing client evaluations, including how changes in a client condition are identified, managed, and communicated; orientation to the home care client bill of rights; infection control practices; reminders for medications, treatments, or exercises, if provided; and tuberculosis screening for staff, consistent with current CDC standards. Comprehensive applicants add the subdivision 2 set on top: assessments by a registered nurse or appropriate licensed health professional, verification that nurses and licensed health professionals hold current valid licenses, medication and treatment management, delegation of home care tasks, and supervision of both licensed professionals and unlicensed personnel performing delegated tasks. The application also names the managerial official in charge of day-to-day operations, with an attestation that the person has reviewed and understands the home care regulations. That attestation is the interview before the interview: the department reviews each application to determine the applicant knowledge of and compliance with Minnesota home care regulations, and it can ask for more information or survey the applicant before granting the temporary license.

The 2026 pause: real, two years long, and aimed at a different license

Now the headline the founder in the opening almost shelved her plan over. The Minnesota Department of Human Services is running a temporary moratorium on 245D licensing for home and community-based services, effective January 1, 2026, with an anticipated duration of 24 months, ending December 31, 2027. Under it, DHS stops accepting new applications for 245D licenses, stops adding new service lines to currently licensed providers, and cancels all submitted applications currently in the queue. The only door left is an exception process, and it does not run through the applicant: exception requests are submitted to DHS by the lead agency, a county or managed care organization, or a Tribal Nation responsible for a person’s waiver case management, and being granted an exception still does not guarantee a license issues.

Read the scope line before you react to any of that. The moratorium covers the DHS 245D license for home and community-based services, the waiver services lane. The MDH home care license in this article is a different license, from a different department, under a different chapter of statutes, and the moratorium announcement does not close it. A founder whose model is 245D waiver services is genuinely blocked until 2028 outside the exception route. A founder whose model is private pay or MDH licensed home care, basic or comprehensive, is in an open lane today. Confusing the two costs a year or more of market position, and it is the single most repeated Minnesota mistake I see right now.

What you still do today: form the business the normal way

The license sits on top of an ordinary business formation, and Minnesota keeps that part cheap. Most founders in this series form a limited liability company. In Minnesota you file Articles of Organization with the Secretary of State, and the Secretary of State business filing fee schedule prices the channels differently: $155 for expedited filing online or in person, $135 if you submit by mail. Pick the channel by how fast you need the entity standing, because the licensing packet above asks for the business as it actually exists, owners, offices, and tax identification included. Beyond the entity, the ordinary employer load applies from the first hire, and two pieces of it are already inside the license application itself, workers compensation evidence and background studies, so setting them up properly at formation is licensing work, not overhead.

The practical warning

Two Minnesota traps cost new owners money, and both are quiet ones. The first is the household chores condition in the basic license. Housekeeping, laundry, meal preparation, and shopping count as basic home care services only when the provider is also providing at least one of the personal care activities in clauses (1) to (5) of subdivision 6, the dressing, bathing, toileting, standby assistance, reminder, and modified diet work. A model built on housekeeping and companionship alone does not map onto the basic license the way founders assume, and an agency that sells chores only packages under a home care license has a scope problem a surveyor can read straight off its own service list. Build at least one qualifying personal care service into every basic care plan, or rethink the model before you file. The second trap is the five day notice. Serving clients during the temporary year without notifying the commissioner leaves the survey clock unstarted, and section 144A.472, subdivision 7(i) sets the fine for failing the notification duty at $1,000. Worse, a temporary licensee that drifts through its year without serving and surveying has nothing to renew: the license expires, and the next attempt starts at a new application and a new $2,100 or $4,200 fee. The notice takes minutes. Send it the week the first client starts.

Your next step

Work in the order the state will check you. Pick the tier first, basic for non-medical personal care, comprehensive if nursing, therapy, or medication management is anywhere in the plan, because the fee doubles between them and every fee is nonrefundable. Form the entity, the Articles of Organization at $155 online or $135 by mail if you choose an LLC. Build the packet straight off subdivision 1 of section 144A.472: owners and managerial officials, background studies started early, workers compensation evidence, and the ten clause (14) policies written before you need them. File through the Department of Health, watch the 14 day acknowledgment and the 90 day decision clock, and when the temporary license issues, calendar the five day client notice and the survey that follows it, not just the one year expiration. An owner who runs that order converts a temporary license into a full one on the first survey, in a market paying about $44 an hour at the median, while the pause everyone is talking about runs on a different license in a different department.
