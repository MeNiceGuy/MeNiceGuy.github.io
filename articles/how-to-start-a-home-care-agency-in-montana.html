How to Start a Home Care Agency in Montana (No License for Non-Medical Care, a $20 License for Home Health)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Montana story starts with a founder quoted a fee for a license that does not exist. She had a personal care plan for families around Billings, and two different sellers offered her a Montana home care license package, complete with application fees. Montana issues no personal care agency license and no home care license. The facility types the state licenses are listed at MCA 50-5-101(20), and no non-medical home care category is in the list. When owners come to me with a Montana plan, the first job is to sort the state into its real shape: one license for skilled care, one license for hospice, and one enrollment door, not a license, for Medicaid non-medical care.

The licensing authority is the Licensure Bureau of the Office of Inspector General, inside the Department of Public Health and Human Services, called DPHHS. Medicaid is Montana Healthcare Programs, also administered by DPHHS, and it pays these home-based services fee-for-service. Montana home-based care runs on one license family and one enrollment door, and the two are easy to merge by mistake. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 254,000 seniors, more than one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Montana. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 1,144,694 as of July 1, 2025, and 22.2 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 254,000 seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Montana puts median household income at $72,509 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

The Montana operating fact underneath that count is distance. The state spreads those seniors across the fourth largest land area in the country, so a service area is measured in driving hours, and the Medicaid frame in this article reflects that: agency-based personal care enrollment requires coverage of at least an entire county or an Indian reservation. An owner who plans a Montana agency as if it were a compact metro market will misprice every route the agency runs.

The license question, answered directly: no Montana license exists for non-medical home care

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Montana? No Montana license exists to get. A business providing only personal care, companion, and homemaker services holds no DPHHS facility license: there is no application to file, no license to hold, and no license fee to budget for this door, so the fee line reads NO FEE PUBLISHED because there is no license to price. Personal care services are help with activities of daily living: mobility, transfers, dressing, eating, toileting, bathing, and hygiene, plus household tasks. The scope line is the service menu, not a license: the moment the menu carries registered nursing or a therapeutic service, the model is a home health agency under MCA 50-5-101(21) and needs the facility license.

The Medicaid door for non-medical care is provider enrollment, not licensure. Community First Choice Services and Personal Care Services, called CFCS/PCS, pay for personal care delivered by enrolled agencies under ARM 37.40.1001 to 37.40.1030, with provider requirements at ARM 37.40.1013. The residential models in the generator, assisted living facilities, adult foster care homes, community homes, mental health group homes, long-term care facilities, ICF/DD settings, and the other all-homes tracks, are facility licenses under their own definitions; a residential care facility license at MCA 50-5-101(45) authorizes no in-home agency work, and no in-home agency needs it. Checked negatives, printed because owners budget against them: the Montana certificate of need law at MCA 50-5-301 reaches long-term care proposals only, so no certificate of need gate sits in front of the home health, hospice, or non-medical tracks, and no Montana Medicaid enrollment moratorium or pause for home health, hospice, or CFCS/PCS was located in the 2026 provider notices checked for the packet.

The Medicaid lane: CFCS/PCS agency-based enrollment under ARM 37.40.1013

Agency-based CFCS/PCS enrollment is a full payer frame, and it governs Medicaid work only; it does not license, register, or restrict private pay non-medical care. Under ARM 37.40.1013, an agency-based provider needs a business entity formed or registered under Montana law, coverage of at least an entire county or an Indian reservation, pre-enrollment and post-enrollment onsite visits, the department mandatory CFCS/PCS training, workers compensation and unemployment insurance, and general liability coverage of $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate at ARM 37.40.1013(5), plus the motor vehicle coverage the rule prints. Services run after the functional assessment and prior authorization the Senior and Long Term Care Division contractor runs, and enrollment runs through the Provider Services Portal, with Conduent as the fiscal agent for claims. The waiver doors beside CFCS/PCS, the Big Sky Waiver through the Senior and Long Term Care Division, the SDMI Waiver, and the 0208 Comprehensive Waiver, run through their owning divisions and are built in the all-homes module.

The skilled lane: the home health agency license, and the fee really is $20

Skilled care in the home is a home health agency license issued by the Licensure Bureau under MCA 50-5-101(21) and 50-5-201, with minimum standards at ARM 37.106 subchapter 15. Home health services must include the services of a licensed registered nurse and at least one other therapeutic service. ARM 37.106.1505 sets the state minimum standard by adopting the federal Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR Part 484; licensure and Medicare certification are still separate doors, because the rule adopts the federal standard for the state license and certification runs through CMS. The sequence is the license itself: the application naming the counties served, the professional staff list with license numbers, the administrator attestation that the home health rules were reviewed, policies and procedures submitted at least 45 days before the expected opening date, an application filed at least 45 days before opening under MCA 50-5-203 and not earlier than 6 months before the desired licensure date, then a 6-month provisional license with the survey inside it. The full license runs 1 to 3 years under MCA 50-5-201 and ARM 37.106.310, and a license may not be sold, assigned, or transferred.

The fee facts for this door sit at MCA 50-5-202: the license fee is $20 for a facility with 20 beds or less, and $1 per bed for a facility with 21 beds or more. A home health agency holds no beds, so this door sits in the $20 tier. The bureau application letter asks for a completed application and fee without restating the amount; the statute figure is the figure to budget. Hospice carries the same fee frame: Montana licenses hospice under MCA 50-5-101(24) and ARM 37.106 subchapter 23 at $20 for a program with no beds, covering the hospice program and the inpatient and residential hospice facility forms the definition prints, and no Montana source read for the packet makes Medicare certification a condition of the state hospice license. The two certifications run as parallel doors: Medicare certification layers on the home health license through Form CMS-855A in PECOS to CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for Montana, with the CY2026 fee of $750, gated for initial enrollment by CMS-6101-N2, effective November 12, 2026, with no calendar end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027), and hospice carries the matching gate under CMS-6102-N2. One sequencing fact decides the Medicaid home health door: under ARM 37.40.702, Montana Medicaid home health requires the agency to be licensed, Medicare certified, and an enrolled Medicaid provider together, so a state-licensed-only agency has no Montana Medicaid home health door until certification lands.

Background checks and visit verification on the Montana doors

The personnel file on the Montana tracks carries license or credential verification for every licensed role, the criminal history record check and the protective services check the applicable standards require, before hire and on the cycle the standards set, OIG and SAM exclusion checks before hire and monthly, and tuberculosis screening under the facility communicable disease standard at ARM 37.106.313 for the licensed tracks. Build the screening file before the first visit, not after the survey finding. Electronic visit verification runs on an open vendor model with a state aggregator: DPHHS provides the Netsmart Mobile Caregiver+ system at no cost and uses it as the state aggregator, and an agency may use an alternate EVV system that is certified by DPHHS and transmits visit data through Mobile Caregiver+. Full EVV compliance has been required for dates of service on and after July 1, 2024, and EVV is required in Montana including for live-in caregivers, under DPHHS Policy 612. EVV exceptions left unworked in Mobile Caregiver+ are a named deficiency in the packet.

What you still do today: form the business the Montana way

On the unlicensed door, form the business with the Montana Secretary of State, hold the EIN and the tax registrations, and put the operating accounts and insurance in place; formation is a business record, not a filing with the Licensure Bureau, and private pay work can operate on that record with no state license to wait for. Build the screening and training systems before the first visit anyway, the criminal history and protective services checks the agency policy sets, the exclusion checks, and the attendant training file for every direct care worker, because the CFCS/PCS enrollment frame and every referral source will read that file. For Medicaid work, enroll through the Provider Services Portal as a CFCS/PCS agency-based provider, provider type 12 personal assistance, complete the onsite visits and the mandatory training, carry the $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate general liability coverage ARM 37.40.1013(5) requires, and configure EVV through Mobile Caregiver+ or a certified alternate before the first billed visit. Montana Healthcare Programs is fee-for-service for these services, so no managed care contracting step exists; plan lists naming managed care plans for Montana home care are imported from other states.

The practical warning

Three Montana traps cost new owners money. The first is paying for the license that does not exist, or its mirror image, treating the missing license as a missing standard: in a state with no licensure gate for non-medical care, the screening file, the training records, the insurance certificate, and the policies are the proof of seriousness a license provides automatically in other states. The second trap is the fee formula misread: MCA 50-5-202 prices a license by beds, $20 at 20 beds or less and $1 per bed at 21 or more, and founders who import another state fee schedule, or the residential treatment center schedule at ARM 37.106.3204, budget a number Montana never asked for. The third trap is sequencing the Medicaid home health door ahead of its gate: billing Montana Medicaid home health as a licensed-only agency, when ARM 37.40.702 requires licensure, Medicare certification, and enrollment together, turns delivered care into unpaid care. The three keys open that door in one order, and the order is the work.
