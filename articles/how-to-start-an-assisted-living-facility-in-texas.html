How to Start an Assisted Living Facility in Texas: Type A vs Type B, the Per-Bed Fee, and the Full Process

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 26, 2026

The call usually starts with a house. An owner has found a property, sometimes a large home in a residential neighborhood, sometimes a small building that used to be something else, and the plan sounds simple: bring in a handful of seniors, feed them, help them with daily care, and charge a monthly rate. Then the questions start. Do I need a license for six people? Is this a group home, a personal care home, or an assisted living facility? What does the state charge? In Texas, all of those questions run through one license, the assisted living facility license under Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 247 and the licensing standards at 26 TAC Chapter 553. Texas has no separate license for residential care homes or board and care homes; if the work fits the definition, this is the license. This article walks the whole process the way I walk it with owners: what triggers the license, the Type A versus Type B decision that shapes everything after it, the small versus large line at 16 beds, the per-bed fee math, the application sequence in TULIP, the manager who has to qualify, and the survey sequence that ends with your first three residents.

The trigger: four unrelated people, food and shelter, and care

The license attaches at a specific threshold, and the HHSC page for new ALF providers states it plainly. An assisted living facility is an establishment that furnishes food and shelter to four or more people who are unrelated to the proprietor, and provides personal care services, or administration of medication by a person licensed or otherwise authorized to administer it, or assistance with or supervision of medication administration. Read the parts together. Four or more unrelated people is the count. Food and shelter is the base. Personal care or medication help is the service that completes the definition. Below four unrelated residents, this state license is not triggered, though your city may still have its own rules for the property. At four and above with care in the picture, you are an assisted living facility in the eyes of the state whether you call yourself one or not, and operating without the license is the most expensive mistake in this whole article. Texas is also a large market for this work: the U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts for Texas put the state at 31,709,821 people on July 1, 2025, with 14.9 percent age 65 and over, about 4.72 million seniors. The demand is real. So is the licensing gate in front of it.

Type A or Type B: decided by who can get out in a fire

The first real decision is not the building. It is the evacuation test in 26 TAC 553.5, and it classifies your license by what your residents can do in an emergency. In a Type A facility, residents must be mentally and physically capable of evacuating the facility unassisted in an emergency, must not require routine attendance during sleeping hours, must be capable of following directions under emergency conditions, and must be able to demonstrate that they can meet the evacuation requirements. A resident who uses a wheelchair can still fit Type A if that resident can transfer and evacuate without physical help from staff. In a Type B facility, a resident may require staff assistance to evacuate, may be incapable of following directions under emergency conditions, may require attendance during sleeping hours, and may need help transferring to and from a wheelchair or bed, but must not be permanently bedfast. That last line is a hard ceiling: a person who is permanently bedfast is above what any assisted living license covers.

Here is why this decision deserves more thought than owners usually give it. Type A is the simpler and cheaper building to run, but it holds only residents who stay independent enough to get themselves out. People age in place. The resident who walks out on her own in year one may need hands-on help in year three, and at that point she no longer fits your license. Your options then are narrow: HHSC can be asked for an evacuation waiver so a specific resident can stay, or the resident has to move. Type B costs more to build and staff, and it is the license that lets you keep residents as their needs grow, including residents who cannot follow emergency directions, which is where most memory care sits. Facilities that serve residents with Alzheimer disease or related disorders can also add the separate Alzheimer certification, which HHSC prices at $300 for a three year certification and which is available to Type B facilities. Decide the type from the residents you actually intend to serve over five years, not from the cheapest building in front of you today.

Small or large: the line at 16 beds

Crossing the type decision is a size decision. Under 26 TAC 553.3, a small facility is licensed for 16 or fewer residents and a large facility is licensed for 17 or more. The same Type A or Type B license covers a six bed house and a hundred unit building, but the rules change at that line in the places that cost money. The clearest example is the manager, covered below: a small facility manager needs a high school diploma or equivalency, while a large facility manager needs a degree or a diploma plus management experience. Night staffing also splits here. Under 26 TAC 553.253, night staff in a small Type A facility must be immediately available; in a large Type A facility, immediately available and awake; and in a Type B facility of any size, immediately available and awake. One more small facility rule owners like: the manager of small Type A facilities may manage up to four facilities, as long as the total is no more than 16 residents. And one legacy category to close out: Type C is a four bed category that exists for renewals only. HHSC takes no new Type C applications, so do not build a plan around it.

The fee: $300 plus $15 a bed, capped at $2,250

The license fee is one of the smallest numbers in this project, and HHSC publishes it on the provider page. For an initial, renewal, change of ownership, or relocation application for a Type A or Type B license, the fee is $300 plus $15 per bed, with a maximum of $2,250 for a three year license. Work it for the sizes owners actually open. A 16 bed small facility pays $300 plus $240, which is $540. A 40 bed facility pays $300 plus $600, which is $900. The cap takes over at 130 beds, where $300 plus $1,950 reaches the $2,250 maximum. The license runs three years. Two related fees belong in the same budget note: increasing licensed capacity costs $15 per added bed, and a renewal application submitted less than 45 days before the license expiration date draws a late fee of one half the basic fee. Set against a building purchase or a renovation, these are rounding numbers. The real money in a Texas ALF sits in the Life Safety Code work the building needs, the staffing pattern you commit to, and the months you carry the property before the license issues, which is why the sequence below matters more than the fee.

The application sequence, in order

HHSC lists the steps to become licensed, and the order is not flexible. First, complete the pre-survey computer-based training and keep the certificate of completion; it goes in with the application. Second, file the license application on Form 3720 through TULIP, the online licensure system, with all required documents, the ownership documents where they apply, and the fee. Completed applications for a new building should be submitted about 45 days before the building is expected to meet the Life Safety Code and architectural requirements. Third, the clocks start. HHSC has up to 30 days to review the application. If it arrives incomplete, HHSC notifies you in writing of the deficient items, and you have 30 days from that notice to respond with complete and correct information; an application that is not completed in time is denied. Fourth, submit a current, approved fire marshal inspection, and give HHSC written notice that the building complies with the construction standards in Subchapter D of the licensing standards and is ready for a Life Safety Code survey. Fifth, pass the Life Safety Code survey. Only then may residents move in, and only one to three of them: you admit at least one and no more than three residents, notify HHSC in writing that you have done so and are ready, and pass the health survey with those first residents in the building. Sixth, on a complete application with passing Life Safety Code and health survey recommendations, the Licensing and Credentialing Unit issues the license within 45 days.

Two shortcuts sit inside that sequence, and both are narrow. An applicant for an initial license can request an expedited Life Safety Code inspection once HHSC has the complete application, all fees, and all required documents; HHSC conducts it within 15 business days after the request and the inspection fee, and each expedited inspection carries its own fee, including a repeat if the first one fails. Separately, newly constructed facilities can qualify for a six month provisional license without a Life Safety Code survey, but only when the plans went to HHSC for early compliance review before construction, all local approvals including a certificate of occupancy are in hand, the complete Form 3720 is filed within 30 days of those approvals, the fee is paid, the health inspection passes, and the applicant or its controlling owner has already built another Texas facility that complies with the Life Safety Code standards. That last condition means the provisional route is a tool for experienced builders, not a first project shortcut.

The manager has to qualify before the doors open

Every facility must designate a manager in writing, with authority over the operation, and 26 TAC 553.253 sets the bar by size. In a small facility, the manager must show proof of graduation from an accredited high school or a certification of equivalency. In a large facility, the manager must hold an associate degree in nursing, health care management, or a related field, or a bachelor degree, or a high school diploma or equivalency plus at least one year of experience in management or health care industry management. Texas does not issue the manager a personal license; the facility has to be able to prove the training instead. The manager must complete at least one educational course on the management of assisted living facilities of at least 24 hours, covering the licensing standards, resident characteristics including dementia, resident assessment, management principles, food and nutrition services, federal law including the Americans with Disabilities Act, community resources, ethics, and financial management. Eight of those hours, on the assisted living standards, must be finished within the first three months of employment, the full course must be complete by the first anniversary, in-house in-services do not count toward it, and the evidence, with content, hours, dates, and provider, must be on file at the facility. After the first year, every manager shows 12 hours of continuing education annually. Managers are on duty 40 hours a week managing only one facility, with the small Type A exception noted above, and a person must be designated in writing to act in the manager absence.

Staffing under the manager follows the same section. Full-time attendants must be at least 18 years old or a high school graduate. All staff complete four hours of orientation before assuming any job responsibilities, and staff training and competence must be documented before responsibilities are assumed. An attendant must be in the facility at all times when residents are in the facility. Your written staffing policies have to carry staffing ratios, and your normal 24 hour staffing pattern must be disclosed to prospective residents before admission and posted monthly. What I tell owners here is simple: the staffing pattern you disclose is a promise the surveyor can test, so write the pattern you can actually staff on your worst week, not your best one.

The warning, and the next step

The practical warning is about order. The two mistakes that cost Texas ALF owners the most are admitting residents before the Life Safety Code survey is passed, which the sequence does not allow at any census, and buying or renovating a building before deciding the license type, because Type B life safety requirements are written for residents who cannot get themselves out, and retrofitting a building that was specced for Type A is where budgets go to die. A quieter version of the same mistake is admitting a resident whose evacuation ability does not match your license and hoping nobody tests it. Surveyors test exactly that.

Your next step costs nothing and decides the shape of the whole project: before you sign for a property, write down the resident you intend to serve in year five, run that resident through the Type A and Type B tests in 26 TAC 553.5, and let the answer pick your license type and your size. Then complete the pre-survey training and file on Form 3720 in TULIP with a building that matches the license you chose.
