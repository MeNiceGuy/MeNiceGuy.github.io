How Much Does It Cost to Start a Home Care Agency? (The Six Piles and the Payroll Float Nobody Budgets For)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 11, 2026

Every month, someone in a home care owner group asks the same question in the same words: how much money does it take to start a home care agency? The answers come back anywhere from $5,000 to $100,000, and every one of those answers can be honest, because each owner counted a different set of bills. One counted the license fee and stopped. Another counted everything through the first three payrolls. When I recorded my startup costs walkthrough, I gave the short answer the way I give it to owners who ask me directly: for a lean, home based launch, plan on $15,000 to $40,000, and if you want the one number to build around, build around $25,000. That range is my professional planning estimate from the startup budgets I build with owners, not a published statistic, and this article shows you what sits inside it, pile by pile, in the order the bills actually arrive, so you can rebuild the total with your own state fees and your own quotes. The mistake that ends agencies is not picking the wrong total. It is confusing starting costs with survival costs, and this walkthrough separates the two.

Pile one: the legal shell, the cheapest pile you will ever pay

Forming the business is the pile founders fear and the pile that costs the least. You form an entity, usually an LLC, and state filing fees vary widely by state. As one verified point on that range, Pennsylvania charges $125 for a Certificate of Organization on the Pennsylvania Department of State fees and payments page. Your employer identification number costs nothing: the IRS page for getting an EIN says to use its tool to get an EIN directly from the IRS in minutes for free, and warns in plain words, beware of websites that charge for an EIN, you never have to pay a fee for an EIN. Add a business bank account, a registered agent fee if you use one, and a local business license where your city requires one. For most founders this whole pile lands in the low hundreds of dollars. Enjoy it. It is the only pile that behaves that way.

Pile two: the license, where the range explodes by state

Here is where one state answer stops sounding like another state answer. At the bottom of the national range, Pennsylvania charges $100 per separately licensed location for a home care agency or registry application under 28 Pa. Code Chapter 611. At the top, California charges $5,603, nonrefundable, for a 24 month home care organization license under Health and Safety Code section 1796.49. Some states do not license private pay non-medical care at all, which makes the state license fee zero and moves the real cost into insurance and compliance instead. There is no national license price, and anyone who quotes you one number for the country is guessing. What I tell owners is the pattern underneath the fees: the fee itself is rarely the expensive part. The expensive part is the weeks or months of preparation the application demands, and the rent, insurance, and payroll clocks that start running before the license arrives.

Pile three: people, screened before your first client signs

Every caregiver you hire carries a screening cost, and you pay it before that caregiver bills a single hour. Pennsylvania publishes its state police criminal history check at $22 on the Pennsylvania State Police criminal history page, and the Commonwealth child abuse history clearance at $13, with a federal fingerprint based check added for workers who have not lived in the state for the two prior years under Chapter 611. Your state has its own version of that stack. Multiply it by every hire, then multiply again by reality: you will screen more people than you keep. Add health screenings, drug testing where you require it, and the orientation and training hours you pay for before a caregiver is client ready. Then anchor the wage itself to published data, not to what a competitor claims to pay. The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the national median wage for home health and personal care aides at $35,800 a year, $17.21 an hour, in May 2025, on its Occupational Outlook Handbook page for home health and personal care aides. That May 2025 figure updates the May 2024 median my earlier cost work used. If you plan to keep caregivers, plan to pay at or above that line, and budget the screening pile as a recurring cost of staying staffed, not a one time cost of opening.

Pile four: insurance, the first pile with real weight

A working agency needs general liability and professional liability coverage, workers compensation on every caregiver hour, and fidelity bonding, with hired and non-owned auto coverage if your caregivers drive clients. Premiums are quoted against your state, your services, and your projected payroll, so no honest national figure exists and I will not invent one. As a planning estimate from my consulting work, labeled as professional judgment and not published data: a small startup should expect its first year insurance outlay to land in the low four figures at minimum, with workers compensation then riding on every payroll dollar you run afterward. Get real quotes before you finalize your budget, because in a high cost state this pile alone reshapes the plan, and a budget built on a guessed premium is a budget you will rebuild in month two.

Pile five: systems and the manual, priced as monthly burn

Scheduling and billing software, an electronic visit verification system if you touch Medicaid, a business phone line, a basic website, and the forms your operation runs on. Software in this industry is sold by subscription, per month or per active client, so read this pile as monthly burn, not as a purchase. Your policies and procedures manual belongs in this pile too. Writing it yourself costs weeks of owner time. Buying a state specific manual or having one prepared costs money instead, in ranges that vary too much by vendor to quote honestly here. Either way it is a real cost, in hours or in dollars, and skipping it is not a savings. In the records I review, a missing manual shows up later as a survey deficiency, and deficiencies cost more than documents.

Pile six: the payroll float, the pile that actually closes agencies

Caregivers are paid weekly or biweekly. Clients and payers pay later. Private pay invoices settle in days to weeks, Medicaid in weeks to longer, and your payroll does not care about any of that. So run this worked example with me, with every assumption labeled. Say you open with three caregivers working a combined 60 billable hours a week. Say you pay $18 an hour, above the $17.21 national median aide wage the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported for May 2025, because you want to keep them. Load that wage with an 18 percent payroll burden for employer taxes and workers compensation, a planning figure I use in my own cost work and label as professional judgment, and your loaded cost is about $21.24 an hour. Sixty hours a week comes to about $1,274 a week in direct labor, about $5,522 a month. Three months of runway on direct labor alone is about $16,600, before rent, software, insurance installments, or a single dollar of your own pay. That number, not the license fee, is the real price of admission, and it scales with every hour you sell. It is also why the revenue side has to be checked against the market before you open: the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey puts the national median rate families pay for in-home care at $35 an hour, $80,080 a year on its 44 hours a week basis. Your float has to bridge the gap between that revenue arriving and your payroll leaving, every single cycle.

The two piles that ambush month two

Two smaller piles ride along with the big six. The first is market entry: a website that looks like a real business, printed materials for referral sources, the one page service sheet you leave with discharge planners and elder law offices, and the mileage and hours of doing those visits yourself. None of it is expensive per item. All of it is real, and referral development done at zero budget usually means referral development not done. The second is the cost of staying legal after you open: annual license renewals, entity renewals and registered agent fees, clearance renewals on their cycle, software subscriptions compounding quietly, and the bookkeeper or payroll service most owners swear they will not need until the first payroll tax notice arrives. Budget both piles as monthly burn from day one and revisit the figure every quarter, because a startup budget that only describes opening day describes a business that plans to be surprised monthly.

The honest total, and how to use it

Stack the six piles for a lean, home based startup in an inexpensive licensing state and the one time costs can stay in the low four figures, with the payroll runway carrying the real plan into the range my video gives, $15,000 to $40,000, with $25,000 as the single planning number I tell owners to build around. Open an office, start in an expensive licensing state, or staff aggressively, and the same stack walks toward the $100,000 answers in those group threads, which is exactly the territory franchise concepts price at. The total is not a trivia answer. It is an output of your state, your staffing plan, and how many months of payroll you can float. The discipline I enforce with every owner I advise is simple: one time costs and monthly burn get different questions. For one time costs, ask what the total is to open the doors legally and safely. For monthly burn, ask how many months the bank account can feed this business at low revenue, including your own survival. Add the two answers and you have your real capitalization number. Compare it to the money you actually have. If there is a gap, the gap is your plan: a smaller launch, a slower staffing ramp, or a later start date. What it is never allowed to be is a hope that clients arrive faster than payroll leaves.

The practical warning

The warning from the video bears repeating because it is the one I watch play out in the budgets owners send me. The agencies that run out of money rarely run out because the license cost more than expected. They run out because they budgeted to open and never budgeted to operate: the first payroll lands on schedule, the first client payment does not, and an agency that looked funded on opening day is borrowing against next month by week six. Validate before you spend. Build the stack, fund the float, and only then sign the lease, print the cards, and set the start date.
