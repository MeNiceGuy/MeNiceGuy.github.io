QAPI for Home Health Agencies: The Five Standards CMS Actually Requires

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 25, 2026

The scene repeats in the records I review. Survey is close, or the surveyor is already in the building, and someone asks for the QAPI program. What comes out is one binder: a policy, a committee roster with names of people who left last year, and a stack of blank forms. Then the surveyor asks the question that decides everything: show me the minutes, the data, and the improvement projects. Silence. Quality Assessment and Performance Improvement, QAPI, is not a paperwork exercise you assemble the week before survey. It is a Condition of Participation for every Medicare certified home health agency, and the surveyor is not grading the binder. The surveyor is checking whether a working program exists, with evidence it actually operates. This article walks what the federal rule really requires, standard by standard, where owners usually misread it, and the order I tell owners to build in so the evidence is real when it is asked for.

The rule itself, in one paragraph

Under 42 CFR 484.65, a home health agency must develop, implement, evaluate, and maintain an effective, ongoing, agency-wide, data-driven QAPI program. The governing body must ensure the program reflects the complexity of the organization and its services, involves all services including those provided under contract or arrangement, focuses on indicators related to improved outcomes, including the use of emergent care services and hospital admissions and readmissions, and takes actions that address performance across the spectrum of care, including the prevention and reduction of medical errors. The last sentence is the one that sinks binder programs: the agency must maintain documentary evidence of its QAPI program and be able to demonstrate its operation to CMS. A policy describes a program. Minutes, data reports, and project records demonstrate one.

One scope point before the standards. This is a Medicare home health rule. It binds Medicare certified home health agencies. A private pay, non-medical home care agency that is not Medicare certified does not carry this federal Condition, though its state license may impose its own quality or record duties, and borrowing the QAPI discipline voluntarily is still good practice. Do not let anyone sell you a federal requirement that does not attach to your certification, and do not skip it if it does.

Standard one: program scope

Section 484.65(a) sets the scope. The program must at least be capable of showing measurable improvement in indicators for which there is evidence that improvement in those indicators will improve health outcomes, patient safety, and quality of care. And the agency must measure, analyze, and track quality indicators, including adverse patient events, and other aspects of performance that let it assess its processes of care, its services, and its operations. Read that plainly: picking measures is not optional, tracking them is not optional, and adverse events belong inside the same system as the outcome measures, not in a separate drawer nobody opens.

Standard two: program data

Section 484.65(b) covers the data. The program must use quality indicator data, including measures derived from OASIS where applicable, and other relevant data, in the design of the program. The data collected must be used for two named jobs: monitor the effectiveness and safety of services and quality of care, and identify opportunities for improvement. And the frequency and detail of data collection must be approved by the governing body. That last clause matters for a common misreading. The regulation does not print a meeting calendar. It does not say quarterly in the text. What it requires is that the governing body approve how often and how deeply data is collected, which means the cadence has to be a documented governing body decision, not a habit. In the agencies I work with, a monthly data review by the person who owns the numbers and a quarterly committee meeting is a workable rhythm, and I recommend it as practice. It is a recommendation, not a line in the rule, and your governing body still has to adopt and approve its own.

Standard three: program activities

Section 484.65(c) is where QAPI stops being measurement and becomes action. Performance improvement activities must focus on high risk, high volume, or problem-prone areas, consider the incidence, prevalence, and severity of problems in those areas, and lead to an immediate correction of any identified problem that directly or potentially threatens the health and safety of patients. Activities must track adverse patient events, analyze their causes, and implement preventive actions. And after the agency takes an action aimed at improvement, it must measure its success and track performance to ensure the improvement is sustained. That sustain clause is the difference between a fix and a project. Retraining one clinician and moving on is not systemic action. Finding why the error happened, redesigning the process so it is harder to repeat, and then watching the measure until the improvement holds is.

Standard four: performance improvement projects

Section 484.65(d) requires the agency to conduct performance improvement projects, and it has since July 13, 2018. Here is the second common misreading, in both directions. The rule does not set a fixed number of projects per year. The number and scope of distinct improvement projects conducted annually must reflect the scope, complexity, and past performance of the agency services and operations. A small agency and a large multi-branch agency do not owe the same project load, and anyone who quotes you an exact required count is adding to the rule. What is not flexible is the documentation: the agency must document the projects undertaken, the reasons for conducting them, and the measurable progress achieved. A strong project is specific and measurable, for example reducing missed visits from four percent to under two percent in six months. Improve quality is not a project, it is a wish. Pick project topics from your own data: a measure that missed its target, a complaint category that keeps repeating, an adverse event pattern that will not go away.

Standard five: executive responsibilities

Section 484.65(e) puts the program on the governing body, in four duties. The governing body is responsible for ensuring that an ongoing program for quality improvement and patient safety is defined, implemented, and maintained; that agency-wide quality assessment and performance improvement efforts address priorities for improved quality of care and patient safety, and that all improvement actions are evaluated for effectiveness; that clear expectations for patient safety are established, implemented, and maintained; and that any findings of fraud or waste are appropriately addressed. That fourth duty surprises owners. Fraud and waste findings are not a separate compliance island; the rule routes them into QAPI oversight. In practice, I tell owners to build a small committee that can carry this: the administrator, the clinical leadership, a field clinician, someone who sees the scheduling and referral data, and one named person who owns data collection outright. Five to seven people is a workable size as practice. The regulation assigns the responsibility to the governing body; the committee is how you organize the work underneath it.

The data system underneath all five standards

Here is where most programs die in the records I review: nobody owns the data, so nothing gets tracked continuously. A working setup keeps three running logs. An incident log covering falls, medication errors, missed visits, and equipment issues, with severity, cause, and corrective action. A complaint and grievance log covering every complaint, including verbal ones, with the investigation, the resolution, and how fast it was resolved. And an infection log with type, onset, outcome, and whether the committee reviewed it. On top of the logs, choose a set of quality measures and set a target for each one. I tell agencies to start with the measures CMS already tracks, such as acute care hospitalization, emergency department use, and improvement in ambulation, because those are the measures that also shape how your agency looks publicly. The eight to twelve measure range in the video is my recommended starting size for a dashboard, not a number in the regulation. What the regulation requires is that you measure, analyze, and track indicators, use the data to monitor safety and find improvement opportunities, and keep the evidence.

The practical warning

The warning is simple, and it is the whole article in one line. You cannot manufacture a year of QAPI evidence in the week before survey, because the evidence is dated. Minutes from meetings that never happened, backdated logs, and a project with no baseline and no follow-up measurement do not survive a surveyor who interviews your staff and compares their answers to the paper. If your program is a binder today, the honest move is to start it operating now, with real meetings and real data from this month forward, rather than printing a history that never occurred.

A build order that produces real evidence

For an agency standing the program up, or rebuilding one that exists only on paper, the sequence I give owners runs like this. First, designate in writing who owns the program and the data, appoint the committee, and hold a real kickoff meeting with real minutes. Second, pick your measures, pull your baselines, set targets, and get the governing body approval of the data collection frequency and detail on the record. Third, charter your first performance improvement project from the data, with a root cause analysis before any fix, an action plan where every action has one owner and one due date, and monthly tracking of the measure. Fourth, run the rhythm: review data monthly, meet as a committee on the cadence your governing body approved, and evaluate the whole program in writing at least annually as good practice. Your next step this week

Here is the one actionable next step. Ask your agency the surveyor question out loud: show me the minutes, the data, and the improvement projects. Lay out what actually exists, with dates, against the five standards above: scope, data, activities, projects, and executive responsibilities. Whichever standard has no dated evidence behind it is where your build starts this week, and it starts with a real meeting and a real measure, not a new binder.
