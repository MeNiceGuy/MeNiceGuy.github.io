How to Start a Home Care Agency in Virginia (The $1,500 License and the Three Month Reserve)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 4, 2026

The Virginia story usually starts with two numbers that are both wrong. A new owner in Richmond or Norfolk reads an older checklist, sees a $500 license fee, and budgets for that. Then a forum post or a consultant tells the same owner that Virginia requires $20,000 in reserve funds, stated like it is a line in the rulebook. Neither number survives a read of the current regulation. The license fee in 12VAC5-381-70 is $1,500 for each initial and renewal application, non-refundable, since the amendment that took effect May 22, 2025. And the reserve rule in 12VAC5-381-190 sets no fixed dollar amount at all. It asks for a budget and proof of funds covering your first three months, sized by your own numbers. An owner who plans off the old pages underbudgets the fee and then chases a reserve figure that does not exist. This article walks the real path in the order the state checks it.

The demand: about 1.63 million seniors, and households that can pay

Virginia is my home state, and the demand case here is not a guess. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 8,880,107 as of July 1, 2025, and 18.4 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.63 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Virginia puts median household income at $93,170 for 2020 to 2024. That pairing matters. A large senior population spread across Northern Virginia, Richmond, Hampton Roads, and the valley, in a state where household incomes in the major metros can carry private pay care.

The price families face explains the revenue side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Virginia, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $80,080, up 6 percent, ranked 26th among states. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $35 an hour. That is a market benchmark, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows that one full time client in Virginia represents a substantial annual contract, which is why the license work below is worth doing carefully instead of fast.

The license: a home care organization, and the scope line that decides whether you need it

Virginia licenses this business through the Virginia Department of Health, Office of Licensure and Certification, usually shortened to the OLC. The license is the home care organization license, or HCO license, under the Regulations for the Licensure of Home Care Organizations at 12VAC5-381. There is no separate non-medical license. Personal care services sit inside the HCO definition, and personal care in that chapter means nonskilled help with the activities of daily living: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, bowel and bladder control, and eating, plus the instrumental activities like meal preparation, housekeeping, shopping, and laundry.

The exemption is narrow, and it is the first decision a Virginia founder has to get right. Under 12VAC5-381-30, the chapter does not apply to providers of only homemaker, chore, or companion services. The definitions draw the line in detail. Homemaker services cover help with instrumental activities and a short list of light assists, and the same definition states what homemaker services do not include: feeding, bed baths, transferring, lifting, putting on braces or other supports, cutting nails, or shaving with a blade. Companion services cover transportation, meal preparation, shopping, light housekeeping, companionship, and household management. The moment your aides provide hands-on personal care, you are outside the exemption and inside licensure. When in doubt, assume the license is required, and note that a licensed organization asking for an exemption files the request with the $75 one time processing fee in 12VAC5-381-70(D). Exempt organizations also stay subject to complaint investigations.

Step one: form the business

The entity comes first, because the license application is filed by a person or entity that already exists. Most Virginia founders form a limited liability company with the State Corporation Commission. On the SCC page for Virginia limited liability companies, forming a new Virginia LLC on Form LLC1011 costs $100, filed online or by form, and Virginia LLCs then pay a $50 annual registration fee rather than filing an annual report. After the entity exists, get the employer identification number directly from the IRS. It is free, and no middleman is needed. Form the entity, get the number, open the business bank account, and only then build the license package, because the financial proof below has to sit in the name and the accounts of the business that is applying.

The money: $1,500 for the license, and a three month reserve sized by your own budget

The fee rule is short. Under 12VAC5-381-70(A), the OLC collects $1,500 for each initial and renewal license application, the fee accompanies the application, and it is not refundable. Licenses in Virginia are renewed triennially under 12VAC5-381-40(E), so that $1,500 is a three year fee, not an annual one. The smaller fees in the same section are worth knowing before they surprise you: a $50 late fee for a renewal application filed late, a $250 processing fee for each reissuance or replacement of a license, for example after a change of operator, name, or address under 12VAC5-381-60, and the $75 exemption processing fee above. Older VDH checklists and secondary guides still circulating with a $500 figure predate the May 22, 2025 amendment. Budget $1,500.

The reserve is where Virginia is different, and where the $20,000 myth does its damage. Under 12VAC5-381-190(A), every initial applicant includes two things. First, a detailed operating budget showing projected operating expenses for the three month period after the license issues. Second, proof of initial reserve operating funds in an amount sufficient to ensure operation for that same three month period. The proof can be cash, cash equivalents, borrowed funds that are immediately available, or a line of credit that is immediately available, documented with a current balance sheet, a letter from the officer of the bank or financial institution holding the funds, or a letter of credit from a lender. Read that section closely and you will find no fixed dollar minimum anywhere in it. Your reserve number is the output of your budget: wages, payroll taxes, rent, insurance, and supplies for three months, built from real line items. An owner who copies a $20,000 figure from a forum either over-saves and delays the launch, or under-saves for a larger service area and fails the sufficiency test the rule actually applies. Build the budget first. The reserve follows from it.

Insurance and the bond: $1 million of liability, and $50,000 against employee theft

Indemnity coverage is set in 12VAC5-381-210, and the application is incomplete without it. The organization must purchase and maintain malpractice insurance consistent with section 8.01-581.15 of the Code of Virginia, general liability insurance covering personal property damage, bodily injury, product liability, and libel and slander of at least $1 million per occurrence, and third-party crime insurance or a blanket fidelity bond of $50,000 minimum. That last item is the bond in the video title, and it is worth stating plainly: Virginia does not set a general surety bond for this license. The $50,000 figure is crime coverage, a bond or insurance that protects the organization against losses from employee theft or fraud. Buy the coverage the section names, get the certificates, and keep them current, because the rule says the coverage is maintained at all times.

The core pieces: an administrator the rule qualifies, a policy manual, and worker files that hold up

Start with the administrator, because this is the requirement that stops the most Virginia founders. Under 12VAC5-381-170(A), the governing body appoints an administrator who has evidence of at least one year of training and experience in direct health care service delivery, with at least one year within the last five years of supervisory or administrative management experience in home health care or a related health program. The administrator must be an employee of the organization, runs the day to day management, and the person who covers the role during an absence has to be able to perform those same duties. A founder with no direct health care delivery experience cannot simply name himself and move on. He hires or partners into that qualification before filing, because the application and the inspection both test it.

The policy manual is the second long lead item. Under 12VAC5-381-180, the organization implements written policies and procedures approved by the governing body, reviewed at least annually, and the list is specific: administrative records, admission and discharge criteria, informed consent, advance directives, client rights, contract services, medication management if applicable, quality improvement, mandated reporting of abuse, neglect, and exploitation, communicable and reportable diseases, client records and confidentiality, record retention, supervision and delivery of services, emergency and on-call services, infection control, handling consumer complaints, telemonitoring, and approved variances, plus the financial and personnel policy sets in the same section. The staffing side sits in 12VAC5-381-200: a staffing plan that matches the services offered, a written job description for each position with title, duties, supervisor, and minimum qualifications, and an orientation program covering the objectives of the organization, confidentiality, client rights, mandated reporting, personnel policies, emergency preparedness, infection control, cultural awareness, and the laws and rules that apply to the position. Policies, job descriptions, and orientation records are not decoration for the application. They are the documents the survey measures the running agency against.

Background checks carry their own clock and their own hard stop. Under 12VAC5-381-110 and section 32.1-162.9:1 of the Code of Virginia, the organization obtains a criminal record report from the Virginia Department of State Police for each compensated employee, plus a sworn disclosure statement about criminal history. The report must be obtained within 30 days of employment, a report dated more than 90 days before the employment date is not accepted, only the original report counts, and the organization must ensure no employee has been convicted of a barrier crime listed in that statute. No employee works in a position involving direct contact with a patient until the original criminal record report has been received, unless that person works under the direct supervision of an employee whose check is complete. The reports and sworn statements are confidential and kept in locked files accessible only to the administrator or a designee. Build that sequence into hiring from the first aide. A caregiver who starts hands-on work while the report is still in transit is a violation on day one, not a paperwork delay.

The clock: 60 days before opening, a consultation after the file is complete, and a six month trap

Virginia publishes the filing clock instead of a processing promise, and the distinction matters. Under 12VAC5-381-40(D), a completed application for initial licensure must be submitted at least 60 days before the planned opening date. The OLC considers an application complete when all requested information and the fee are submitted, and if it finds the application incomplete, the applicant is notified. The OLC also offers prelicensure consultation and technical assistance to explain the regulation and the survey process, but under 12VAC5-381-40(A) that consultation is arranged only after a completed initial application is on file. Treat the consultation as a rehearsal that happens inside the process, not as a pre-application meeting you can book to get ready.

Two clocks then run against the applicant and the licensee. First, the trap: an incomplete application becomes inactive six months after the OLC receives it, and the applicant must then reapply with a completed application and a new application fee. A half-built package filed early to hold a place does the opposite of holding a place. It starts a six month countdown to paying $1,500 again. Second, inspection. Under 12VAC5-381-80, OLC representatives make periodic unannounced on-site inspections as necessary, and not less often than biennially, and the applicant's activities and services are subject to inspection to determine compliance. After an inspection, the administrator submits an acceptable plan of correction within 15 working days of receiving the report, covering the corrective actions, who implements them, the expected correction date, and the measures taken to prevent a recurrence, and completion of corrective actions may not exceed 45 working days from the last day of the inspection. The 15 and 45 working day pair is the rhythm of Virginia licensure after the surveyor leaves, for the initial survey and for every survey after it.

After the license: a three year cycle with its own calendar

Licensure in Virginia runs in threes, and putting the dates on a calendar the day the license arrives is what keeps them from sneaking up. The license renews triennially, the OLC makes renewal applications available at least 60 days before the expiration date, and it is the organization's own responsibility to complete and return the renewal on time, with the current license staying in effect only when a complete and accurate renewal was filed on time. Unannounced inspections continue at least every two years. Financial records are kept under generally accepted accounting principles and audited at least triennially by an independent certified public accountant under 12VAC5-381-190(D). The policy manual gets its governing body review at least once a year under 12VAC5-381-160(C) and 12VAC5-381-180(B). Renewal, inspection window, audit, annual policy review. Four dates, all known on day one.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Virginia owner, and it is stronger here than in most states in this series. Under 12VAC5-381-20(F), any person establishing, conducting, maintaining, or operating a home care organization without a license is guilty of a Class 6 felony under section 32.1-162.15 of the Code of Virginia. Unlicensed operation in Virginia is not a fine and a warning letter. It is a felony statute, and 12VAC5-381-150(A) repeats the rule in plain terms: no person shall establish or operate a home care organization without having obtained a license. Add the quieter errors that cost owners here: budgeting the old $500 fee off an outdated checklist, inventing a fixed reserve number instead of building the three month budget the rule asks for, naming an administrator who does not meet 12VAC5-381-170, letting a caregiver take direct patient contact before the State Police report is in hand, and filing an incomplete application that goes inactive at six months with the fee gone. Virginia is a clear state because the whole rulebook is one public chapter. It is an unforgiving state for owners who plan off summaries of it.

Your next step

Work in the order the regulation checks you. Form the LLC with the SCC and get the EIN, then qualify the administrator before anything else, because that hire shapes the package. Build the three month operating budget from real line items and line up the reserve proof the bank or lender will document. Bind the $1 million general liability coverage, the malpractice coverage, and the $50,000 crime coverage or fidelity bond, and get the certificates. Draft the policy manual, the staffing plan, the job descriptions, and the orientation program, and set up the State Police background check process with the sworn disclosure statement. Then file the completed HCO application with the $1,500 fee at least 60 days before your planned opening, use the prelicensure consultation once the completed file is in, and run the agency from day one the way the unannounced inspection will measure it. An owner who files in that order spends the survey proving a working agency, not explaining a paper one.
