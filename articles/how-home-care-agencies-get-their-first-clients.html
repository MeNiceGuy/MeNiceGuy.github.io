How to Get Your First Home Care Clients: Where Private-Pay Clients Actually Come From

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 16, 2026

The license was approved, the caregivers were screened, the phone was charged, and nobody called. In the records I review, that silent month is the most dangerous month in a new agency's life, not because the business is failing, but because of what the owner does while waiting. She boosts a post. She prices an ad she cannot judge yet. She redesigns a logo. Meanwhile the families who will become her first ten clients are already asking for help that week, in rooms within driving distance of her office, and the question they ask is not directed at an ad. They ask a person they already trust: who do you recommend? First clients do not come from ads. They come from the people in your town who meet families on the worst week of their lives and get asked that one question. This article is about becoming the name those people say. In the video below I walk the rooms in order, discharge planners first, then elder law attorneys, senior centers and churches, and assisted living front desks, plus the one page sheet that makes every visit work and the log that tells you which rooms are producing. The article walks the same ground in story order, with the one federal rule underneath the first room checked against its source, so you can work the rooms correctly from the first visit.

Room one: discharge planners, and the rule that shapes the visit

Start where families have the least time. A hospital or rehab discharge planner carries one risk above all others: placing a patient with an agency that fails after discharge, because the failure lands back on that desk as a readmission, a complaint, or a family in crisis. So what a planner needs from you is not a pitch. It is proof of reliability: you answer the phone, you can staff quickly, you say no honestly when you cannot cover a case, and the first referral that reaches you goes well.

One federal rule shapes how this room works, and owners who know it visit better. Under the Medicare hospital Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR 482.43, a hospital must run an effective discharge planning process, and the evaluation behind it has to weigh a patient's likely need for post-hospital services, including non-health care services and community based care providers, and whether those services are actually available to the patient. The same rule protects the family's choice: the hospital must tell the patient or the patient's representative that they are free to choose among participating providers of post-discharge services, and it must respect the goals and preferences they express. Read what that means for you. No planner can hand you a family's business, and a good one will not try. What a planner can do is know your name, know what you cover, and know from experience that when a family chooses you, the start goes well. Your job in that room is to make all three easy to believe.

Start where the volume of discharges matches your size. Large hospitals have established agency lists and long memories. Smaller facilities, rehab units, and skilled nursing discharge offices turn over their trusted names more often, and one well handled case moves you up the list faster than any brochure.

Room two: elder law attorneys

Families meet elder law attorneys at the exact moment care gets planned and paid for: a diagnosis, a spouse who cannot manage alone, a benefits application on the horizon. Attorneys keep short lists of agencies they trust with their own clients, because referring badly reflects on the firm. Your approach is simple and specific: introduce yourself, name the exact services and areas you cover, and make it easy for the office to hand you to a family. Guardianship and care management practices in your area live in the same conversation, because their clients face the same week.

Room three: senior centers and churches

This is slower ground, and owners underrate it because it does not feel like business development. Senior center staff hear family problems all week, including the ones nobody posts anywhere. Churches carry care ministries, visitation teams, and pastors who know which families are quietly drowning. You are not there to sell. You are there to be known: attend, be useful, leave your material, and come back. Trust in these rooms compounds monthly, and the referral, when it comes, arrives pre-sold, because the family heard your name from a person they have trusted for years.

Room four: assisted living front desks

This one surprises new owners. Assisted living residents age in place until they need more help than the community provides, and families then arrange private duty care inside the building, or the resident needs a higher level of care and the family needs guidance fast. Front desk staff and resident services directors hear those family questions first, and they remember the agency owner who introduced himself properly and followed up with thanks instead of pressure.

The tool that makes every visit work: one page

Every one of these visits runs on the same tool: a one page services sheet, delivered in person. One page, no more. Your agency name and the name of the person responsible. The services you provide, in plain words a stressed family member understands. The areas you serve. How fast you can start. How to reach a human, with a phone number that gets answered. One page works because it survives: it gets pinned to a board, filed in a drawer, and handed across a desk, while a business card sits in a stack of forty. Print it on decent stock, carry it yourself, and deliver it on a rhythm: a first visit to introduce, a short follow up visit or call in a few weeks, and a standing habit of showing up before you need anything.

What to say in the room: the three minute visit

Owners freeze here and over-explain, so here is the whole visit. Three minutes, not thirty. Your name and your agency. What you do, in one sentence a family would use. The specific thing that makes you easy to work with: the areas you cover, how quickly you can assess and start, and the fact that a human answers your phone. Then stop talking. Ask about their world instead: what do families here struggle to find, what makes an agency hard for your office to work with. Note what they say, because that answer is your service design handed to you for free. Close by leaving the sheet and your card, thank them for their time by name, and go. The goal of the first visit is not a referral. It is permission to come back, and a reputation for being brief, prepared, and easy. The first referral usually arrives after the second or third contact, when a family in front of them matches the sentence you left behind.

The room most owners miss: the family itself

Every client you serve well is connected to neighbors, church members, and adult children who will face this decision within a few years. You do not ask a client for referrals like a salesperson. You do two things. First, you ask every family, at intake and again after the first month, how they heard about you, and you record the answer, because that tells you which rooms are actually producing. Second, when a family thanks you, you give them the simplest possible instruction: if a friend ever faces this, hand them this sheet. Then you make sure they have two copies. Satisfied families are the cheapest referral channel in this business, and the only cost is remembering to equip them.

Online does three supporting jobs, and only three

Your online presence supports the visits. It does not replace them. First, claim and complete your Google Business Profile, because families search for care near them by town and service. Google's own help page confirms the mechanics: you can add or claim your Business Profile at no charge, and once it is verified, customers can find your business on Search and Maps, with your services, areas, hours, and reviews in front of them. Second, be present on Nextdoor, where recommendations come from neighbors in the same streets your caregivers drive, and answer care questions helpfully when they appear. Third, be active in your local senior and community Facebook groups in the way the group rules allow: answer questions with real help and let competence be the advertisement. None of these replace the visits. They make your name familiar before and after you walk in, so the sheet on the desk connects to a face the family has already seen.

The discipline: log every source

Here is what turns all of this into a system instead of a hopeful month: log every referral source. A simple notebook or spreadsheet with the name of every person and office you visited, the date, what you left with them, and every inquiry that comes in afterward, tagged with where it came from. Within ninety days that log tells you which rooms produce and which visits to repeat, and it stops you from judging the whole effort by whichever week happened to be quiet. What I tell owners is plain: most owners quit this work about two weeks before it starts paying, because the payoff arrives as a trickle that begins weeks after the visits. The agencies that survive the empty month are the ones still making visits in week six.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every new owner working these rooms. Never promise a referral source a start you cannot staff. The fastest way off a trusted list is a yes that turns into a scramble, a late caregiver, or a quiet no-show, because the planner or the front desk hears about it from the family, not from you. An honest no, given fast, with the name of what you can cover instead, protects the relationship and usually earns the next call. A hopeful yes spends it. Reliability is the entire product you are selling in these rooms, and the first referral is the audit.

Your next step

This week, before you buy any marketing at all, make the list of rooms in your service area: the smaller hospitals, rehab units, and skilled nursing discharge offices, the elder law firms, the senior centers and churches, and the assisted living front desks. Print the one page sheet with your services in plain words, your areas, your start speed, and a phone number a human answers. Then make the first round of three minute visits, log every one, and schedule the follow up round before you leave your office. You do not need a marketing budget to fill your first schedule. You need a list of the rooms where families ask for help, a one page sheet, a phone that gets answered, and the patience to be a familiar face before you are a busy agency. Walk the rooms. The phone follows.
