How to Start a Home Care Agency in California (The $5,603 License and the Registry Every Aide Joins)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 2, 2026

The California story usually starts with an owner who has already done the exciting parts. The name is picked, the logo is done, a cousin in Los Angeles knows three families who need help, and the plan is to start taking clients next month. Then the owner goes looking for the license and finds the real California sequence. Non-medical home care in this state runs through a license from the Department of Social Services, the application travels by mail as a paper packet, the state does not start reviewing it until the packet is complete, and every caregiver the agency sends into a home has to be listed on a state registry first. The owner who planned a launch in weeks is now looking at a launch measured in months, with rent, insurance, and recruiting all running while the application sits in Sacramento.

That gap between the plan and the process is the reason this video exists, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against a California primary source today. Two points deserve care because short versions flatten them. First, California is not a registration state for agencies and it is not a no-license state. A Home Care Organization license is the license, it has carried a state fee since January 2016, and operating without it carries a per-day civil penalty. Second, the state publishes no guaranteed processing timeline for this license. The five to six month window in the video is a planning figure, and the only clock the state states plainly is its own rule that review does not begin until your fee, your forms, and your documents have all arrived.

The demand: about 6.89 million seniors, the largest senior market in the country

Start with why California draws new owners despite the entry cost. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 39,355,309 as of July 1, 2025, and 17.5 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 6.89 million seniors, more older adults than any other state has people in most age groups, and the largest senior population in the country. The same Census QuickFacts page for California puts median household income at $99,122 for 2020 to 2024. That income figure is the business case under the headcount. Private pay home care is bought out of household income, and California pairs the biggest senior population in the nation with household incomes that can carry private pay rates in its major metros.

The price families face explains the margin side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for California, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $91,520, up 5 percent and ranked 10th among states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to $40 an hour. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows the most expensive large market in the country for this service, which is why owners keep choosing California even after they read the licensing rules.

The mandate: a Home Care Organization license from Social Services, not Public Health

California licenses this business through the Department of Social Services, Community Care Licensing Division, Home Care Services Branch, under the Home Care Services Consumer Protection Act, Health and Safety Code sections 1796.10 through 1796.70. The Act has required Home Care Organizations to be licensed since January 2016. One wrong-door warning belongs up front, because founders lose weeks to it. The Department of Public Health licenses home health agencies, the medical track. The non-medical, private pay agency most founders are building is licensed by Social Services as a Home Care Organization, usually shortened to HCO. Send your questions, and later your packet, to the Home Care Services Branch in Sacramento, not to Public Health.

The statute draws the line at arranging care. Under Health and Safety Code section 1796.35, an organization that arranges or provides home care services through registered home care aides needs the HCO license, subject to the exceptions listed in section 1796.17, and providing those services without one exposes the operator to a civil penalty of $900 per day for each violation, with referral for a cease and desist order or an injunction. Read that as the state means it. Marketing, signing families, and sending aides into homes ahead of the license is not a head start. It is a per-day liability that grows while you wait for the license you should have filed for first.

The money: $5,603 for two years, $35 per aide, and the proofs that ride with the fee

The fee schedule sits on the Department of Social Services application fees page, and it is short enough to memorize. A Home Care Organization license costs $5,603 and runs two years. Home Care Aide registration costs $35 per aide and also runs two years. A change of location costs $100 and a change in corporation costs $200. The application fee is non-refundable, and the fee statute is Health and Safety Code section 1796.49. That two year structure has an end date on the books: the version of section 1796.49 that becomes operative January 1, 2029 keeps the $5,603 initial fee and moves renewals to an annual license fee of $2,802, so an owner filing today should expect the renewal rhythm to change midstream. One fee covers one location. The application instructions are explicit that an applicant licensing more than one location files a separate application for each one, and the department conducts an unannounced inspection at the licensed location, so a second office is a second fee and a second inspection file, not an amendment.

The fee is not the whole money test, because the application itself demands three financial proofs. Under Health and Safety Code section 1796.37 and the insurance section of the Department's own application instructions, form HCS 281, a complete application includes a certificate of general and professional liability insurance of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence and $3,000,000 in the aggregate, a certificate of a valid workers compensation policy covering your affiliated home care aides, and an employee dishonesty bond of at least $10,000 with third-party coverage, filed on form HCS 402. No proofs, no license. Budget the insurance and the bond as application costs, in the same line as the $5,603, because the state treats them that way.

The packet: six forms up front, six documents behind them, all by mail

California still runs this application as a mailed paper packet. The Department's Home Care Organization application process page lays out the sequence: review the HCS 281 instructions, complete the Section A forms, compile the Section B documents, and mail the completed application with the fee to the Home Care Services Branch at 744 P Street in Sacramento. The Section A forms are the HCS 200 application for a Home Care Organization license, the HCS 215 licensee applicant information, the HCS 308 designation of organizational responsibility, the HCS 309 for a partnership, corporation, or limited liability company, the HCS 402 employee dishonesty bond, the LIC 508 criminal record statement, and the HCS 9165 board of directors statement. Every person who owns 10 percent or more of the company completes the applicant information form, so ownership paperwork is part of the packet, not an afterthought.

The Section B documents are where the operating business gets described, and they are the heart of the review. B1 is your entity record: partnership agreement, articles of incorporation, or articles of organization, with an operating agreement for an LLC. B2 is a job description for every position you intend to hire, including duties, minimum qualifications, and lines of supervision. B3 is your personnel policies, which must cover abuse reporting under Health and Safety Code section 1796.42, hiring conditions that include fingerprint clearance and a statement of prior criminal convictions, and the aide conditions covered below. B4 is your training plan. B5 is your program description: business hours, the services you will provide, and the counties or area you will serve. B6 is the insurance information from the section above. The Department states the rule that drives the whole timeline in the HCS 281 introduction: an application is not considered complete, and review will not begin, until the fee, the Section A forms, and the Section B documents have all been received. An incomplete packet does not hold your place in line. It waits, and so do you.

After a complete packet lands, the Department sends a receipt notice with your HCO number, instructions for starting the fingerprinting process, and information on completing the online orientation the licensee must finish. Keep a full copy of everything you mailed in your own administrative records. The instructions require it, and the packet may be inspected during your unannounced inspection.

The registry: no aide works before the state lists her

The second system every California owner lives in is the Home Care Aide Registry, also run by the Home Care Services Branch. An affiliated home care aide, the caregiver your HCO employs, must be registered on the registry before providing services to a client. Registration runs through the Department's Guardian portal, costs the $35 fee from the schedule above, lasts two years, and turns on a criminal background clearance built on Live Scan fingerprinting submitted to the California Department of Justice and the FBI. The personnel policy section of the HCS 281 instructions adds the rest of the aide file: TB clearance, plus entry-level and annual training, are conditions of employment as an affiliated aide. Registration, TB clearance, and the five entry-level training hours all have to be done before the aide provides services. An owner who hires first and registers later has the sequence backwards and a compliance problem on the first shift.

The training floor: five hours before the first client, five hours every year

The training numbers are set in Health and Safety Code section 1796.44, and the HCS 281 training plan section tracks them exactly. An affiliated home care aide completes a minimum of five hours of entry-level training prior to presence with a client: two hours of orientation on the aide role and the terms of employment, and three hours of safety training covering basic safety precautions, emergency procedures, and infection control. On top of that, the aide completes a minimum of five hours of annual training, tied to core competencies and specific to the population served, including client rights and safety, responding to daily living needs, reporting, preventing, and detecting abuse and neglect, and help with personal hygiene. The training may be completed through an online program. Your B4 training plan, filed with the application, has to name who delivers the training, its objectives, and the title and duration of each piece, and include a sample training verification log. The state approves your training system before you have a single client, which is why the plan belongs in the first draft of the business, not the week before launch.

The timeline: plan on months, and spend them building

Here is the honest timeline picture. The Department publishes the fee, the forms, and the completeness rule. It does not publish a guaranteed number of days to issue this license, and any exact day count you hear is an estimate, not a promise from the state. The video this article is built from uses five to six months as the planning window, and that is the right way to hold it: a planning figure from the field, not a state commitment. What you control is when the clock starts. Because review does not begin until the packet is complete, an owner who spends a month assembling documents, mails a packet missing the bond form, and waits for an analyst to ask for it has not bought a five month process. That owner has bought a one month delay followed by the process. File the entity first, bind the insurance and the bond early, write the job descriptions, personnel policies, and training plan while the entity paperwork settles, and mail one complete packet. Then spend the wait the way the strong California operators do: building referral relationships, interviewing aides you will register the moment the license issues, and setting up the registry and training records your first inspection will ask to see.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every California owner. The expensive mistakes in this state are sequence mistakes, and they all happen before the first client. Take clients before the license issues and the exposure is $900 a day, not a warning letter. Hire aides before they clear the registry, the TB check, and the five training hours and your first shifts are out of compliance no matter how good the care is. Assume the $5,603 is the cost of entry and the insurance minimums, the workers compensation policy, and the bond arrive as surprises in the middle of the application, when they were printed in the instructions from the start. And plan a launch date off a processing promise the state never made, and payroll, rent, and marketing all come due in a month when you still cannot legally serve anyone. California is not a hard state because the rules are hidden. The HCS 281 instructions run fourteen pages and tell you exactly what a complete packet contains. It is a hard state for owners who read them after they have already spent the money.

Your next step

Start the registration clock before anything else, and start it with a complete packet. Pull the HCS 281 instructions from the Home Care Services Branch, form your entity, get quotes for the $1,000,000 and $3,000,000 liability policy, the workers compensation policy, and the $10,000 employee dishonesty bond, and write the six Section B documents in the order the state lists them. Mail the packet with the $5,603 fee, keep a full copy for your records, and use the months that follow to line up aides you can register, clear, and train the week the license arrives. An owner who files in that order spends the wait building the agency instead of repairing the application.
