How to Start a Home Care Agency in Wisconsin (No State License for Private Pay Non-Medical Care)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 8, 2026

The Wisconsin story starts with a founder who kept waiting for a permission slip that does not exist. She had priced insurance, she had two caregivers ready to talk, and she had a list of families in the Milwaukee suburbs who needed help at home. What she did not have was the license, and every week she spent hunting for the application was a week she was not serving anyone. When I recorded my Wisconsin walkthrough, I called this state the sleeper market in the Midwest, and the reason is in that hunt. Wisconsin has no state license for private pay non-medical home care. There is no licensure application standing between a prepared founder and a first private pay client. That is not the same as no rules, and it is not the same as no work. The regulated lanes that sit right next to this one, Medicaid personal care and skilled home health, are real, licensed or certified, and easy to wander into by accident. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

The demand: about 1.24 million seniors, more than one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Wisconsin. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 5,972,787 as of July 1, 2025, and 20.8 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.24 million seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Wisconsin puts median household income at $77,485 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

The price families face explains the revenue side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Wisconsin, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $82,940, down 2 percent, ranked 22nd among states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $36 an hour. The competition side is the other half of the sleeper claim. In my own 50 state analysis, Wisconsin runs thin on agency density compared with its Midwest neighbors, roughly 5 to 10 agencies for every 100,000 seniors depending on which count you use. That density figure is my analysis, not a state published number, and I label it that way because the state does not publish a count of private pay agencies it does not license.

The license question, answered directly: private pay non-medical care has no Wisconsin license

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Wisconsin? For private pay care, no state license exists to get. Read the structure of Wisconsin law and you can see why founders get confused, because the state does regulate the lanes around this one, hard. What Wisconsin calls supportive home care is defined in Rule DHS 73.03(17) as the provision of services except nursing care, intended to maintain participants in independent or supervised living in their own home, help them meet daily living needs, address their need for social contact, and keep them out of a nursing home. That definition sits in a chapter about Medical Assistance waiver administration. It is a service definition, not a license, and no Wisconsin rule attaches a licensure application to opening a private pay supportive home care agency.

The two regulated lanes are specific, and naming them is how you stay out of trouble. Skilled home health is licensed, under the statute at section 50.49 and Rule chapter DHS 133, covered in the next section. Personal care billed to Medicaid is certified, under Rule DHS 105.17, covered after that. A founder whose clients pay out of pocket for companionship, homemaker help, and non-medical personal care assistance is in neither lane on day one. That is the whole opening in Wisconsin: the state gate that slows founders down in most of the states in this series is simply not there for this model, so the timeline is set by your own preparation instead of a department queue.

The skilled lane: home health is licensed under DHS 133, and the word home health is protected

The moment your plan includes skilled nursing or therapy, you have left the no license lane. Under Rule DHS 133.03(1), no person or business may establish, operate, or maintain a home health agency, or represent and advertise by any means that it operates a home health agency in Wisconsin, without first obtaining a license from the Department of Health Services. Hold the advertising half of that sentence, because it reaches further than most founders expect: the license duty attaches to holding yourself out as a home health agency, not only to delivering skilled visits.

The home health application itself is set out in DHS 133.03(3) and (4). The application goes to the department on its form with a nonrefundable fee of $300, and it includes proof of sufficient financial responsibility to operate the agency for at least 90 days. Within 90 days after receiving a complete application, the department either approves it and issues a license or denies it, unless it first issues a provisional license for a term of 90 days, renewable on request for additional 90 day terms up to one year from the original provisional date. During the provisional period the agency must actively serve at least 10 patients requiring skilled nursing care or other therapeutic services in Wisconsin. A regular home health license is valid indefinitely unless suspended, revoked, or surrendered, and after the first license the annual continuing fee is 0.25 percent of patient fee revenue, with a minimum of $500 and a maximum of $2,500 under DHS 133.03(4)(d). Operating a home health agency without that license is unlawful under section 50.49(8), punishable by a fine of not more than $100 for the first offense and not more than $200 for each subsequent offense, with each day of violation after the first conviction a separate offense. The dollar fines are small. The record of operating unlicensed in a licensed lane is the real cost, and it follows an owner into every later application in this field.

The Medicaid lane: personal care agencies are certified under DHS 105.17, in three steps

The second regulated lane is the one most private pay founders eventually ask about, because Medicaid personal care is a large payer in Wisconsin. It is not a license either. It is a certification, and the Department of Health Services runs it through the Division of Quality Assurance. The department page on personal care agency application and certification lays out a three step process. First, you complete and submit a personal care agency application through the DHS DQA Provider Portal. Second, your agency must pass a survey by the Division of Quality Assurance against Rule DHS 105.17 and Rule DHS 107.112, the personal care services rule. Third, on a successful survey the division recommends approval for Medicaid certification to Wisconsin Medicaid, and only then does the agency apply for Medicaid certification through the ForwardHealth portal. The department states the sequence plainly: you may not apply for a Medicaid contract until the division recommends approval after a successful initial survey. Serving Medicaid clients first and certifying later is not a shortcut in this state, it is unpaid work at best.

The certification rule, DHS 105.17, shows what survey readiness actually means, and private pay founders should read it even if Medicaid is a year away, because it is the closest thing Wisconsin has to a published standard for running a personal care agency. A certified provider must be fit and qualified, present a proposal and a plan of operation covering the whole process from referral through delivery of services and follow-up, adopt written policies and procedures that outline agency operations, and document adequate resources to maintain a cash flow sufficient to cover operating expenses for 60 days under subsection (1g)(a). It must employ or contract with at least one registered nurse, and its RN supervisor assesses each client, secures physician orders renewed once every 3 months unless the physician specifies a longer period up to one year, builds the plan of care, and reviews that plan at least every 60 days. Personal care workers must be trained in the provision of personal care services and in each skill they are assigned, demonstrate each skill successfully before providing it to a client independently, receive orientation before providing services, and be at least 16 years old. The rule also requires the provider to capture and retain electronic visit verification records under subsection (1g)(cm). None of that is a private pay license requirement. All of it is the standard a certified competitor down the road is already being surveyed against, which is why families and referral sources ask private pay agencies the same questions.

Background checks: mandatory in the regulated lanes, expected in every lane

Wisconsin has a caregiver background check system, and its scope is worth stating exactly. Section 50.065 of the statutes and Rule chapter DHS 12 require caregiver background checks for entities regulated by the department, and the list of covered entities in DHS 12.02 includes programs regulated under chapter DHS 105, which carries the personal care certification, and chapter DHS 133, which carries the home health license. The department guidance on offenses affecting caregiver eligibility states the rhythm: entities must conduct and document caregiver background checks before hiring or contracting with an individual, every four years after that, and when a change in status occurs, and an entity may not employ a caregiver whose record carries one of the listed barring offenses unless eligibility is restored through rehabilitation review.

For a purely private pay agency, I give the same advice I gave in the video, and I label it as my professional judgment: run caregiver background checks anyway, on the same before hire and periodic rhythm, and keep the documentation where a family or a referral partner can be shown it exists. No license does not mean no standards. In a state with no licensure gate, your screening file, your training records, your insurance certificate, and your policies are the proof of seriousness that a license provides automatically in other states. Founders who treat the missing license as a missing standard are the ones referral sources quietly pass over.

What you still do today: form the business the Wisconsin way

The business formation step has one Wisconsin twist that trips up founders following generic guides. Wisconsin has no secretary of state corporations office for this filing. Limited liability companies file with the Department of Financial Institutions. You file Articles of Organization, Form 502, under section 183.0201 of the statutes, and the Department of Financial Institutions online filing page prices the online filing fee at $130, and states that the fee is not refundable. File online, get the entity standing, then do the ordinary employer load in the right order: an EIN from the IRS, liability insurance in place before the first client, written policies and procedures covering the services you will actually sell, caregiver training and background screening set up as a system rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do, with the skilled and medical tasks on the does not do side of the line.

The practical warning

Two Wisconsin traps cost new owners money, and both come from misreading the no license headline. The first is lane drift. A private pay agency that starts with companionship and homemaker work, then lets a caregiver help with a wound, a medication decision, or a therapy exercise because a family asked, has drifted toward skilled care without the DHS 133 license, and an agency that markets itself with the words home health has stepped into that licensed lane by advertising alone under DHS 133.03(1). Write the service boundary into the care plan and the caregiver instructions, and train to it, because in Wisconsin the boundary is the license. The second trap is the opposite mistake, assuming no license means fast money with no proof. Sleeper markets do not fill themselves. Even with thin competition, families cannot hire an agency they have never heard of, and the founders who win in a no license state are the ones who built the insurance, the policies, the screening, and the training records before they needed them, then marketed hard enough to be found. Low friction cuts both ways: it lets you start fast, and it lets every unprepared competitor start fast too. Your preparation is the moat.

Your next step

Work in lane order. Decide first which lane your first clients are in, private pay non-medical care, Medicaid personal care, or skilled home health, because that one decision determines whether you file nothing with the Department of Health Services, apply for certification under DHS 105.17 in its three step order, or apply for a license under DHS 133 with its $300 fee and 90 day decision clock. If private pay is the lane, form the entity this week, Articles of Organization at $130 online with the Department of Financial Institutions if you choose an LLC, then stand up the EIN, the insurance, the policies, and the caregiver screening and training file before you market. Put the $36 an hour CareScout median next to your own cost math, not in place of it, and price from your numbers. An owner who runs that order can be serving a first private pay client at the speed of her own preparation in a state with about 1.24 million seniors, while founders in licensed states are still waiting on a department to answer.
