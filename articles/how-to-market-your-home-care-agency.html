How to Market Your Home Care Agency: Call Back in One Hour, and Build the Referral System Behind It

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 27, 2026

The call came in on a Tuesday afternoon, while the owner was out on an assessment. A daughter had just been told her mother was being discharged on Friday, and she was working down a list of agencies, stressed and in a hurry. She left a message. The owner saw it that evening, meant to call first thing in the morning, and did. By then the daughter was polite and short: the family had already found someone. Nothing about that loss showed up in any marketing report. There was no ad to blame and no website to fix. The agency lost the client in the gap between a message and a call back, and that gap is where a lot of home care marketing actually happens. This article walks the system I teach owners for marketing a non-medical home care agency, in the order to build it: referral partners first, your Google storefront second, the people already around your agency third, community presence underneath, response speed over all of it, and paid advertising last.

Private pay clients come from people, not ads

Start with the fact the whole system rests on. Private pay clients do not come from a logo, a boosted post, or the ad an owner bought last month. They come from people: a discharge planner, a social worker, a pastor, a neighbor, someone who gets asked one question on a family's worst week, which is who do you recommend. Marketing a home care agency means becoming the name those people say, and then proving them right fast enough that they say it again. Every section below is one part of that, and the order matters, because the channels that compound are built before the channels that rent attention.

First, referral partners, and what they actually risk

When a patient is leaving a hospital or a rehab facility, a discharge planner or a social worker helps the family find post-hospital help. Understand the legal shape of that room before you walk into it. Under 42 CFR 482.43, the hospital discharge planning evaluation weighs a patient's likely need for post-hospital services, including non-health care services and community based care providers, and the availability of those services. The hospital must assist the patient and family in selecting a post-acute care provider, and the choice belongs to the patient. No planner can hand your agency a family's business, and any pitch that assumes otherwise misunderstands the room.

What a planner can do is remember your agency when a family asks for names, and that memory is earned on risk, not on brochures. If a planner sends a family to an agency that does not answer the phone, cannot staff the case, or handles it badly, the failure lands back on that planner's desk. So they do not need your pitch. They need proof. Answer when they call. Staff quickly when you say yes. Say no honestly when you cannot cover a case, because an honest no protects your place in their memory and a bad yes ends it. In the agencies I work with, the practical entry point is the smaller facility, the rehab unit, and the skilled nursing discharge office, where one well handled case moves an agency up the list faster than any printed material.

The wider circle around the hospital

The circle is wider than the discharge office. Elder law attorneys meet families at the exact moment care gets planned and paid for, after a diagnosis, or when a spouse cannot manage alone, and those firms keep short lists of agencies they trust with their own clients. Senior center staff hear family problems all week, including the ones nobody posts online. Faith communities and care ministries know which families are quietly drowning. A useful referral relationship looks the same in each of these rooms. You show up with value, in the form of a one page sheet that says what you do, where you serve, and how fast you can start. You respond fast when they call. And after every case they send you, you report back, good outcome or bad, and you thank them by name. That reporting step is what most agencies skip, and it is what turns one referral into a habit.

Second, your Google storefront and real reviews

Your Google Business Profile is your always on storefront, and it is free. Google's own Business Profile Help covers how owners add or claim a profile, verify it, and manage how the business appears on Search and Maps. Set it up correctly: the categories that fit the work, the services you actually provide, the areas you serve, your hours, and a phone number a human answers. When a family searches for home care in your town, this profile is what they find, and the reviews on it are what they read before they call anyone.

So feed it real reviews. Ask every family you have served well, and make it easy by handing them the direct review link instead of hoping they find it. Respond to every review, the good ones and the hard ones, in plain language. And never buy reviews or have your own staff write them. That is not only a trust problem. Fake and incentivized reviews violate Google's review policies and can be removed. One honest review from a real family is worth more than a wall of perfect ones nobody believes.

Third, the people already carrying your name

Look inside your own operation before you spend anywhere else. Your caregivers are in homes and neighborhoods across your service area every day, and people ask them who they work for. Give every caregiver your cards and your one page sheet, and make it normal to hand them out. Your current clients are the second source. A family you serve well knows neighbors, church members, and adult children who will face this same decision. You do not ask like a salesperson. You ask at the right moment, after a problem you solved well, or at a regular check in, and you keep it simple: if a friend ever faces this, hand them our sheet. Then make sure they have two copies.

Fourth, community presence, the slow channel that compounds

Health fairs, senior expos, caregiver support groups, library talks, and church health ministries will not fill your schedule next week, and owners underrate them for exactly that reason. What they do is make your name familiar before a family ever needs you, so that when the discharge planner or the neighbor says your name, the family has already heard it somewhere. Show up repeatedly, not once. Teach something small and real when you get the chance, such as how to spot when a parent needs help at home, or what questions to ask an agency. Being known locally is an asset that builds every month you stay visible.

Fifth, response speed, the marketing nobody reports

Now the piece this article is named for. I count response speed as marketing even though it never shows up in a marketing report, and I state the one hour standard plainly as professional practice from the agencies I work with, not as a study finding. When a family finally calls agencies, they usually call down a list. In my experience, the agency that answers live, or calls back within the hour, is the one that gets the assessment visit. The one that calls back tomorrow gets told the family already found someone, which is exactly the Tuesday story this article opened with.

Speed is not a personality trait. It is a system, and a new owner can build it before the first client arrives. Name who answers inquiries and who backs that person up. Decide what happens after hours before an after hours call tests it. Log every inquiry, with the source, the time it came in, and the time of the first response, so the gap is a number you can see instead of a feeling. Give every inquiry a same day response. If the first call goes unanswered, place a second call the next morning, and send a short note after the assessment either way. That log does double duty: it is also the record that tells you which referral rooms actually produce, which is the subject of the close below.

What to stop paying for

Here is the practical warning, and it is about sequence, not about advertising itself. If your referral base does not exist yet, generic advertising is where your money goes to die. Boosted posts, broad ads, and bought leads sell you attention from people who are not looking for care today, at prices a new agency cannot carry. I am not against paid marketing. I am against paying for it first. Ads rent attention. Referrals compound. The sequence that works is referral partners first, your storefront and reviews second, community presence building underneath, and paid spending last, only to amplify a channel that is already producing. Until then, every marketing dollar you were about to spend is better spent on the gas, the printing, and the hours it takes to walk into the rooms where your clients actually come from.

The system in one breath, and the product that carries the startup documents

Referral partners are the channel. Your Google profile and real reviews are the storefront that backs them up. Your caregivers and your clients carry your name into rooms you will never enter. Community presence makes the name familiar. Response speed decides who gets the assessment. And the discipline underneath all of it is a simple log of every source, every visit, and every inquiry. If you are still in the startup stage and want the documents and templates behind the agency itself in one place, that is what the Home Care Startup Core Kit is for. The marketing system above is the work that makes those documents worth having, because a licensed agency nobody can name is the problem this article opened with.

Your next step this week

Here is the one actionable next step. Pull every inquiry your agency received in the last thirty days, or, if you have not opened yet, set the log up today so the first inquiry lands in it. For each one, write the source, the time it arrived, and the time of your first response. Any gap over one hour during your stated business hours is your first fix, before any new spending: name the backup person, write the after hours rule, and start the clock on the next inquiry. Then take your one page sheet to one discharge office, one elder law firm, and one senior center this week, in that order.
