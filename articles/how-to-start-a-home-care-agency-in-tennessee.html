How to Start a Home Care Agency in Tennessee (The $810 PSSA License and the Department Nobody Expects)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 10, 2026

The Tennessee story starts with a founder in the Nashville suburbs who did almost everything right and still nearly filed with the wrong state department. She had her entity formed, a caregiver list, and families asking about start dates. She had also read a stack of startup guides that told her Tennessee home care runs through the health department, so that is where her paperwork was headed. It does not. For the private pay, non-medical agency most founders are building, Tennessee calls the business a Personal Support Services Agency, a PSSA, and the license comes from the Tennessee Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services. That one routing fact decides your forms, your fee, and your survey. When I recorded my Tennessee walkthrough, the headline was that Tennessee has no Certificate of Need for non-medical care, and that part holds up. The licensing name in that video does not, and this article follows the verified rules, not the video, wherever the two part ways. Here is the full picture in the order a founder actually meets it: the demand, the license and its 50 percent test, the Certificate of Need line, the process and the fee, and the policy and personnel files the desk audit will read first.

The demand: about 1.36 million seniors in a state still growing

Start with why owners keep looking at Tennessee. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 7,315,076 as of July 1, 2025, up 5.8 percent from the April 1, 2020 estimates base, and 18.6 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.36 million seniors, the same senior count my video carried. The same Census QuickFacts page for Tennessee puts median household income at $69,595 for 2020 to 2024. That income figure is the honest context for private pay in this state: the demand is large and growing, and the rate has to fit a middle income household budget, which makes cost control part of the business model from day one.

The price families face is documented in the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Tennessee. The annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $70,928, up 3 percent, ranked 42nd among the states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $31 an hour. Tennessee care costs less than the national median, and that cuts both ways: families can afford more hours, and an owner has less room for sloppy scheduling and mileage waste. One number from my video I am not carrying forward: the projection of senior growth to 2040 attributed to the University of Tennessee Boyd Center. Its parts do not reconcile, a 25 percent rise on 1.36 million does not land at 1.53 million, and I could not re-verify the underlying Boyd Center figure against a primary release today, so the article rests on the Census figures above instead. The competition side is the other half of the story. In my own 50 state analysis, Tennessee runs about 0.10 Medicare certified home health agencies for every 1,000 seniors, a low density figure. That density figure is my analysis, not a state published number, and I label it that way.

The license question, answered directly: a PSSA license, and the 50 percent test picks the department

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Tennessee? Yes. To provide personal support services, an agency needs a license, and the program rules are Tennessee Rule Chapter 0940-05-38, Minimum Program Requirements for Personal Support Services Agencies. Rule 0940-05-38-.01 defines the work in four parts, all delivered in the client permanent or temporary residence: self-care assistance such as eating, dressing, toileting, bathing, mobility, and transfer help; household assistance such as housekeeping, laundry, meal planning and preparation, shopping, and bill paying; personal assistance to access community activities such as transportation and social or recreational activities; and education services, which the same rule defines as consultation by a registered nurse about a chronic condition. An agency, under that rule, includes every entity that employs or subcontracts with individuals who provide those services. If you hire caregivers and send them into homes to do this work, you are a PSSA in Tennessee law whether or not your marketing ever uses the term.

The licensor turns on who your clients are, and the rule states the test as a majority count. Under Rule 0940-05-38-.04(3), an agency seeking an initial license submits an attestation of the categorization of the majority of its client population. An agency with 50 percent or more of its client base being aged, or having a diagnosis of mental illness or substance abuse, should obtain its license from the Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services, whose Office of Licensure runs the process on its Become a Licensed Provider page. A senior focused private pay agency sits squarely in that first group, because aged clients are the majority by design. An agency with 50 percent or more of its client base having a developmental or intellectual disability goes to the other licensor named in the rule, the department that now operates as the Tennessee Department of Disability and Aging, whose licensure FAQ page lists Personal Support Services among the services it licenses. One agency needs one of the two licenses, not both, and the attestation is not a formality: Rule 0940-05-38-.05(5) requires the licensee to serve only recipients who will not cause the agency to violate its licensed status based on that category.

The exemptions in Rule 0940-05-38-.03(2) are narrow, and founders should read them before assuming one fits. A person who serves only one recipient and does not hold out to the public as being in the personal support business is exempt, as is a person serving only family members, a person providing only housekeeping with no other assistance with major life activities, a person providing only transportation, delivery only services, and services in homeless shelters. A home care organization licensed under Title 68, Chapter 11, Part 2 is also exempt from this chapter, which is the skilled lane covered in the next section. An agency marketing to the public and serving multiple households fits none of the individual exemptions.

The Certificate of Need line: none for a PSSA, and a hard gate for home health

My video headline was that Tennessee requires no Certificate of Need for non-medical care, and on the verified record that is right, with the boundary stated plainly. A Certificate of Need is a separate approval run by the Tennessee Health Facilities Commission, and it attaches to the skilled home health lane, not to a PSSA. The Commission home health licensure procedure states it as step one: you must first apply for a Certificate of Need from the Health Facilities Commission prior to applying for licensure of a home health agency, with narrow exceptions for an agency providing only pediatric services or only services in the EEOICPA federal program, which instead takes on an accreditation duty. That procedure and the home health application are posted with the Health Facilities Commission licensure applications. Home health means skilled nursing, therapy, medical social services, and home health aide services under physician orders, licensed as a home care organization at its own annual fee of $1,404 on that application fee schedule. None of that is the PSSA lane. A founder who stays non-medical never files a Certificate of Need application, and a founder who later wants skilled services is looking at a different license, a different commission, and the Certificate of Need fight first.

This is also where the correction to my video belongs, stated once and clearly. The video named a Home Care Organization license through the Tennessee Department of Health as the required license. For the senior focused, non-medical model this article covers, that naming is wrong on both parts: the license is the PSSA license under Rule Chapter 0940-05-38, and the department is Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services for an aged majority client base. Health facility licensure in Tennessee has also moved since the older guides were written, from the Department of Health to the independent Health Facilities Commission. An application built on the video naming would go to the wrong counter. The article follows the current rules.

The process: four forms to the regional office, an invoice, then a desk audit

The TDMHSAS process is a sequence, and the department posts it on the Become a Licensed Provider page. First, review the licensure rules for the service. Second, complete and submit four forms to the Regional Office of Licensure nearest the location to be licensed: the Initial Application, the Fact Sheet, the Background Check and Privacy Statement Form, and the Financial Statement. Third, the Office of Licensure sends an invoice for the licensing fee once those forms are received, and the fee is paid on that invoice. Fourth, life safety and fire inspection: PSSAs currently do not require one, because clients do not come to the agency place of business for care. Fifth, when all paperwork is received and the fee is paid, the office arranges the initial inspection, and for PSSAs that inspection is conducted as an electronic desk audit, covering items such as policies and procedures. Sixth, once the inspection is conducted and any deficiencies are corrected, an initial license is issued for up to one year. Under Rule Chapter 0940-05-02, which governs licensure administration for this department, an initial license will not exceed 12 months and a full license is valid for up to 12 months, so this is an annual license, renewed every year. Rule 0940-05-38-.04(6) adds two items founders forget: submit the list of counties where you will provide services, and the address where the agency maintains its employee and service recipient records.

On timing, be precise about what the state does and does not promise. TDMHSAS publishes no processing timeline for a PSSA application in the sources above, so no honest article can quote you a number of days for this lane. What the rules do set is the order: no license issues until the fee is paid, deficiencies must be corrected before a license issues under Rule 0940-05-02-.06, and the desk audit reads your paper before anyone watches you work. Plan the calendar around readiness, not around a guessed approval date, and do not sign client start dates against a license you do not yet hold. For contrast only, the Department of Disability and Aging states on its FAQ page that review of an initial license in its lane may take at least six months due to application volume. That six month figure belongs to the disability lane, not to TDMHSAS, and it is a useful warning about how Tennessee licensure queues move in general.

The fee: $810 per site for one category, non-refundable, and due before any determination

The fee schedule lives in Rule 0940-05-02-.05, in the licensure administration rules. The rate is based on the number of distinct categories of service to be operated at each site. For a nonresidential site, one distinct category of service is $810. The schedule then steps up: $1,010 for two categories, $1,220 for three, $1,420 for four, and $1,620 for more than four. A PSSA is one category, so a single site, senior focused agency budgets $810 for the initial application and $810 again at each renewal, because the same fee applies to each initial and renewal application. Three conditions in the same rule matter as much as the amount. All fees submitted are non-refundable. The fee must be submitted before the department makes a determination to grant or deny licensure. And Rule 0940-05-38-.04(5) closes the loop: no license shall be issued until the appropriate fees are submitted. In practice the department invoices you after your forms arrive, per its process page, so the practical step is to have the $810 ready the day you file, not to go looking for it when the invoice lands.

The paper the desk audit reads: 17 policy subjects and a named operator

Because the initial inspection is a desk audit, your policies are the inspection. Rule 0940-05-38-.06(1) lists the subjects the written policies and procedures must cover, and a founder should build to the list, not to a generic manual bought off the shelf. The list includes a description of services with enrollment and termination criteria, an organizational chart showing lines of authority, the plan of action when a personal support worker is absent, including notice to the recipient and timeframes, a schedule of fees when applicable, a statement of service recipient rights with grievance procedures, confidentiality protections, emergency plans including fire evacuation and natural disaster, reporting and investigation of suspected abuse or neglect and other critical incidents with corrective action, communicable disease detection and prevention, receipt and disbursement of money on behalf of recipients, access by department licensure staff during an investigation, medication assistance procedures where that service is offered, transportation policies covering safe vehicles, a Tennessee driver license for anyone transporting recipients, and vehicle liability insurance, policies on devices such as a Hoyer lift or gait belt after training, the categorization policy for the majority of the client base, cooperation with department investigations, and an alternate staffing plan for absences. That is 17 lettered subjects, (a) through (q), and the desk audit can ask for any of them.

Governance is lighter than in most licensed states, and founders should hear that accurately. Rule 0940-05-38-.05 requires the licensee to comply with applicable laws, to administer the agency under its policies, to exercise general direction over the agency, and to designate an individual responsible for the operation of the agency. The PSSA rules set no degree, license, or experience credential for that designated individual, and they require no supervising nurse for the agency as a whole. A registered nurse enters the rule in two specific places only: education services are by definition consultation by a registered nurse under Rule 0940-05-38-.01(6), and medication assistance training must be performed by, or under the general supervision of, a registered nurse under Rule 0940-05-38-.08(1)(b). An agency that offers neither education services nor medication assistance does not need an RN on staff to hold this license. Medication assistance itself is tightly defined in Rule 0940-05-38-.01(9): reminders and opening packaging, loosening a cap, placing medication within reach, steadying a hand, and similar help. It does not include injections or any administration reserved to licensed medical personnel, and workers may provide it only after documented training, with written authorization from the recipient or representative first, under the policy terms in Rule 0940-05-38-.06(1)(l).

The people rules: age 18, three registry and record checks, and competency in seven areas before the first visit

Rule 0940-05-38-.07 builds the personnel file, and it is specific enough to use as a hiring checklist. Every personal support services worker must be 18 years of age or older under the personnel policies in .07(2)(a). Each file holds a signed and dated job description, verification the worker meets the position requirements, evidence of a criminal background check as required by T.C.A. section 33-2-1202, evidence the worker status on the Tennessee Abuse Registry has been checked, evidence of status on the state sexual offender registry, the worker date of birth, annual performance evaluations, a worker notice covering the agency responsibility for wages, taxes, workers compensation, unemployment, and overtime past 40 hours, a work history covering the past five years, and personal references from at least three individuals, one of whom has known the worker for at least five years. No person listed on the Abuse Registry or the sexual offender registry may be hired or permitted to provide services. The personnel policies add the rhythm: a criminal background check every two years, or within ten days of employment, or within ten days of a change of responsibilities that adds direct contact with recipients; the Abuse Registry and the sexual offender registry checked annually and prior to direct contact; and a bar on any worker borrowing or taking funds or property from a recipient.

Training is competency based, with no fixed hour count in the rule. Under Rule 0940-05-38-.08(1)(a), before beginning work with service recipients, every worker must demonstrate basic competency in seven areas: observing, reporting, and documenting changes in daily living skills; abuse, neglect, and exploitation detection, reporting, and prevention; service recipient rights; universal health precautions including infection control; how to assist with personal hygiene; service recipient safety; and emergency and disaster procedures, including emergency transportation, emergency medical care, and staff coverage. The personnel policy rule adds language skills sufficient to read instructions, prepare reports, and communicate with the recipient, plus documented training specific to individual recipient needs in self-care, household management, and community living. Training on job related topics continues at least annually under .08(1)(c). One financial proof sits in this section too: Rule 0940-05-38-.07(3) requires the licensee to have proof of liability insurance coverage for the agency, workers, and others who provide services. The rule sets no dollar minimum for that coverage, so get the quote early and size it with your agent and your referral partners expectations, rather than hunting for a number the rule does not contain.

What you still do today: form the business the Tennessee way

The entity step is its own Tennessee surprise. On the Tennessee Secretary of State Business Forms and Fees page, Articles of Organization for a domestic LLC, Form SS-4270, are listed at $300 with an asterisk, the highest formation fee in this state series, and the annual report carries the same per member structure. The rule for a PSSA does not force an LLC: the agency definition in Rule 0940-05-38-.01(3) expressly includes a sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation, limited liability company, and limited liability partnership. Choose the entity with your tax professional, file it, get the EIN, then work the license order: settle your majority client attestation, build the 17 policy subjects, line up liability coverage, and submit the four forms to the regional licensure office that serves your location.

The practical warning

Two Tennessee traps cost new owners money, and the first is the one my video got wrong, so learn from it instead of repeating it. Sending your application, your questions, or your fee to the health facilities counter because a guide called this a home care organization license wastes the one resource a startup cannot buy back, which is queue time. The second trap is starting early. Rule 0940-05-02-.03 is blunt: no person, partnership, association, or corporation may begin delivering services until the department issues a license, and providing personal support services without a license is unlawful and may result in civil or criminal sanctions under the cited sections of Title 33. A founder who covers a family shift as a favor while the desk audit is pending, under her agency name, is not being helpful. She is operating unlicensed. Market, recruit, and build files during the wait. Serve nobody until the license is in hand.

Your next step

Work in license order. This week, answer the one question that routes everything: will 50 percent or more of your clients be aged, or have a mental illness or substance abuse diagnosis? For a senior focused agency the answer is yes, and your licensor is the TDMHSAS Office of Licensure. Say so on the attestation, submit the Initial Application, Fact Sheet, Background Check and Privacy Statement Form, and Financial Statement to the regional office nearest your location, with the counties you will serve and your records address, and keep the $810 fee ready for the invoice. While the desk audit runs, build the files it will read and the files your first survey will read after it: the 17 policy subjects, personnel files with the background check, both registry checks, five year work history, and three references, and documented competency in the seven training areas for every worker before that worker meets a client. Put the $31 an hour CareScout median next to your own cost math, not in place of it, and price from your numbers. An owner who runs that order opens into a state with about 1.36 million seniors, no Certificate of Need standing in front of the non-medical license, and a desk audit she was ready for.
