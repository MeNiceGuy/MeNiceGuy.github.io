How to Start a Group Home (Licensing by Population, Waiver Funding, Step by Step)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 15, 2026

A group home looks simple from the street: a house, a small number of residents, staff around the clock. The licensing behind it is not simple, because there is no single group home license in America. There are dozens, one for each population and each state, and the first decision you make, who will live in the home, decides which license, which authority, and which funding stream you are dealing with. This page walks the decision in the order that keeps owners out of trouble, and each state page in this guide then gives you the exact types, authority, and application sequence for your state.

The group home types, and who licenses them

IDD and DD group homes serve adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities, usually in homes of four to eight residents. They are licensed by the state developmental disabilities agency or the department of human services, and placements are funded mostly by Medicaid 1915(c) home and community based services waivers.

Residential care and assisted living homes serve mostly older adults who need help with daily living, meals, medication support, and supervision. They are licensed by the department of health or the aging and adult services agency in most states, under names that vary: assisted living facility, residential care facility, personal care home, adult care home. Small homes and large facilities often sit in the same rule chapter at different levels.

Adult foster care is the smallest licensed form, a family home caring for a handful of unrelated adults, licensed in many states as its own category.

Behavioral health residential homes serve adults with mental health or substance use needs and are usually licensed by the behavioral health authority, often with a separate certification for the treatment program inside the home.

Children's residential and therapeutic foster care sit with the child welfare agency, under different rules again. Searchers landing here from foster care questions should know the license is real but separate, and the Texas state page in this guide addresses the Texas foster care cluster directly.

The steps to start a group home, in order

  1. Choose the population and the home type. IDD adults, older adults, behavioral health, children: the population picks the license, the authority, and the payer. Everything after this step is paperwork in service of this decision.
  2. Confirm the license with the licensing authority. Your state page names the authority and the exact home types it licenses. Read the rule chapter before you sign a lease, because bed count, staffing, and bathroom and accessibility rules are all in it.
  3. Check the local layer. Zoning, occupancy, and fire and life safety codes apply to the house itself. Most owners clear the state license path and then discover a local rule that costs them the property. Run the local check before the money moves.
  4. Form the business and secure the home. Entity, EIN, insurance, and a home that can pass inspection: accessibility, alarms and life safety systems, resident bedrooms and common space that meet the rule chapter's square footage and ratio requirements.
  5. Write the operating system. Policies for admission, care planning, medication handling, incident reporting, emergencies, staffing and training, and resident rights, written to your state rule chapter, plus the staffing plan that covers the home around the clock.
  6. Apply and pass inspection. The application, background checks for owners and staff, the home inspection, and the life safety survey run in the authority's order. Corrections are normal; an incomplete application is what actually costs time.
  7. Enroll for funding. A license lets you operate. It does not pay you. Medicaid waiver provider enrollment, and any managed care or state contract the placements flow through, is a separate approval with its own application, and in waiver programs the state also controls how many homes or beds it will fund.
  8. Open and admit against the license. Admit only residents whose needs match your licensed level, staff to the assessed need, and keep the records the surveyors will read at the first renewal.

Group home requirements, the pattern every state follows

  • The license for your population and bed count, from the authority your state assigns.
  • A home that passes health, sanitation, and life safety inspection, with local zoning cleared.
  • A qualified administrator or manager, and background checks on owners and every staff member.
  • Written policies matched to the rule chapter: care, medication, incidents, emergencies, rights, grievances.
  • Staffing and training that match the residents' assessed needs, documented per person.
  • For funded placements, Medicaid waiver provider enrollment after the license, not instead of it.

How long does it take, and how much does it cost?

Both answers are state answers. Licensing reviews run months in most states because application review, background checks, inspection, and corrections happen in sequence, and the license fee is usually the smallest cost in the project, well behind the home itself, the life safety work, and staffing hired before the first resident arrives. Your state page prints every fee and timeline the state publishes, and the group home pages never quote a figure the state did not print first.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a group home?

In order: choose the population you will serve, because the license follows the population; confirm which state authority licenses that home type; check local zoning and life safety requirements for the house; form the business and secure the home; write the policies and staffing plan the license requires; apply to the licensing authority and pass the home inspection and life safety survey; then enroll as a Medicaid waiver provider so placements can be funded, and open. Each state page in this guide names the authority, the home types that state licenses, and the application sequence.

What are the requirements to open a group home?

Every state requires its own list, but the pattern is constant: the right license for the population, a home that passes life safety and health inspection, a qualified administrator or house manager, background checks on every staff member, written policies covering care, medication, incidents, and emergencies, and staffing that matches the residents' assessed needs around the clock. Your state page carries the exact list your licensing authority publishes.

How do I get licensed for a group home?

You apply to the authority that licenses homes for your chosen population. In most states that is the department of health, the department of human services, or the developmental disabilities agency, and children's homes usually sit with the child welfare agency. The application, the home study or inspection, and the life safety survey all run through that one authority, and your state page names it.

How long does it take to get a group home license?

Licensing reviews run on the state calendar and take months, not weeks, in most states, because the application review, the background checks, the home inspection, and any corrections all happen in sequence. The honest plan is to carry the home and the build-out costs for the full review period before the first resident arrives. Your state page prints any timeline the state itself publishes.

How much does it cost to open a group home?

The license fee itself is set by the state and is usually the smallest line. The real costs are the home (purchase or lease, accessibility and life safety work), furnishings, insurance, and staffing hired and trained before the first placement. Medicaid waiver rates, which fund most IDD group home placements, are also set by the state. Your state page prints every figure the state publishes, and the free startup cost worksheet linked from this page helps you lay the rest out in order.

What is the difference between a group home and an assisted living facility?

Size and service level. A group home is a small residence, often four to eight beds, serving a defined population such as adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities. An assisted living facility is usually larger, serves mostly older adults, and is licensed for a broader package of personal care and supervision. Some states license the same building under either name depending on size and services, which is why the population and bed count decision comes first.

How do group homes get paid?

Most IDD group home placements are funded by Medicaid home and community based services waivers, the 1915(c) waivers each state runs for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Elderly residential care is funded by a mix of private pay, state plan personal care, and in many states a Medicaid waiver for assisted living or residential services. Licensing makes you eligible to apply; waiver provider enrollment is a separate approval, and your state page separates the two.
