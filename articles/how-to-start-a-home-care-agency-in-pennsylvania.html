How to Start a Home Care Agency in Pennsylvania (The $100 License and the Agency vs Registry Decision)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 28, 2026

The Pennsylvania story usually starts with a confident search and a cheap answer. Someone in Philadelphia, Pittsburgh, or Harrisburg decides to start a non-medical home care agency, finds that the state license fee is $100, and assumes the hard part is over. Then the real question lands, usually from a spouse, a partner, or the application itself: are you an agency or a registry? In Pennsylvania that is not a branding choice. It is the first legal decision in the business, it decides whether your caregivers are your employees or independent contractors you refer, and picking the wrong one means licensing a business you did not mean to build.

That decision is the moment this video was made for, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against a primary source. One thing has changed since the video was recorded, and it changes how you file: Pennsylvania moved home care applications online, and mailed or emailed submissions are no longer accepted. The article follows the current process, not the older one in the video. The short version is this: the license is cheap, the paper is not hard, and the worker files are where Pennsylvania applications live or die.

The demand: an older state that pays for care at home

Start with why Pennsylvania draws so many new owners. The Census Bureau puts Pennsylvania population at 13,059,432 as of July 1, 2025, and 21.4 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 2.79 million seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Pennsylvania puts median household income at $77,971 for 2020 to 2024, which matters because private pay home care is bought out of household income.

The price families face explains the business case. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Pennsylvania, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $77,792, up 3 percent from 2024. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows that Pennsylvania families are already paying for help at home at a level that can support a real agency, if the agency is built right.

Two models, one chapter of regulations

Pennsylvania licenses home care through the Department of Health, Division of Home Health, under 28 Pa. Code Chapter 611. The Department states the scope plainly on its home care page: home care agencies and home care registries provide non-skilled services to individuals in their homes or other independent living environments, and home care services means personal care, assistance with instrumental activities of daily living, companionship services, respite care, and specialized care. Skilled home health is a separate license and a separate lane. Chapter 611 does not apply to a home health care agency, and Medicare certification is a second process, not part of this application.

Chapter 611 defines the two models by who the worker belongs to. A home care agency is an organization that supplies, arranges, or schedules employees to provide home care services. A home care registry is an organization that supplies, arranges, or refers independent contractors to provide those same services. Same service list, different business underneath it. Under the agency model, the workers are your employees: you withhold their taxes, carry workers compensation on them, supervise them, and answer for their work. Under the registry model, the workers are independent contractors referred to consumers, and the consumer notice rules below put that arrangement, and where the tax and insurance duties fall, in front of the family before services start.

Neither model is a loophole, and the choice is not cosmetic. The registry model looks cheaper because the payroll burden moves off your books. What also moves is your control over scheduling, training, and standards, and your fit for the Medicaid and managed care work that expects an employer model. Decide the model before you file, because the license itself will specify whether you are licensed as an agency, a registry, or both, and switching later means licensing again.

The money and the calendar in Chapter 611

The fee is the easy part, so get it off the table. Under section 611.2, no one may operate or hold out a home care agency or registry without a license, each physical location must be separately licensed, and the Department will conduct an inspection before issuing an initial license or a renewal. The application or renewal fee is $100. There is no rebate, refund, or prorating of that fee, a separate application and a separate fee are required for each separately licensed location, and a renewal form is due at least 60 days before the expiration date on the license. Two offices means two licenses, two fees, and two renewal calendars. Chapter 611 also has no certificate of need step: if you meet the standards, you can apply, and no one has to prove the market needs another agency.

Two practical consequences follow from that text. First, submitting the application does not authorize you to start seeing clients. The license and the pre-licensure inspection come first. Second, the calendar discipline starts in year one, not year three. Put the 60 day renewal deadline on the calendar the week the license arrives, along with the annual competency reviews and the 12 month health screening updates covered below. A Pennsylvania license is not a certificate you frame. It is a set of recurring deadlines you either build systems for or eventually miss.

How you file now: online only

Here is where the article parts company with the video, on purpose. The video was recorded when the packet went in by email with the fee mailed separately. The Department of Health has since implemented a new online application process for home care agencies and registries, and its home care page states that mailed or emailed submissions will no longer be accepted effective March 31, 2026. Confirm the current submission route on that page before you prepare anything, and send process questions to the Division of Home Health resource account the page lists. An owner who builds the old packet and mails it to Harrisburg has not applied. That owner has lost weeks.

What the online process still asks for is the substance Chapter 611 has always required: the application specifying agency, registry, or both, the identifying information for the business and its owners, and the forms the updated application carries, which now include references to the relevant regulation for each section. The cheapest timeline move in Pennsylvania is still a complete filing the first time. Most delay I see traces back to the same handful of causes: a missing form, clearances missing for a listed worker, a competency test referenced but never actually built, or a consumer disclosure that does not match the model you applied under. Every correction round restarts the clock on somebody's desk.

The worker file is the application behind the application

Before a direct care worker is hired or rostered, section 611.51 requires at least two satisfactory references, a criminal history report under section 611.52, and a ChildLine verification if applicable under section 611.53. Owners and office staff are not exempt: section 611.52 also requires a criminal history report for each owner and for office staff applicants.

The criminal history rule turns on residency, and this is the detail that stalls files. If the person has been a Pennsylvania resident for the 2 years before the request, the person requests a State Police criminal history record. The Pennsylvania State Police puts an individual criminal history check at $22 on its criminal history background check page. If the person has not been a Pennsylvania resident for those 2 years, the person must obtain a federal criminal history record and a letter of determination from the Department of Aging based on that record. Build proof of residency into the file either way, because the regulation lists the documents that can prove it, from a driver license or state identification to housing, utility, tax, and employment records.

Now the correction that matters for senior care. The ChildLine child abuse clearance is not a blanket requirement for every Pennsylvania home care worker. Under section 611.53, an agency or registry that serves persons under 18 years of age must require a ChildLine verification for its direct care worker, office staff, and owner files. A senior only agency that never serves a minor is in a different position from an agency that takes family cases with children in the home. Know which business you are running, check the section before you order clearances you do not need, and if you do serve anyone under 18, treat the verification as a hard gate, because section 611.53 bars employing or rostering anyone ChildLine has verified as the perpetrator of a founded or indicated report of child abuse.

Health screening is simpler and has no exceptions built into the launch rush. Under section 611.56, each direct care worker, and other office staff or contractors with direct consumer contact, must provide documentation, before consumer contact, of screening for active tuberculosis conducted under CDC guidelines, dated no more than 1 year before the start date, and that documentation must be updated at least every 12 months.

Competency: Pennsylvania makes you prove it, then prove it again every year

Pennsylvania does something many states skip. It makes you prove your workers are competent, not just cleared. Under section 611.55, before a worker is assigned or referred, the worker must qualify through one of these pathways: a valid Pennsylvania nurse license; passing a competency examination developed by your agency or registry; or completing a qualifying training program, which includes a program your agency or registry develops that meets the regulation subject areas, a home health aide training program meeting 42 CFR 484.36, the nurse aide certification and training program sponsored by the Department of Education, or a training program meeting the standards of a Medicaid Waiver or other publicly funded program you participate in.

Read the examination pathway again, because it puts a deliverable on you. If your workers qualify by examination, the examination is yours to build, administer, and document, and the file has to show it. For personal care workers, the examination or training must cover the regulation subject lists, which run from confidentiality, consumer control, infection control, universal precautions, emergencies, documentation, and recognizing and reporting abuse or neglect, to bathing, grooming, dressing, ambulation and transfers, meal preparation and feeding, toileting, and assistance with self-administered medications. Competency is not a one time event either. Section 611.55 requires a documented review of each worker's competency at least once a year, and more often when a quality of care problem shows up.

What the consumer has to be told before the first visit

Section 611.57 is the part of Chapter 611 families feel directly. Before services start, the agency or registry must give the consumer, the legal representative, or a responsible family member an information packet that is easy to read and understand. The packet lists the services that will be provided and the worker who will provide them, the hours, the fees and total costs on an hourly or weekly basis, who to contact at the Department about licensure and compliance, the Department complaint hotline and the local Area Agency on Aging Ombudsman number, the hiring and competency requirements that apply to the worker, and a disclosure of whether the direct care worker is an employee or an independent contractor, with the tax and insurance obligations and other responsibilities that follow from that status for the consumer and for the agency or registry.

That disclosure is why the agency versus registry decision cannot be made casually at the application screen. The model you choose is the model you have to explain to every family, in the packet, before the first visit. The same section gives the consumer the right to be involved in service planning and, with narrow exceptions for nonpayment and worker safety, at least 10 calendar days advance notice before services are terminated. Build the packet as part of the business, not as a form you hunt for after the first client signs.

The Medicaid door is a second door

Getting the license is the middle of the story, not the end. If Medicaid waiver work is part of your plan, that enrollment is separate from this license and runs through the Department of Human Services provider enrollment system, PROMISe, with the Community HealthChoices managed care plans on top of it. The Department of Health license makes you legal to operate. It does not enroll you with a single payer. Sequence it the way Pennsylvania operations actually work: license first, payer enrollment second, clients third, and start the referral conversations while the license processes, because owners who wait open their doors to an empty calendar.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Pennsylvania owner. The risk in this state is rarely the $100 fee. The risk is a worker file that cannot survive inspection: a federal check missing for a worker who moved to Pennsylvania 18 months ago, a competency examination that exists as a sentence in a policy instead of a test in the file, a tuberculosis screening that expired 13 months after hire because nobody owned the renewal date, or a consumer packet whose worker status disclosure describes a registry while the license says agency. Pennsylvania surveys licensed agencies, inspections are part of licensure and renewal, and the state's sample should never be the first sample anyone took of your files. Run your own file audits on a schedule, personnel files against sections 611.51 to 611.56 and client files against section 611.57, and fix what you find while fixing is still cheap.

Your next step

Decide the model first, agency or registry, and write down why, in one page, before you open the online application. Then build the worker file template before you hire anyone: the reference checks, the residency based criminal history route, the ChildLine check if you will serve anyone under 18, the tuberculosis screening dates, and the competency pathway with its annual review date. An owner who does those two things in that order files once, staffs clean, and opens as a business instead of a correction project.
