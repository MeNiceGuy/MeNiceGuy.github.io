How to Start a Home Care Agency in Oregon (Non-Medical Care Needs the In-Home Care Agency License)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Oregon story starts with a founder who had the right license name and the wrong license. She planned personal care with medication help, priced the Limited classification because it was the cheapest line on the schedule, and built her staffing plan around caregivers reminding clients to take their pills. The service she actually described, helping clients take medication, sits one rung up the ladder, at Basic, and the rung above that, administering medication, sits at Intermediate and requires an employed Oregon registered nurse. In Oregon the license names are the trap, because one regulator, the Oregon Health Authority, issues three different home-based licenses that share vocabulary and nothing else, and inside the non-medical license the classification on the license, not the name on the door, decides what staff may do. Founders who settle the classification before anything is filed save themselves a refile. Founders who settle it after the survey pay for the lesson twice. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

The demand: about 897,000 seniors, one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Oregon. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 4,273,586 as of July 1, 2025, and 21.0 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 897,000 seniors, one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Oregon puts median household income at $83,011 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

The demand side is only half the Oregon calculation. The other half is time: the authority's own program material states that the in-home care initial application process is currently taking an average of six to nine months end to end. That figure is a published average, carried here as a planning fact, not a rule deadline. An owner who starts the classification work, the administrator record, and the policy set before filing is an owner whose six to nine months runs against a complete file.

The license question, answered directly: non-medical care needs the in-home care agency license

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Oregon? Yes. Non-medical home care sits on the in-home care agency license issued by the Oregon Health Authority under ORS 443.305 to 443.350 and OAR 333-536, and ORS 443.315(1) is plain: a person may not establish, manage, or operate an in-home care agency without a license from the authority. The license comes in four classifications at OAR 333-536-0007, and the classification is a scope ladder. Limited covers personal care, which may include medication reminding. Basic adds medication assistance. Intermediate adds medication administration and employs an Oregon registered nurse. Comprehensive adds nursing services.

The neighboring licenses are separate doors. Skilled home health sits on the home health agency license under ORS 443.014 to 443.105 and OAR 333-027. Hospice sits on its own license under ORS 443.850 to 443.869 and OAR 333-035. A caregiver registry, which refers caregivers without employing them, holds its own license under ORS 443.100, and the Agency with Choice model for self-directed services holds a license under ORS 443.360. The residential doors, ODDS 24-hour residential settings and group homes, assisted living and residential care facilities, adult foster homes, and the rest, sit in their own license families, and ORS 443.350 carves those families out of the in-home care series entirely. No one license covers another license's services, and no certificate of need sits in front of any home-based door: Oregon certificate of need review at ORS 442.315 reaches hospitals and long term care facilities only.

The non-medical lane: four classifications, four fees, one year at a time

The sequence starts with the classification decision, before anything is filed. Test the service menu against the ladder at OAR 333-536-0007: personal care and medication reminding sit at Limited, medication assistance needs Basic, medication administration needs Intermediate and an employed Oregon registered nurse, and nursing services need Comprehensive. Then form the business, hold the EIN and tax registrations, and put the operating accounts and insurance in place. Submit the initial application through the authority's online licensing portal with the nonrefundable fee for the classification (ORS 443.315(4); OAR 333-536-0010(3)), the administrator record, and the policy and form set the initial checklist requires.

The fee schedule at ORS 443.315(4) is classification priced: initial licensure is $2,000 for Limited, $2,250 for Basic, $2,500 for Intermediate, and $3,000 for Comprehensive, with an additional $1,250 for the initial licensure of each subunit. Renewal is $1,000 for Limited or Basic, $1,250 for Intermediate, and $1,500 for Comprehensive, with an additional $1,000 for each subunit. A change of ownership away from the renewal date is $350, plus $350 for each subunit. The license is valid for one year under ORS 443.315(5), and the authority inspects on site before services are rendered and once every two years after, under ORS 443.315(7). Hold the license before the first client visit.

The skilled lane: the home health agency license at $4,000

A home health agency must be primarily engaged in providing skilled nursing services and at least one other service ORS 443.075 lists, therapy, medical social services, home health aide services, and the other listed items, under an order for treatment and a plan of treatment from a physician, naturopathic physician, physician associate, or nurse practitioner. The agency holds an organized governing body under ORS 443.055, policies set by professional personnel, supervision by a physician, physician associate, nurse practitioner, naturopathic physician, or registered nurse, and clinical and financial records on all patients, under ORS 443.065. OAR 333-027-0001 ties state licensure to the Medicare Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR Part 484. The fee facts sit at ORS 443.035(1): $4,000 for a new home health agency license, $2,125 for renewal, and $1,250 for a change of ownership away from the renewal date. The license runs on a calendar year and expires December 31, with renewal due at least 30 days before expiration under OAR 333-027-0020. One bridge runs from this license back toward personal care: a licensed home health agency that provides personal care services needs no separate in-home care license for that work, under ORS 443.090. No mirror excuse runs the other way.

The hospice lane, and the Medicare layer on the skilled doors

Hospice holds its own license: a person may not establish, conduct, or maintain a hospice program, or hold itself out as one, without a license from the authority, under ORS 443.860(1). The fee to obtain or renew a hospice program license is $1,140 under ORS 443.860(3), the license is valid for one year and is not transferable, and licensure requires an in-person site inspection at least once every three years. A licensed hospice program needs no in-home care agency license to provide palliative care, under ORS 443.867. Keep one classification line straight: a freestanding hospice inpatient facility can also be classified as a special inpatient care facility under ORS chapter 441, a hospital-family door with its own fees, and that classification is not the hospice program license.

Medicare certification layers on the state license for home health and hospice. Enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS to National Government Services, Jurisdiction 6, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for Oregon, with the CY2026 institutional enrollment fee of $750. The A/B MAC is Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction F, and Noridian Jurisdiction D is the DME MAC, a different door. Read the moratorium gate before timing an initial enrollment: the federal Medicare enrollment moratorium (CMS-6101-N2 for home health, CMS-6102-N2 for hospice) gates initial enrollment on the certified tracks only (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027), and it blocks no Oregon state license.

Background checks and the Medicaid doors: screening first, then the contract frame

Build the screening and file systems before the first visit: the criminal records check under ORS 443.004 for every caregiver, the training record ORS 443.011 requires, and the client disclosure statement the rules require before services are rendered. In the agencies I work with, the expensive version of this lesson is services delivered above the classification on the license, and the cheap version is a personnel file that was complete before the first assignment and stayed complete.

Medicaid runs on two desks. The Oregon Health Authority administers Oregon Medicaid as the single state agency, and the Oregon Department of Human Services administers long term services and supports. Most Oregon Health Plan benefits run through coordinated care organizations under OAR 410-141, with fee-for-service Open Card for members outside CCO assignment. Home health services sit at OAR 410-127, and Medicaid home health electronic visit verification runs on the Provider Choice Model at OAR 410-127-0046: the agency provides its own EVV method capturing the federal elements, including start and end time to the minute and geolocation at start and end, with monthly reporting to the authority. The Medicaid door for in-home care agencies is the ODHS contract frame at OAR 411-033, at the Comprehensive classification, and APD in-home services sit at OAR 411-030. No Oregon Medicaid payer enrollment moratorium was located in the primary sources checked for this article, and I print no Medicaid pause for Oregon: the authority at OAR 410-120-1260(14) to impose one exists, and no notice invoking it against these provider types was located. The one Oregon pause that did exist, the ODDS endorsement moratorium of 2025, governed residential endorsements and was lifted effective November 25, 2025.

What you still do today: form the business the Oregon way

Form the business with the Oregon Secretary of State, hold the EIN and tax registrations, and put the operating accounts and insurance in place before the license application goes in. Then do the ordinary employer load in the right order: liability insurance in place before the first client, the policy and form set the initial checklist requires with page references to the rules behind it, caregiver screening and training set up as a system rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that states what the agency does and does not do at the classification it holds, with medication administration and nursing services on the does not do side of the line until the license classification carries them. No Oregon formation fee is printed in the sources checked for this article, so I print none: confirm the current filing fee on the day of filing.

The practical warning

Oregon's traps are figure traps and scope traps. The scope trap is the classification: reminding at Limited, assistance at Basic, administration at Intermediate, nursing services at Comprehensive, and services delivered above the classification on the license are the most expensive scope error in Oregon home-based care. The figure traps are older numbers that still circulate. Older code copies print a flat $1,500 initial fee and a $750 renewal for the in-home care license; the classification schedule in the current statute controls, and the flat figures are never budgeted. Older copies of OAR 333-027-0025 print $1,600 initial and $850 renewal for home health; the statute at ORS 443.035 and the current application control, at $4,000 new and $2,125 renewal. And the $1,500 new caregiver registry fee printed inside ORS 443.035 belongs to the caregiver registry license at ORS 443.100, not to any agency door. Budget from the statute in front of you, not from the summary you found first.
