Caregiver Pay Rates: What Home Care Agencies Actually Pay in 2026

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 15, 2026

The owner had done everything else in the right order. The license application was moving, the policies were drafted, and the first referral source was warm. Then she sat down to make her first caregiver offer and realized she had no idea what number to say. She had a bill rate she believed in, copied from the market around her, but the wage underneath it was a guess. She typed caregiver pay into a search bar, the same search bar her future caregiver was typing into that night, and got job ads, salary sites, and three different answers for the same town. That moment is where pay bands are usually born, out of a competitor's ad and a hope. It is also where staffing problems are born, because a wage guessed that way has no relationship to the labor market the caregiver actually lives in, or to the rate the agency charges. In the records I review, the owners who struggle to keep caregivers rarely have an effort problem. They have a first number problem. In the video below I walk the federal wage ladder for this occupation and the band method I teach owners: find your floor, set your standard band, and build your differentials on top of it, all before the first offer goes out. This article walks that same ladder in story order, with the wage figures re-verified against the current federal data, so you can set a pay band your rate can actually carry.

The ladder: what the federal data says agencies pay

Start with the shape of the workforce, because it explains every number that follows. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics counts home health and personal care aides in one combined occupation, and employers reported about 4.31 million of them on payroll in the May 2025 Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics. It is one of the largest occupations in the country, and the Bureau projects it to grow 18 percent from 2025 to 2035, with about 760,500 openings a year. Sit with what that scale means for you as an employer. Your competition for a caregiver is not only the agency across town. It is every warehouse, hospital, fast food counter, and retail floor in your county, all hiring from a pool the entire economy is drawing on at once.

The national ladder for that occupation in May 2025 ran like this: the 10th percentile at $13.00 an hour, the 25th percentile at $15.35, the median at $17.21 an hour, which is $35,800 a year, the 75th percentile at $18.98, and the 90th percentile at $21.65 an hour. Read that ladder as a staffing map. The bottom quarter of the market pays wages that compete with the lowest wage work in any local economy. The top quarter pays a visible premium. And the distance between the 25th percentile and the 75th percentile is $3.63 an hour. That gap is the whole argument: it is small enough to fit inside a healthy bill rate, and large enough for a caregiver to feel in every paycheck.

One cut inside the data matters more to a non-medical owner than the national figure. The Bureau also publishes the occupation by industry. Aides working in home health care services had a median of $17.42 an hour in May 2025, while aides in services for the elderly and persons with disabilities, the industry where most non-medical home care agencies sit, had a median of $16.73 an hour. If your agency is non-medical, that second figure is the closer mirror. Pricing your pay band off the higher industry number without knowing it is the higher one is one of the quiet ways a first budget goes wrong.

Nobody hires nationally: localize the ladder

The national ladder is a frame, not an offer. The Bureau publishes the same occupation state by state, and the spread is wide. Washington had the highest state median in May 2025 at $22.95 an hour, several dollars above the national median, while the lowest paying states run several dollars below it. Your state profile is the version of the ladder your applicants are standing on. Pull it before you set a single number. Then check it against live evidence: the postings your applicants are actually choosing between this month, in your county, including the employers outside health care. The federal data tells you where the market settled. The postings tell you where it is moving.

The other side of the ledger: the spread your pay comes out of

Now set the wage against what families pay. The CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, released March 2, 2026 from more than 25,000 provider rates gathered between July and November 2025, put the national median rate for a non-medical caregiver at $35 an hour, up 3 percent, which comes to $80,080 a year on the survey 44 hours a week basis. Put the national median wage of $17.21 next to that $35 bill rate and you see the gross spread the entire business runs on, roughly two to one. That spread is not profit. It is the raw material profit is made from. Payroll burden, supervision, travel, scheduling, and overhead all take their shares before anything is left, and every dollar you add to pay comes out of that spread dollar for dollar. That is why pay decisions cannot be made from job postings. They have to be made from your rate, your burden, and your billable hours, in that order.

Run the loaded math once, in full, so the band is a decision instead of a wish. Take the national median wage of $17.21 an hour and load it with an 18 percent payroll burden for employer taxes and workers compensation, the planning figure from my own cost work with owners, and one billable hour of that caregiver's time costs you about $20.31 before supervision, scheduling, travel, or office overhead. Against the $35 national median bill rate, that leaves about $14.69 an hour to carry every overhead dollar and whatever margin you intend to keep. Move the wage to the 75th percentile, $18.98, and the loaded cost rises to about $22.40, and the room shrinks to about $12.60. That is the true size of a top quartile pay decision: real money, about $2.09 more per loaded hour, not the business ending event owners imagine when they freeze wages at the bottom of the ladder and then pay replacement costs all year.

The band method: floor, standard band, differentials

Here is the method I teach, presented as method, built on the published percentiles. Step one, find your floor. Your floor is the highest of three numbers: your state or local minimum wage, the wage your realistic competitors pay, meaning the warehouse and the hospital as much as the other agency, and the 25th percentile of the national ladder, $15.35 an hour in May 2025, adjusted against your state profile. Hiring below that floor is not frugality. It is a decision to staff only with people who could not get hired anywhere else, and your clients will meet them. Step two, set your standard band at the industry median to the 75th percentile for a caregiver who has cleared probation and can work unsupervised. On the national ladder that is $17.21 to $18.98 an hour. That is the band where experienced aides with reliable transportation actually live. Step three, build your differentials on top of the band, and document them where a caregiver can see them: weekend shifts, dementia and high acuity cases, short shifts that cost a caregiver a drive across town, and lead caregiver duties. Differentials are cheaper than across the board raises because they pay the premium only on the hours that are hard to fill, and caregivers read them as fairness, because the hard hours are exactly the ones they were already resenting at flat pay.

Two disciplines before the paycheck prints

First, reviews on a clock. A pay band nobody revisits is a slow resignation letter, because the ladder moves. The national median aide wage has climbed in recent survey cycles, and your band has to be rechecked against the new data at least annually, or your best people get recruited away at the new market rate while you are still paying the old one. Second, count the full cost of the alternative before you call a raise expensive. Replacing one caregiver costs you recruiting, screening, training, and the billable hours nobody covered while the seat sat empty, and it can cost you the client who leaves when her caregiver quits. Run that against a raise in plain arithmetic. A one dollar raise for a caregiver working a thousand hours a year costs you a thousand dollars to keep a person you already trust in a client's home. Pay is not your biggest cost problem. Churn is. Pay is how you buy churn down.

And lay the band out where a caregiver can see the path through it: the starting rung, the post probation rung, the experienced rung, and the differentials, in the offer letter and the handbook. A caregiver who can see the next dollar and what earns it negotiates with her own effort. A caregiver who cannot see it negotiates with a recruiter at the agency across town.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every owner setting a first pay band. Do not set the wage by subtracting what you want to keep from the rate you copied. That order guarantees the wage is whatever is left over, and what is left over is usually below the floor your local market will accept. Set the wage from the ladder first, load it, and then test whether your rate carries it. If it does not, the honest levers are your rate, your overhead, and your service mix, not a quieter wage that your first three hires will fix for you by leaving. The owners who get this right stop asking what is the least they can pay and start asking a better question: what does the caregiver they actually want cost in this market, this year, and does their rate carry her?

Your next step

This week, before you make or renew an offer, pull your state profile from the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics for home health and personal care aides, write down your floor as the highest of your minimum wage, your realistic competitor wage, and the 25th percentile, set your standard band from the median to the 75th percentile, and load every figure at your real payroll burden before you test it against your bill rate. If you want the documents and templates that carry this work into the rest of a launch, the pay band alongside the application, policies, and startup paperwork in filing order, that is the Home Care Startup Core Kit. The data gives you the ladder. Your market, your rate, and your honesty about both decide the rung you stand on.
