How to Start a Companion Care Business: The Hands-Off Line That Decides Your License

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 24, 2026

The call I hear most often in this lane starts the same way. An owner has found her first client, a sharp older woman who lives alone, cooks a little, and mainly needs company, rides, and help keeping the house in order. The daughter who arranged the care lives two states away and is relieved. Then, on the third visit, she adds a small request: could the companion help her mother into the shower, just this once? That one sentence is where companion care businesses get into trouble. The service that was sold sits on one side of a legal line, and the favor sits on the other. This article walks the line the way I walk it in the video: what companion care actually is, what a companion may never do, how three states open the same business three different ways, the federal wage trap that ruins budgets, and the build order that keeps a new owner on the right side of all of it.

What companion care is, in the federal definition

Start with the cleanest definition available. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, companionship services means the provision of fellowship and protection for an elderly person or a person with an illness, injury, or disability who requires assistance in caring for himself or herself, as the U.S. Department of Labor explains in its domestic service guidance and Fact Sheet 79A. Fellowship is the human part: conversation, activities, engagement, getting out of the house. Protection is being present to monitor safety and well-being. Around that core sits the homemaker work families actually schedule: meals cooked, laundry done, a clean kitchen, groceries in the fridge, a ride to an appointment. None of it is medical, and none of it is hands-on personal care. The buyer is usually an adult child arranging help from a distance, or a senior who is steady on her feet and determined to stay in her own home. She does not need hands on help. She needs a person.

The three lanes, and why mixing them is the expensive mistake

In-home care runs in three lanes. Lane one is companion care: non-medical and hands-off, covering presence, conversation, meals, housekeeping, errands, and rides. Lane two is personal care: still non-medical, but hands-on, covering help with the activities of daily living such as bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting, eating, and transfers. The moment your service list includes those tasks, you are in a different legal category in most states. Lane three is home health: skilled medical care such as nursing and therapy under clinical orders, delivered by licensed clinicians. A companion business lives entirely in lane one. In the records I review, nearly every problem in this corner of the industry starts the same way, when a lane one business quietly starts doing lane two work while keeping lane one paperwork, lane one training, and lane one insurance.

The line, in the states own words

Florida states the line in one sentence: a companion may not provide hands-on personal care to a client. The Florida Agency for Health Care Administration says the same thing in its Homemaker and Companion Services guidance: the registration does not allow hands-on personal care such as bathing, feeding, and changing a client. Georgia writes the line as a list. Under Georgia Code section 31-7-300, companion or sitter tasks are transport and escort services, meal preparation and serving, and household tasks essential to cleanliness and safety, and those tasks do not include assistance with bathing, toileting, grooming, shaving, dental care, dressing, and eating. Medication sits on the same line in every state I check: a companion may remind a client that it is time for a medication, and a companion may never administer it, because administering medication is personal care or medical work, not companionship.

Here is the practical warning. Nobody plans to cross the line. A family asks for help with a shower just this once, the companion wants to help, and the business has drifted out of its legal category with no license, no training file, and no insurance position to stand on. What I tell owners is simple: the line is not a suggestion, it is the business model, and every companion you hire has to be able to say no to a kind request and offer the right referral instead.

Three states, three different doors

There is no national companion care license, and the same service list opens three different ways in three states. In Florida, an organization that provides companion or homemaker services and no home health service is exempt from home health licensure but must register with AHCA as a Homemaker and Companion Services provider. AHCA puts the fee at $50.75 for a two year registration, and the people who will have contact with clients go through the background screening the registration requires. In Texas, the door swings the other way: under Texas Health and Safety Code section 142.003(a)(15), explained in HHSC Provider Letter 2023-15, a person or organization providing sitter-companion services or chore or household services that do not involve personal care, health, or health-related services is exempt from the Home and Community Support Services Agency license. The moment personal care enters the service list, that exemption ends and the HCSSA license is required before you operate. In Georgia, there is no shortcut at all: companion or sitter tasks are one of the service categories on a Private Home Care Provider license from the Department of Community Health, under the same statute that defines the tasks, and companions work under a qualified supervisor against a service plan for the client.

Same business, three doors: a registration, an exemption with a hard boundary, and a full license. Your state has a door of its own, and the sequence matters. You find it before you sell your first hour, not after a complaint or a surveyor finds it for you.

The wage trap that ruins first year budgets

One federal rule catches new owners who price from old advice. You will still hear that companion workers are exempt from minimum wage and overtime. For an agency, that has been false since January 1, 2015. The Department of Labor closed the companionship exemption to third party employers, and an agency is a third party employer. The exemption survives only for the individual, family, or household that employs the worker directly, and even there, care counts as companionship only when it is attendant to and in conjunction with fellowship and protection and does not exceed 20 percent of the total hours worked per consumer per workweek. That 20 percent cap is a family rule, not an agency loophole. As an agency owner, you pay at least the federal minimum wage, you pay overtime after 40 hours in a workweek, and you build both into your rate before you quote a family a price.

Price against the current benchmark, not the old one

The video was built on the 2024 survey figure of $33 an hour for homemaker services, so check the current number before you copy it into a rate sheet. The CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey now reports homemaker and home health aide services together as one non-medical caregiver category, at a national median of $35 an hour, up 3 percent, or $80,080 a year on the survey basis of 44 hours a week, drawn from more than 25,000 rates gathered between July and November 2025. A median is a benchmark, not your rate: your state median, your wage, and your shift minimums decide what you charge. The demand underneath the benchmark is real. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects employment of home health and personal care aides to grow 18 percent from 2025 to 2035, adding about 847,300 jobs, with about 760,500 openings projected each year on average, and it puts the May 2025 median pay for those aides at $35,800 a year, $17.21 an hour. That wage figure is the floor your pricing has to respect, because it is what the labor market already pays the people you need to hire.

The build order I give new owners

First, pick your lane and write your service list down, because the list decides your legal category. Second, read your state rule and confirm whether your services need a license, a registration, or fit an exemption, before you operate. Third, form your entity with your state and get an employer identification number from the IRS, which is free. Fourth, put your insurance in place before your first client. The stack I tell owners to price out is general liability, professional liability, a fidelity bond against employee dishonesty, workers compensation, and hired and non-owned auto coverage if your companions will drive clients; that stack is my professional recommendation, not a state requirement list, and your state or your referral partners may ask for more. Fifth, build your paper with the line inside it: a service agreement, a service plan, and a one page scope sheet that every companion signs, listing what they do and what they never do. Sixth, background check every hire the way your state requires. Seventh, hire for temperament, because families are buying trust, and train every companion on the line until they can repeat it back to you. Eighth, price against the current benchmark above and set shift minimums so short shifts still cover travel and supervision.

Use your state pack only after step two tells you which door your state actually has.

Your next step this week

Here is the one actionable next step. Write your exact service list, task by task, then find your state rule for that list and mark which door it opens: license, registration, or exemption. If even one task on your list is hands-on personal care, stop and re-plan in the personal care lane before you market a single hour, because no rate fixes a business that is operating in the wrong category. Owners who can say exactly what their service is, and exactly where it stops, are the ones who stay out of trouble in this lane.
