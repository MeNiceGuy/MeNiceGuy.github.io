How to Start a Home Care Agency in Florida (No License Required for Non-Medical Care)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 24, 2026

Picture the moment it usually happens. You have decided to start a home care agency in Florida. You have some money saved, a name picked out, and a list of families who already told you they need help. Then you open the state website, see the words home health agency license, and your plan slows to a crawl. Applications, fees, surveys, months of waiting. Most people I talk to assume that license is the only door into this business in Florida.

For a large part of the work families actually ask for, it is not. If the services are companionship and homemaking, things like conversation and supervision, meal preparation, laundry, light housekeeping, and rides to appointments, Florida does not license that business at all. It registers it. That one distinction changes the cost, the timeline, and the order you do everything else in. This article walks the registration route the way I walk it in the video below, with the rules checked against the statute and the Agency for Health Care Administration, which everyone in Florida calls AHCA.

The demand is not the hard part

Start with why Florida draws so many new owners. The Census Bureau puts Florida's population at 23,462,518 as of July 1, 2025, and 22.8 percent of those residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 5.35 million seniors, nearly one in four people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Florida puts median household income at $74,568, which matters because private pay home care is bought out of household income, not out of a program budget. The clients are there, and a large share of them can pay for help at home. Demand is not the problem a new Florida owner has to solve. Getting legally open, at the right level of regulation for the services you plan to sell, is.

The rule that changes the plan

The rule lives in section 400.509, Florida Statutes. Read the first subsection closely, because everything else follows from it. A person who provides companion services or homemaker services and does not provide a home health service is exempt from licensure. Exempt from licensure does not mean exempt from the state. The same subsection says that person must register with AHCA, and registration is required before the business operates. AHCA's own guidance is blunt about the reach of that duty: even a single individual working alone, with no staff at all, has to register.

There is one narrow exception worth knowing so you do not trip over it in conversation. An organization under contract with the Agency for Persons with Disabilities that provides companion services only to people with developmental disabilities is exempt from registration under that same subsection. Everyone else offering companion or homemaker services to the public is in the registration system.

What registration actually involves

The application is the Health Care Licensing Application for a Homemaker and Companion Services Provider, AHCA Form 3110-1003, listed on AHCA's applications page, and AHCA accepts applications through its online licensing system. Under Rule 59A-8.025, Florida Administrative Code, an initial application has to name the specific geographic area you will serve, built from counties inside your health service planning district. Pick your counties deliberately. You cannot serve a county that is not on your registration, although AHCA allows you to add counties inside your designated geographic area later at no charge.

The fee is the part that surprises people. Section 400.509 sets the registration fee at $50 per biennium, and AHCA states the amount due as $50.75 for a two year registration. Compare that, in your own planning, with what a full home health agency license costs in time and money, and you see why the registration route is where so many non-medical owners start.

Before you file, the business itself has to exist in the form AHCA expects. A corporation or LLC registers with the Florida Department of State, Division of Corporations, and AHCA notes that fee is separate. You do not have to form an entity, though. AHCA confirms a sole proprietor can register under their legal name. One naming rule catches new owners off guard: the name of a homemaker and companion business cannot include words like home health, personal care, nursing, medical, or health care, or anything else that implies services beyond what this registration allows. If your legal entity name contains one of those words, expect to register a fictitious name that does not. And once you are registered, your registration number has to appear in your advertising. Section 400.509 requires it in ads to the public, and AHCA extends the same expectation to your website and printed materials.

The timeline, in the state's own numbers

Owners ask me how long Florida registration takes, and the honest answer is that the clock is written into the process. AHCA says it reviews an application and, within 30 days after receipt, notifies the applicant in writing of any apparent errors or omissions. If you get an omissions letter, you have 21 days from receipt to send the missing information, and if it does not arrive, the application is withdrawn and the fee is not refunded. Within 60 days after AHCA receives a complete application, it must approve or deny it. So the timeline is mostly in your hands. A complete first submission moves on the 60 day decision clock. A sloppy one spends its first month collecting an omissions letter instead.

The registration itself is valid for no more than two years. Renewal applications are due at least 60 days before the registration expires, there is a late fee of $25.38 when that 60 day mark is missed, and AHCA is clear about the consequence of letting it lapse entirely: an expired registration cannot be renewed, you apply again as an initial applicant, and you must stop operating until the new registration is issued. Put the renewal date on your calendar the day the certificate arrives, not the month it expires.

The line you cannot cross

Here is the practical warning, and it is the one I give every Florida owner who takes this route. The registration covers companionship and homemaking. It does not cover hands-on personal care. AHCA answers this question directly: a homemaker and companion registration does not allow personal care services such as bathing, feeding, and changing a client. The moment a family's request moves from keeping Mom company and keeping the house running to physically helping her bathe, dress, or transfer, you are outside your registration. Hands-on personal care belongs under a home health agency license, or with caregivers referred through a licensed nurse registry. Agencies get in trouble in Florida not because the registration was hard to get, but because the work quietly crept past what the registration covers. Write your service list to match your registration, train your caregivers on where the line sits, and turn down or refer out work that crosses it.

Screening and the unglamorous parts

Registration is cheaper and faster than licensure, but it is not a formality. AHCA requires Level 2 background screening, the fingerprint based screening, for the managing employee and the financial officer before registration, and registered providers must have Level 2 screening completed for every new homemaker and companion before hiring. Screening results age out: if an employee has not been screened within the last five years, expect rescreening at renewal. Section 400.509 also requires you to obtain the employment or contract history of everyone who will have contact with clients in their homes, and to verify that history unless diligent efforts make verification impossible. Keep those files from day one. They are the first thing a complaint driven inspection will ask about, and AHCA can inspect a registered provider on an unannounced basis during the week when a complaint comes in.

Two more facts that shape the business plan. First, you can operate from home. AHCA allows it, as long as the home is in Florida and inside your service area, with the understanding that your business name, street address, and phone number will appear publicly on Florida Health Finder. Second, insurance. Current Florida law does not require liability insurance for this registration, and AHCA says so plainly. My professional judgment is that you carry it anyway. Families and referral sources vet registered providers the same way they vet licensed ones, and the first serious incident without coverage can end a young agency. Not required is not the same as not needed.

How much does a home care license cost in Florida?

The home health agency license fee is $1,705. AHCA also charges a $300 biennial assessment, charged for initial, renewal, change of ownership, and addition of skilled care services. All AHCA fees are nonrefundable. A change during licensure or a replacement license certificate is $25. State, county, and municipal governments are exempt from license fees. Do I need a license for non-medical home care in Florida?

It depends on the services. Hands-on personal care by your employees routes to the Home Health Agency license. Companion and homemaker services only route to the Homemaker and Companion Services registration, which is a registration, not the agency license. Your next step

If Florida non-medical care is your plan, do these three things this week, in this order. First, settle the business form and the name, entity or sole proprietor, and check the name against AHCA's prohibited words before you print anything. Second, start the Level 2 background screening for yourself as managing employee, because screening runs on its own clock and the application cannot finish without it. Third, file Form 3110-1003 complete the first time, with your counties chosen and your fee enclosed, so your application lands on the 60 day decision clock instead of in the omissions loop.
