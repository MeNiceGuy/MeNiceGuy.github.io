How to Start a Home Care Agency in Alaska (No License for Private Pay Non-Medical Care, Certification for Medicaid)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Alaska story starts with a founder in Anchorage who had the clients before she had the paperwork sorted. Families were calling, caregivers were available, and she kept asking the same question in different forms: which license do I file for first? The honest answer surprised her, because for the work she planned to sell, private pay personal care in client homes, Alaska has no license to file for. What Alaska has instead is a split map, held by different units, and the doors are easy to merge by mistake. I work with owners who lose months merging them. Here is the map, door by door, in the order a founder actually meets it.

Three sentences carry the whole state. Non-medical home care has no Alaska license; the Medicaid door for that work is provider certification, not licensure. Skilled care in the home is a licensed home health agency. Hospice is its own license, in three types. An owner who keeps those sentences separate sequences Alaska correctly, and an owner who blends them budgets the wrong fees on the wrong door.

The demand: about 116,000 seniors in a small, spread-out state

Start with why owners keep looking at Alaska. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 737,270 as of July 1, 2025, and 15.8 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 116,000 seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Alaska puts median household income at $92,788 for 2020 to 2024, one of the stronger income bases in this series, which is the base a private pay agency sells into. That senior count is the population figure multiplied by the age 65 and over share, labeled as that math because the Census page publishes the two inputs, not the product.

The Alaska caution sits inside the same numbers. The senior count is small next to the lower 48 states in this series, and the population is spread across distances that turn travel time into a real cost line. The founders who do well here price the service area honestly, hold a tight map at the start, and let the certification and license clocks, not optimism, set the opening date.

The license question, answered directly: certification for Medicaid personal care, a license for home health and hospice

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Alaska? For private pay care, no state license exists to get. Alaska licenses the entities listed at AS 47.32.010(b), and non-medical home care agencies are not on that list. A private pay agency holds a State of Alaska business license and its own operating record. The moment the agency wants Medicaid payment for personal care, the door changes: Senior and Disabilities Services (SDS) provider certification under 7 AAC 125.060, with Community First Choice personal care under 7 AAC 127.050, plus Alaska Medicaid enrollment under 7 AAC 105.210.

The licensed doors sit with a different unit. Skilled care in the home is a home health agency license under AS 47.32 and 7 AAC 12.500 to 12.590, issued by Health Facility Licensing and Certification (HFLC). Hospice is a hospice agency license under AS 47.32 and 7 AAC 12.310 to 12.349. Residential models sit on their own doors again: assisted living homes under AS 47.33 are the building a client lives in, at $25 for one or two residents and $25 per resident for three or more, and that license authorizes no in-home work. No one door covers another door's services.

The non-medical lane: SDS certification is the Medicaid door, and it starts with training

Personal care services are help with activities of daily living such as mobility, transfers, dressing, eating, and toileting, plus household tasks. Under the SDS Personal Care Services Conditions of Participation, an agency-based program retains a supervising registered nurse, and personal care assistants work to the service plan the program sets. No practitioner order is required on this door, and no skilled task sits on it. The sequence has a step founders miss: the program administrator must attend the department training course before the agency applies for certification, and a new provider receives a one-year probationary certification on the first certification.

Certification and Alaska Medicaid enrollment are separate records, and both must be active before the first billable visit. Enrollment runs under 7 AAC 105.210 through the Alaska Medicaid Health Enterprise portal. The fee fact for this door is plain: NO FEE PUBLISHED. No fee figure for Personal Care Services or Community First Choice provider certification was located in the SDS materials checked on November 17, 2026. The trap runs the other way: the home health agency fee tiers at 7 AAC 12.615, $1,000 to $3,000, belong to the HFLC home health license. They are not the certification fee and are never budgeted on this track. Medicaid personal care visits are recorded through the state Therap EVV system at no cost, or an approved third-party system feeding the Therap aggregator, under the open choice model Alaska runs.

The skilled lane: the home health agency license, tiered by staffing size

Your service plan on this lane is skilled nursing as the baseline service, plus at least one additional service: physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, or home health aide services. AS 47.32.020 bars operating a home health agency without a license, and the standards sit at 7 AAC 12.500 to 12.590. Name the people the rule names before filing: an administrator who meets 7 AAC 12.510, and a physician or registered nurse as director of clinical services under 7 AAC 12.516. Home health aides are certified nurse aides with the competency evaluation at 7 AAC 12.519. HFLC reviews an electronic copy of all policies and procedures with the application, so the policy build comes before the filing, not after it.

The fee facts at 7 AAC 12.615, as the HFLC home health page prints them: the provisional fee is $1,000 at 5 or fewer full-time equivalents, $1,500 at 6 to 15, $2,000 at 16 to 50, and $3,000 at 51 or more, plus $500 per branch or subunit. The biennial renewal fee is $2,000, $3,000, $4,000, and $6,000 on the same tiers, plus $1,000 per branch or subunit. Relocation carries a $150 fee and a miscellaneous modification $250. The full license is biennial, running July 1 of an even-numbered year to June 30 two years later under 7 AAC 12.610(d), renewal filings are due before April 1 of even-numbered years, and renewal applications are due at least 90 days before expiration. Two checked negatives belong in the startup file: no certificate of need gate reaches this license, because home health agencies sit outside the definition at AS 18.07.111(8), and no Alaska Medicaid enrollment moratorium for this type was located in the sources checked.

The certified and hospice lanes: Medicare is a layer, and Medicaid home health waits on it

Medicare certification is a federal layer on the Alaska home health license, never a substitute for it. Enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS with the federal enrollment fee of $750 for 2026, to Wellpoint Federal, Jurisdiction 6, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for Alaska, formerly National Government Services before the name changed on April 1, 2026. The A/B MAC is Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction F, and Noridian Jurisdiction D is the DME desk, a different door. The federal gate: CMS-6101-N2 blocks initial Medicare enrollment of home health agencies, effective November 12, 2026, with no end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). The Alaska license door stays open while the federal filing waits, and the state work proceeds on the state clock.

Hold the payer consequence, because it is the single most expensive sequencing error in Alaska home-based care: Alaska Medicaid enrollment as a home health agency requires Medicare certification. A state license alone does not open Medicaid home health billing, so an agency planning Medicaid home health budgets on the certified track clock. Hospice is cleaner. HFLC licenses three types, full-service, volunteer, and inpatient, and Medicare certification is not required for the state license. Full-service hospice pays a $250 provisional fee and $500 biennial renewal, plus $100 provisional and $200 renewal per additional branch or subunit. Volunteer hospice pays $100 provisional and $200 renewal. An inpatient hospice facility pays $250 plus $75 per bed provisional, and $500 plus $150 per bed renewal. Modification fees are $250 each. Initial Medicare hospice enrollment is gated by CMS-6102-N2 on the same federal terms, and that gate reaches the federal layer only.

Background checks: the Background Check Program covers every worker in a client home

Every worker in a client home carries a Background Check Program check under AS 47.05.300 to 47.05.390 and 7 AAC 10.900 to 10.990, on the non-medical door and the licensed doors alike. Complete the checks for all staff before the onsite inspection on the licensed tracks, and before the first visit on the certification track. Build the rest of the personnel file to the same standard the survey tests: qualifications for the role held, orientation and training records with dates, competency evaluation for the services assigned, and the supervision record the program sets.

I give private pay founders the same advice I give certified agencies, and I label it as my professional judgment: run the check anyway, on the same before-assignment rhythm, even where no payer door requires it yet. In a state with no license for this door, your screening file, your training records, your insurance certificate, and your policies are the proof of seriousness that a license provides automatically in other states.

What you still do today: form the business the Alaska way

Form the business and take the State of Alaska business license, with the EIN, tax accounts, workers compensation coverage, and insurance in place, and add city or borough business registration where the locality requires one. Then do the ordinary employer load in the right order: written policies and procedures covering the services you will actually sell, the Background Check Program screening set up as a system rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do. Decide the payer mix on day one, because private pay only needs no state door beyond the business license, while Medicaid personal care planned means the SDS certification build, starting with the administrator training, starts now. I print no Alaska formation fee here because no formation fee figure sits in the primary sources this article is built from.

The practical warning

Three Alaska traps cost new owners money. The first is buying a license application for a door that has none: anyone selling an Alaska non-medical home care license is selling the wrong door, because AS 47.32.010(b) does not list this work. The second is merging certification with licensure: SDS certifies personal care and waiver providers, HFLC licenses facilities, and filings that merge the two stall. The third is importing a certificate of need step from another state plan: the definition at AS 18.07.111(8) does not reach home health agencies, hospices, or assisted living homes, so no certificate of need step belongs in a home-based filing, while the certificate of need gates that do exist on the nursing facility, ICF/IID, and residential psychiatric treatment center tracks, at the $1,000,000 threshold in AS 18.07.031, stay exactly where they are.
