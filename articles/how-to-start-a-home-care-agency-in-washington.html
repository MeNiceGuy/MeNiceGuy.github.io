How to Start a Home Care Agency in Washington (The $5,000 License and the Class That Comes First)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 29, 2026

The Washington story usually starts with the rate. Someone in Seattle, Tacoma, or Spokane runs the numbers, sees that families in Washington pay some of the highest home care prices in the country, and decides this is the state to build in. Then the license fee lands: $5,000, up front, for a twelve month initial license, before the first client, the first caregiver, or the first dollar of revenue. Half the people I talk to stall right there. The other half make a worse mistake. They pay the fee, file the application, and find out the Department of Health will not even process it, because they skipped a free four hour class that has to come first.

That sequence is the moment this video was made for, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against a primary source. Two numbers have moved since the video was recorded, and both moved in the applicant's favor on paper and against the applicant in cost. The CareScout median for Washington care went up again, and the fee schedule in the Washington Administrative Code was amended effective June 1, 2026. The article follows the current figures, not the video era ones. The short version is this: Washington is a premium market with a real gate in front of it, and the gate rewards owners who file in the right order.

The demand: 1.46 million seniors in a high income state

Start with why Washington draws premium operators. The Census Bureau puts Washington population at 8,001,020 as of July 1, 2025, and 18.3 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.46 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Washington puts median household income at $98,141 for 2020 to 2024, which matters because private pay home care is bought out of household income, and Washington households sit near the top of the national table.

The price families face explains the business case, and it has gone up since the video. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Washington, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $102,960, up 6 percent from 2024 and ranked second highest in the country. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $45 an hour. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows that Washington families are already paying for help at home at a level that can carry a properly run agency, if the agency clears the license and staffs to the standard that price implies.

The license is not optional, and advertising counts

Washington licenses home care as one service category of an in-home services agency, alongside home health and hospice, through the Department of Health. The statute is direct. Under RCW 70.127.020, a license is required for a person to advertise, operate, manage, conduct, open, or maintain an in-home services agency. Read that list again, because the first word does real work: advertising an unlicensed agency is itself a violation, not just serving clients. A person who violates the section is guilty of a misdemeanor, and each day of a continuing violation is a separate violation. There is no soft launch in Washington, no taking deposits or building a waitlist in public while the application sits in Olympia.

The application requirements sit in RCW 70.127.080: a written application on the department form, evidence of professional and public liability and property damage insurance, documentation of the organizational structure and the people who own and run the agency, a description of the service area and of how the agency will provide management and supervision throughout it, a list of the services provided directly and under contract, the license fee, and criminal background checks that comply with RCW 43.43.830 through 43.43.842. The Department of Health turns that statute into a filing checklist, and the checklist has a first item most applicants do not expect.

Gate one: the orientation class, before anything else

Under WAC 246-335-320(1), anyone who wants to apply for an in-home services license must first complete a Department of Health sponsored orientation class. The department states the consequence plainly on its home care orientation class page: initial applications submitted without an orientation class certificate of completion will not be processed. The class explains the licensing process, the differences between the home care, home health, and hospice service categories, and what the department expects of licensees caring for vulnerable people.

The practical details make this the easiest gate in the whole process, if you take it first. The class is online, runs about four hours, and is free of charge. Each session is limited to 80 participants, the certificate is sent within two business days after the class, and the certificate stays valid for one year. The remaining 2026 dates on the department schedule are November 4 and December 9. An owner who registers for the November class can be filing inside the certificate window with the whole next year to work in. An owner who builds the packet first and discovers the class later has built the packet on a clock that has not started.

The fee: $5,000 a category, plus a county decision

The fee schedule lives in WAC 246-335-990, as amended effective June 1, 2026. An applicant submits an initial twelve month license fee of $5,000 for each service category, home care, home health, or hospice, if the applicant is not already licensed in that category. A home care only agency pays one $5,000 fee. An applicant who wants home care and home health on day one pays for two categories, which is one reason the category decision belongs at the start of the plan, not at the application screen.

The second money decision hides in the county surcharge. One county of operation carries no surcharge. Each additional county costs $500 if it is an urban county and $100 if it is a rural county. That sounds small next to $5,000, and it is, but it forces an honest service area decision before you file. The application also asks how you will provide management and supervision across every area you claim. An owner who claims six counties to look big on a map has bought surcharges, a supervision description that has to be true, and a survey conversation about coverage. Claim the ground you can actually supervise, and expand by amendment when the census supports it.

Renewal runs on a different meter. The renewal license covers 24 months, and for home care the standard fee is set by full time equivalents: $2,400 at 5 or fewer FTEs, $2,800 at 6 to 15, $3,000 at 16 to 50, $3,500 at 51 to 100, and $3,700 at 101 or more, with lower Table 2 fees for agencies that meet the rule conditions, including home care agencies under contract with and monitored by the Department of Social and Health Services or an Area Agency on Aging. The department assumes a minimum of 2 FTEs for each approved service area per service category. A late renewal carries an added fee of $50 a day, capped at $1,000. Your staffing plan is part of your license cost in Washington in a way it is not in most states, so the FTE worksheet in the packet is a financial document, not a formality.

The packet: dated documents go last

The department lists what a home care agency applicant submits on its home care license requirements page: the orientation certificate, the in-home services application with the initial fee, proof of current commercial general liability insurance, disclosure statements for the on-site administrator and the supervisor of direct care services, criminal history background checks processed through the Washington State Patrol for those same two people, copies of the government issued business licenses for each office location, a completed full-time equivalent worksheet, a description of the organizational structure, a description of how the agency will manage and supervise services throughout the requested service areas, and a description of the services offered directly and under contract.

Two items on that list carry dates, and the dates are where Washington packets die. The disclosure statements and the State Patrol background checks for the administrator and the supervisor must be dated within three months of the application date. Pull them in month one of a four month packet build and they expire before you file, and you pay and wait to run them again. Sequence the build the way the rule is written: entity, insurance, policies, organizational and supervision descriptions first, the dated disclosures and checks last, as close to the filing date as the process allows. After the department reviews and accepts the materials, its Office of Investigation and Inspection contacts the applicant to schedule the initial survey, and the license issues only after the survey process is passed.

One more clock runs underneath the whole filing. The department warns on that same requirements page that, under WAC 246-335-320(4), it may close out an application, with no refund of licensing fees, if the applicant has not completed every step required for licensure within nine months. The $5,000 is not a placeholder you can park. File when the packet is ready to finish, not when you are ready to start it.

The caregivers have a credential clock too

Licensing the agency is half the compliance build. The people you send into homes carry their own Washington credential. The department explains on its home care aide frequently asked questions page that long term care workers, including home care agency caregivers, must be certified as home care aides within 200 days of the date of hire, unless an exemption applies. A new caregiver may begin working after completing two hours of orientation and three hours of safety training approved by the Department of Social and Health Services, must complete 75 hours of approved training within 120 days of hire, and must hold the certification by day 200 or stop working. In a premium market that is not paperwork trivia. Your hiring plan, your training vendor, and your scheduling system all have to track three dates per caregiver from the first shift.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Washington owner. The risk in this state is rarely the $5,000 fee, because the fee is visible. The risk is sequence: an application filed without the orientation certificate that the department will not process, background checks that went stale inside their three month window, a certificate that lapses because the packet took longer than its one year of validity, or a filing that hits the nine month close out with the fee gone. Every one of those failures is a calendar failure, not a competence failure. Put the four dates on one page before you spend anything: class date, certificate expiry, the three month window for the dated documents, and the nine month completion deadline that starts when the department receives the application.

Your next step

Register for the next orientation class first, the November 4 session if a seat is open, and build the packet while your certificate is fresh. Decide the service category and the county list before you pay anything, run the renewal math on the FTE table so the second license period is already in the budget, and pull the administrator and supervisor disclosures and State Patrol checks last. An owner who files in that order spends the $5,000 once, on a license that actually issues.
