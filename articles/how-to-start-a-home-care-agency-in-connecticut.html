How to Start a Home Care Agency in Connecticut (Registration for Non-Medical Care, a License for Home Health)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Connecticut story starts with a founder who walked into the wrong department, politely and completely prepared. She had a policy binder, a staffing plan, and a fee budget, all addressed to the Department of Public Health, for a non-medical agency that department does not touch. Connecticut splits home-based care across two regulators and three credentials, and the split is easy to merge by mistake. I tell owners in this state to name the regulator before they name the service, because the regulator decides the form, the fee, and the clock. Here is the full picture, door by door, in the order a founder actually meets it.

Hold the headline first. Non-medical care in Connecticut is registered, not licensed, and the register sits with consumer protection, not public health. Skilled care is licensed, by public health. Hospice at home is not a standalone filing at all; it is an addition to the skilled license. An owner who sorts those three sentences before spending sorts Connecticut correctly.

The demand: about 752,000 seniors, more than one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Connecticut. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 3,688,496 as of July 1, 2025, and 20.4 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 752,000 seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Connecticut puts median household income at $95,781 for 2020 to 2024, the strongest income base in this batch of states, which is the base a private pay agency sells into. That senior count is the population figure multiplied by the age 65 and over share, labeled as that math because the Census page publishes the two inputs, not the product.

The payer side is simpler here than in managed care states, and founders still get it wrong by importing another state map. Connecticut Medicaid, HUSKY Health, pays home and community-based services fee-for-service through the Connecticut Medical Assistance Program (CMAP). There is no capitated managed care plan between the agency and the Department of Social Services for these services. Community Health Network of Connecticut is the medical administrative services organization, and enrollment and claims run through the DSS fiscal agent portal.

The license question, answered directly: registration with Consumer Protection for non-medical care

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Connecticut? You need a credential, and the credential is a certificate of registration as a homemaker-companion agency from the Department of Consumer Protection (DCP) under sections 20-670 to 20-682 of the General Statutes. Section 20-671 bars operating without the certificate. The Department of Public Health (DPH) does not license homemaker-companion agencies, and DPH prints that exclusion on its own Home Health Unit page. A homemaker-companion agency is an organization that employs one or more persons to provide companion services or homemaker services, or a registry that supplies or refers those workers, under section 20-670. Registries are inside the rule, not outside it.

The licensed doors sit beside the registration. Skilled home health sits on the home health care agency license under section 19a-490(d), with operating standards at sections 19-13-D66 to 19-13-D79 of the Regulations of Connecticut State Agencies. Supportive services only, under registered nurse supervision, sit on the separate home health aide agency license under section 19a-490(e) and sections 19-13-D80 to 19-13-D92. Hospice runs as an addition to the home health license for services in the home, and as its own facility license for inpatient beds. Residential doors carry their own credentials in the Connecticut packet, including DDS community living arrangement group homes under section 17a-227 at a $50 application fee, assisted living services agencies at $500 per site, and residential care homes at $565 per site on the triennial cycle. No one credential covers another credential's services.

The non-medical lane: the homemaker-companion registration, step by step

Step one is confirming the service menu is non-skilled: companion supervision, homemaker help, and hands-on personal care only, with no nursing, no therapy, and no service that requires registered nurse supervision. Homemaker services include help with personal hygiene, so hands-on personal care sits on this registration when no skilled service is delivered. That line is the whole credential decision on this track. Step two is forming the business with the Connecticut Secretary of the State, holding the EIN and tax registrations, and putting the operating accounts and insurance in place.

Step three is securing the surety bond or insurance policy of not less than $10,000 covering theft by an employee, under section 20-672(a)(3), and running the state and national criminal history records checks under section 29-17a for the principals. Step four is filing the registration application with DCP with the fee at section 20-672(b), $375, and holding the certificate before the first client, under sections 20-671 and 20-673. DCP issues or refuses the certificate on a completed application and fee; the statute prints no inspection or policy review before registration. Step five is building the worker file system before the first visit: the comprehensive background check materials for every worker, kept and produced for DCP on request, plus competency and orientation records. Step six is calendaring the renewal: every registration expires on October 31, and renewal is $375 each year. For Medicaid work, enroll with DSS in CMAP service by service, starting with the Connecticut Home Care Program for Elders under section 17b-342, where access agencies assess clients and build the care plans the agency delivers against, and configure EVV through Sandata or a certified alternate before the first billed visit.

The skilled lane: the home health care agency license at $300, with no certificate of need

A home health care agency provides professional nursing services and at least one of home health aide services, physical therapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy, or medical social services, available twenty-four hours per day, in the patient home, accepting new patients seven days a week. The agency provides nursing and at least one additional service directly, and the rest directly or through contract. Section 19a-491(a) bars operating an institution without a DPH license. Form the business, settle the service area, the governing body, the administrator, and the clinical leadership the standards require, and build the licensure file: the policy register, the personnel file system, the plan of care process, and the around-the-clock availability the definition requires.

File the DPH application with the fee at section 19a-491(c)(12), $300 per agency for the biennial licensing and inspection cycle, plus $100 for each satellite patient service office under section 19a-491(c)(13). No certificate of need stands in front of this license: section 19a-638(b)(6) exempts home health agencies, and subdivision (7) exempts hospice programs. A Connecticut filing that budgets a certificate of need review budgets a step the statute removes. The certificate of need program itself transferred to DPH effective July 1, 2026, under Public Act 26-68, which changes the address for facility filings, not the exemption for this one. Complete the licensure survey, correct deficiencies through the plan of correction process, and hold the license before operating. The aide agency variant sits here so it is never silently absent: the satellite office fee reaches aide agencies at $100 per satellite office, and no per agency fee figure for an aide agency appears in the schedule at section 19a-491(c) or (d), so the packet prints none for that license.

The certified and hospice lanes: license first, addition second

Medicare certification layers on the licensed agency, and a certified agency then sits on the triennial cycle: $300 under section 19a-491(d)(3), plus $100 per satellite office under (d)(4). Enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS to National Government Services, Jurisdiction K, with the federal enrollment fee of $750 for 2026. One contractor holds both Medicare desks for Connecticut, the A/B desk and the Home Health and Hospice desk, and guides that route a Connecticut enrollment to a second contractor are working from another state map. Read the gate before timing the filing: initial enrollment is blocked under CMS-6101-N2, effective November 12, 2026, with no end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). The state license sequence runs on its own clock while the federal door is gated.

Hospice sequencing is the Connecticut trap in reverse: there is no standalone outpatient hospice application to file first. DPH requires licensure as a home health care agency before hospice services are added to that same license, and the agency then seeks Medicare hospice certification through an accrediting organization under 42 CFR Part 418, gated for initial enrollment by CMS-6102-N2. No separate fee figure is published for adding outpatient hospice services to the license. For inpatient beds, a freestanding hospice or a dedicated unit holds a hospice inpatient facility license at $440 per site plus $5 per bed, biennial, under section 19a-491(c)(17) and (18). The related hospital figures, $940 per site and $7.50 per bed at subdivisions (15) and (16), price a short-term hospital special hospice, not a freestanding program, and borrowing them overstates the filing by more than double.

Background checks: records checks for principals, a full file for every worker

The registration application carries state and national criminal history records checks under section 29-17a for the principals, and the agency keeps the comprehensive background check materials for every worker and produces them when DCP asks. Build the worker file before the first visit: background check materials, competency records, and orientation records for each person who will enter a client home. On the licensed doors, the personnel file system is part of the licensure file itself, tested at survey alongside the plan of care process.

EVV discipline belongs in the same operating file. DSS runs an open vendor model: the state provided Sandata system, Sandata Agency Management, also called Santrax, or an alternate system that has completed Sandata testing and certification, with visits flowing through the Sandata Aggregator. Provider Bulletin 2024-15 carries the open model for personal care services, alternate system data has been accepted since May 8, 2024, and home health claim edits apply to dates of service from July 1, 2024. A claim for an EVV mandated service without a matching confirmed visit is denied, so the visit record, the care plan, and the claim must agree from the first billed visit.

What you still do today: form the business the Connecticut way

Form the business with the Connecticut Secretary of the State, get the EIN from the IRS, hold the tax registrations, and put liability insurance in place before the first client. Then do the ordinary employer load in the right order: written policies and procedures covering the services you will actually sell, background screening set up as a system rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do, with skilled tasks on the does not do side of the line for a registered agency. Calendar the October 31 registration expiry on day one, because a registration approved within ninety days of the expiration date moves to the next renewal cycle on the DCP instructions, and a missed renewal is an unregistered agency. I print no Connecticut formation fee here because no formation fee figure sits in the primary sources this article is built from.

The practical warning

Three Connecticut traps cost new owners money. The first is the wrong department: a file that looks for a DPH home care license for non-medical care is looking at the wrong regulator, because homemaker-companion agencies register with DCP and DPH prints that it does not license them. The second is the $10,000 figure read as a fee: section 20-672(a)(3) sets a minimum amount of surety or insurance cover against theft by an employee, and the registration fee is $375. Coverage is not a fee, in this state or any other. The third is sequence: hospice runs license first, addition second, and a program plan that starts with a standalone hospice application starts in the wrong order, while a home health budget that adds a certificate of need review spends money on a step section 19a-638(b) removes.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a home care agency in Connecticut?

You start with the credential that matches the service menu. Non-medical care files the homemaker-companion agency registration with the Department of Consumer Protection under sections 20-670 to 20-682. Skilled care files the DPH home health care agency license, supportive services only file the home health aide agency license, and hospice at home is added to the home health license after licensure. What are the Connecticut home care license requirements?

Connecticut home-based care runs on two regulators. Non-medical care holds a DCP certificate of registration as a homemaker-companion agency (sections 20-670 to 20-682); DPH does not license these agencies. Home health care agencies are licensed by DPH under section 19a-490(d) and sections 19-13-D66 to 19-13-D79. Home health aide agencies hold a separate DPH license under section 19a-490(e). Hospice in the home is an addition to the home health license, and inpatient hospice holds its own facility license. No certificate of need is required for home health agencies or hospice programs under section 19a-638(b)(6) and (7).

How much does a home care license cost in Connecticut?

The homemaker-companion registration is $375 with the application and $375 at each annual renewal, expiring every October 31, and the $10,000 bond or insurance figure is a coverage minimum, not a fee. The home health care agency license is $300 per agency plus $100 per satellite office, biennial, or $300 triennial for a certified agency. A hospice inpatient facility pays $440 per site plus $5 per bed. No Connecticut fee is published for CMAP enrollment on the non-medical track, so that layer is NO FEE PUBLISHED. Do I need a license for non-medical home care in Connecticut?

You need the DCP certificate of registration, not a DPH license. Section 20-671 bars operating a homemaker-companion agency without registration, and the registration covers companion services, homemaker services including help with personal hygiene, and registries that supply or refer workers. A service that requires registered nurse supervision crosses into home health aide agency licensure, and nursing or therapy crosses into home health care agency licensure.
