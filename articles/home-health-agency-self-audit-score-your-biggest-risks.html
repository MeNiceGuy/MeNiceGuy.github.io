Home Health Agency Self-Audit: Score Your Biggest Operational Risks in Five Minutes

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 28, 2026

Most agencies find out they have an operations problem when it is already expensive. A star rating slips on Care Compare and a referral source quietly sends the next case somewhere else. Readmissions climb for a quarter before anyone adds them up. Caregivers start leaving in a bunch, and by the time the pattern is obvious, the schedule has holes in it and the office is hiring in a panic. In the records I review, the numbers almost always knew first. The owner just had not lined them up in one place and looked at them together. That is the whole idea behind the free self-audit I built: eight questions about your own numbers, answered honestly, turned into one risk score and a short list of what to fix first.

Two tracks, because the two businesses run on different numbers

The audit opens with a choice, and the choice matters. A Medicare certified home health agency lives on clinical quality measures: its Quality of Patient Care star rating, its hospital readmission rate, its patient satisfaction scores, and how reliably its OASIS assessments go in on time. A non-medical home care agency lives on staffing and delivery numbers instead: caregiver turnover, unfilled shifts, EVV and documentation exceptions, and how much of the authorized care actually gets delivered. Score a home care agency on OASIS timeliness and you measure a world it does not live in. Score a certified agency only on turnover and you miss the measures its referral sources and surveyors watch. So the audit runs two tracks, and each track scores four inputs that belong to that business. The other four questions on each track shape your recommendations. They never change your score, and the tool says so on its face.

The certified track, and the public facts underneath it

On the certified track, the scored inputs start with the number the public sees. CMS publishes two kinds of home health star ratings on Care Compare: the Quality of Patient Care Star Rating, built from OASIS assessments and Medicare claims data, and the Patient Survey Star Rating, built from the Home Health CAHPS survey. CMS updates both quarterly. To receive a Quality of Patient Care rating at all, an agency needs reported data on at least five of the seven measures in the calculation, with at least 20 complete quality episodes or home health stays behind each reported measure. That is why the star gap carries the heaviest weight in the audit: each star below five adds 15 points to your risk score. It is the one number on the list that hospitals, families, and competitors can all read without asking you.

Readmissions come next, at two points for each point of readmission rate, because a rising readmission rate is rarely one clinician's problem. It is usually a system talking: visit timing, medication teaching, escalation when a patient changes, and documentation that does not match the care delivered. Patient satisfaction and OASIS timeliness fill out the scored four, each adding a little over one point for each point below 100. The OASIS input has a hard federal clock behind it. Under 42 CFR 484.45, a home health agency must encode and electronically transmit each completed OASIS assessment to the CMS system within 30 days of completing the assessment, and the encoded data must accurately reflect the patient's status at the time of assessment. An agency whose OASIS work runs late is not just slow on paperwork. It is brushing against a Condition of Participation standard, and its own quality data arrives too stale to manage with.

The non-medical track, scored on delivery

The home care track scores the four numbers that decide whether a non-medical agency keeps its promises. Caregiver turnover adds 0.4 points for each point of turnover, because turnover is the slow leak under everything else: every departure restarts hiring, orientation, and the family's trust in a new face. Unfilled shifts add 1.5 points for each point, the heaviest weight on this track, because a shift nobody worked is the one failure the client experiences directly. EVV and documentation exceptions add 1.2 points for each point, because an exception pattern is tomorrow's claim denial pattern, worked out in advance. Authorized hours not delivered add 0.7 points for each point below full delivery, because hours authorized but never staffed are revenue the agency already earned the right to collect and then handed back. The remaining questions, on speed to first visit, supervisory visits, care plan reviews, and referral source, shape the recommendations without moving the score.

How to read the score, honestly

The score runs from 0 to 100. Under 50 reads as low risk, 50 to 74 as moderate, and 75 and up as high. Alongside the number, the audit names your three biggest problem areas and gives a plain language action for each one. Now the honest part, which the tool prints in its own methodology section and which belongs in this article too: the weights and thresholds are a working model built on my professional judgment. This is not a CMS validated instrument. CMS does not publish this score, no surveyor will ask for it, and it does not diagnose a compliance problem. What it does is force eight numbers you already own into one room, in about five minutes, and rank them by how much damage each one does when it drifts. Owners do not lack data. In the agencies I work with, the data sits in four different reports that nobody opens in the same week. The audit is a structured way to look, not a verdict.

The practical warning

The warning is about the answers, not the tool. A self-audit scored on numbers you guessed at will hand you back a guess with a decimal point on it, and a flattering guess is worse than none, because it closes the question. Pull each number from where it actually lives before you answer: your star rating and satisfaction scores from Care Compare and your survey reports, your readmission rate from your quality reports, your OASIS timeliness from your submission reports, your turnover from your payroll roster, your unfilled shifts and EVV exceptions from your scheduling and EVV dashboards. If you cannot find one of the numbers, that is your first finding. An owner who cannot produce the agency's turnover rate or exception count in five minutes has located a risk no score needs to name.

Your next step this week

Here is the one actionable next step. Run the audit once, with real numbers, and write down the three problem areas it names. Then pick only the first one and give it one owner and one dated action this week: the report you will pull every Monday, the exception queue you will clear every Friday, the exit conversation you will start having with every departing caregiver. A score you recheck next quarter against the same eight questions tells you whether the fix moved anything. A score you admire once and file away tells you nothing.
