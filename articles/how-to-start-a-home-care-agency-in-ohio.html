How to Start a Home Care Agency in Ohio (The $250 Fee and the $20,000 Bond)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 1, 2026

The Ohio story usually starts with a founder in Columbus, Cleveland, or Cincinnati who did the homework the way most people do it: search first, read the top guides, build a budget. The guides disagree with each other. Some say Ohio does not license non-medical home care at all. Others quote a fee and stop there. So the founder picks the answer that fits the plan, prints business cards, and starts telling families what the agency will do. That is the moment the real Ohio rule surfaces, and it is not a paperwork rule. Since October 1, 2022, providing non-medical home care in Ohio without a state license is a criminal offense, not a fine you pay and forget. The founder who marketed first and checked the license second has a problem no budget fixes.

That gap between the old guides and the current law is the reason this video exists, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against an Ohio primary source today. Two points deserve care because short versions flatten them. First, the solo caregiver question has a real statutory boundary: a self-employed provider is covered by the license mandate, but the definitions in the statute carve out a person who provides direct care to not more than two individuals who are not immediate family members. Second, neither the statute nor the licensing rule sets a state processing deadline for this license, so any day count you hear for approval is an estimate from someone else, not a promise from the Ohio Department of Health. The clocks Ohio does publish are the three deadlines in the rule, and they all run against the applicant.

The demand: about 2.40 million seniors in a middle income state

Start with why Ohio draws new owners. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 11,900,510 as of July 1, 2025, and 20.2 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 2.40 million seniors today. The same Census QuickFacts page for Ohio puts median household income at $71,389 for 2020 to 2024. Private pay home care is bought out of household income by families who need help at home, and Ohio pairs a senior population larger than most states total population with three major metros and a long tail of mid-size cities where a new agency can still get known by name.

The price families face explains the business case. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Ohio, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $77,792, up 6 percent and ranked 32nd among states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to $34 an hour. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows a state priced just under the national median with about 2.40 million seniors in it, which is the gap new agencies form to fill.

The mandate: a license since October 1, 2022, for agencies and solo providers alike

Ohio licenses this business through the Ohio Department of Health under Chapter 3740 of the Revised Code. The mandate sits in section 3740.02: no home health agency shall provide nonmedical home health services through one or more employees, and no agency shall hold itself or any employee out as a provider of those services, unless it holds a current, valid nonmedical home health services license or a skilled home health services license. The same section applies the same rule to nonagency providers, the self-employed caregivers Ohio calls nonagency providers. Going solo does not dodge the requirement.

The timeline matters because the old guides were once right. Section 3740.02 was enacted by House Bill 110 of the 134th General Assembly, effective September 30, 2021, and the section states that the license mandate begins one year after that effective date. That is October 1, 2022. Before that date Ohio had no state license for non-medical home care. Now it does. Any guide, checklist, or course that still describes Ohio as a no-license state is describing a market that closed four years ago.

What counts as non-medical care, and where the boundary sits

The definitions in section 3740.01 decide who needs the license, so read them the way a surveyor would. Under division (N), nonmedical home health services means home health aide services plus personal care services. Home health aide services, under division (H), are hands-on bathing or help with a tub bath or shower, help with dressing, ambulation, and toileting, catheter care but not insertion, and meal preparation and feeding. Personal care services, under division (Q), are hands-on help with activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living, help managing the home and handling personal affairs, help with self-administration of medications, homemaker services when they are incidental to that hands-on care or essential to the individual health and welfare, respite services for the caregiver, and errands run outside the presence of the individual when needed for health and safety, including picking up prescriptions and groceries. That list is the private pay lane most founders are building: personal care, homemaking tied to care, and companion type support around a client who needs hands-on help.

The exclusions are just as specific, and they are where founders misread the law in both directions. The definition of a home health agency in division (G) leaves out nursing homes, residential care facilities, hospice care programs, providers certified by the Department of Developmental Disabilities, residential facilities licensed under the sections named there, shared living providers, and immediate family members. The definition of a nonagency provider in division (M) leaves out immediate family caregivers, volunteers, and, the one solo operators need to know cold, a person who provides direct care to not more than two individuals who are not immediate family members of the provider. It also leaves out a person who provides self-directed services as defined in the federal provision the statute cites. Care for one or two non-family clients on your own sits outside the nonagency definition. The third client, the first employee, or the first advertisement holding you out as a provider moves you into licensed territory. Decide which side of that line your business plan lives on before you print anything.

The money: $250 to apply, a $20,000 bond if you are new, three years per license

The fee is set in the licensing rule, and the form of payment is part of the requirement. Under Ohio Administrative Code rule 3701-60-03(B)(2), a complete nonmedical application includes a non-refundable license fee of $250 in the form of a cashier's check or a postal money order payable to the Treasurer, State of Ohio. A personal check does not satisfy the rule, and a non-refundable fee sent in the wrong form buys you a delay, not a license.

The bond is the line item new owners miss. Under paragraph (B)(3)(k) of the same rule, an applicant who was not providing nonmedical home health services on or immediately prior to September 30, 2021 must file a surety bond in the amount of $20,000, issued by a company licensed to do business in Ohio. A surety bond is not a $20,000 payment to the state. You pay a premium to a surety company for a bond that guarantees that amount, and the premium a brand new owner pays depends on credit and history. Budget the premium next to the fee from day one. For comparison, the skilled home health services license in paragraph (A) of the same rule carries a $50,000 bond on the same new entrant condition, which is one more reason the non-medical track and the skilled track should never share a budget.

The term softens the math. Under section 3740.05, a license issued under this chapter is valid for three years, and the Department shall renew it for three more years when the applicant has paid the renewal fee of $250 and shown that it still meets the requirements. One fee covers three years of operation. The renewal clock that controls that renewal is in the rule, and it is covered below, because it is the deadline Ohio owners actually miss.

The application packet: eleven items, and the policies go in with it

Rule 3701-60-03(B)(3) lists what a complete nonmedical application contains, and the list is the checklist. It names the name, address, and business telephone number of the operating location; the names and addresses of every person with an ownership or controlling interest, with the other ownership information the rule requires; the corporate name, if any, and the names, titles, addresses, and telephone numbers of officers and the statutory agent; a list of the nonmedical services provided or to be provided, with any policies and procedures related to those services; copies of all documents filed and recorded with the Ohio Secretary of State; a notarized attestation verifying identity, for a nonagency provider applicant; a copy of the agency written criminal records check policy, for a home health agency applicant; a statement of the days and hours of operation; a description of the geographic area to be served; fingerprint impressions of the primary owner or of the nonagency provider; and the bond documentation or proof that the applicant was already providing services before the September 30, 2021 cutoff. Completed forms prescribed by the director and the $250 fee in the required form sit on top of that list under paragraphs (B)(1) and (B)(2).

Two features of that list shape the work. First, the policies and procedures tied to your services go in with the application, not after approval. An owner who plans to write policies once clients arrive has the sequence backwards: in Ohio the documents are part of how the state decides whether you open at all. Second, the rule builds in relief for two kinds of applicants. Under paragraph (E), items (B)(3)(d) through (B)(3)(k) are waived for a provider certified by the Department of Aging under section 173.391 to provide community-based long-term care services. Under paragraph (F), items (B)(3)(d) through (B)(3)(i) are waived for a provider accredited by a national accreditation organization the rule names. A first-time private pay founder qualifies for neither waiver, so the full list is the plan.

Fingerprints and the criminal records check policy: the two background pieces are different documents

Ohio asks for two separate background items, and confusing them is a classic stall. The first is personal to the owner: fingerprint impressions of the primary owner of the agency, or of the nonagency provider, under paragraph (B)(3)(j). The rule asks for the fingerprint impressions themselves. A background check report you bought from a third-party service is a different document, and mailing the report instead of the impressions leaves the application incomplete. The second item is institutional: a copy of the agency written criminal records check policy under paragraph (B)(3)(g). The state is not only asking whether you ran checks. It is asking to read the policy that governs how your agency checks direct care employees, and the statute defines criminal records check and disqualifying offense in section 3740.01 by reference to section 109.572. Build the policy before the first hire, get the owner fingerprints done in the form the application expects, and keep the two documents in two separate envelopes in your own file so neither one gets substituted for the other.

The surprise: no training hours in the non-medical license rule

Read the application list in rule 3701-60-03(B) again and notice what is not on it. There is no aide training hour requirement in the nonmedical license rule: no classroom hours, no competency program named as a condition of this license. That surprises founders who arrive with numbers borrowed from other programs. The separate Ohio paths are different. The PASSPORT program through the Department of Aging, the Ohio Home Care Waiver through the Department of Medicaid, and developmental disabilities provider certification each run under their own rules, and training requirements live there. Keep the lanes separate. This license, the private pay lane, sets no state training hours in its rule, and that is not permission to skip training. It is a warning about where your protection sits. When something goes wrong in a client home, your policies, your supervision, and your records are what you have. An agency with no state training floor has to set its own floor and be able to prove every caregiver met it.

The three clocks: 14 days, 90 days, 10 days

Ohio publishes no issuance deadline for this license in the statute or the rule I verified for this article, so the timelines you can control are the three the rule sets for you. Clock one is the information clock. Under paragraph (G), when the director requests additional information to decide compliance, the applicant is obliged to furnish it within 14 days after the mailing date of the director request. Count from the mailing date, not the day the letter reaches your desk. Clock two is the renewal clock. Under paragraph (H), an application for license renewal is considered timely only when it is made at least 90 days prior to the expiration of the license, with a complete application and the renewal fee. Ninety days, on a three year license, means the renewal belongs on the calendar in year three, not in the final month. Clock three is the change clock. Under paragraph (I), notice to the director is required within 10 days for any change in the information in the license application, or any other change that would make the submitted information inaccurate. New address, new owner, new service area: the state hears about it within 10 days. None of the three clocks is hard to meet. All three are easy to miss if nobody owns them.

The penalty: a misdemeanor, and a license the Department can take

The enforcement side is what separates Ohio from most non-medical markets. Under section 3740.99, whoever violates section 3740.02 is guilty of a misdemeanor of the second degree on a first offense, and for each subsequent offense a misdemeanor of the first degree. Operating without the license is a criminal charge, not an administrative fee. Separately, under section 3740.07, the Department of Health may, for the reasons set in its rules, refuse to issue a license, refuse to renew or reinstate one, impose limitations, revoke or suspend a license, or place a holder on probation or reprimand the holder, with actions taken under Chapter 119. The application includes notarized attestations and sworn level statements about ownership and identity. Treat every line on it as what it is: a statement the state can hold you to.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Ohio owner. The expensive mistakes in this state happen on paper before the first client, not in the client home. Trust an old guide that says Ohio has no license and you can build a whole launch on a criminal exposure. Send the $250 as a personal check and the non-refundable fee stalls on its form. Mail a purchased background report instead of the owner fingerprint impressions and the packet sits incomplete. Skip the bond because nobody mentioned it and a new entrant application stops cold. Then, three years later, let the renewal drift inside 90 days and a license you already earned is no longer timely. Every one of those failures is a document or a date, and every one is knowable before you spend anything beyond the fee itself. Ohio is not a hard state to enter. It is a state that punishes entering carelessly.

Your next step

Start with the boundary, not the application. Write down the services your caregivers will actually perform and the number of clients you will serve in your first ninety days, and test that plan against the definitions in section 3740.01, including the two client carve-out for a solo nonagency provider and the hands-on list in divisions (H) and (Q). If your plan needs the license, and most agency plans do, build the packet in rule order: Secretary of State filings first, then the service list with its policies and procedures, the criminal records check policy, the owner fingerprint impressions, and the $20,000 bond quote, with the $250 fee as a cashier's check or postal money order payable to the Treasurer, State of Ohio. Put the 14 day, 90 day, and 10 day clocks on a calendar the day you file, with a named owner for each one. An owner who files in that order spends the wait building the agency instead of repairing the application.
