How to Start a Home Care Agency in North Dakota (No License and No Published Fee for Non-Medical Care, a QSP Door for Public Pay)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The North Dakota story starts with a founder in Fargo who did everything right except read the map. She formed the business, priced insurance, built her screening file, and applied for the public pay credential that pays for personal care in this state, the Agency Qualified Service Provider enrollment. What she had not seen was a state notice, effective that June, pausing new enrollments for agencies in her county. Her county is Cass. The pause did not touch her private pay work, her license posture, or agencies one county over, and it carried an end to its first term in December that her plan never accounted for. North Dakota rewards that level of precision everywhere: this is a state that licenses skilled care and hospice, creates no license at all for non-medical care, publishes no fee for the licenses it does issue, and runs its one enrollment gate by county and provider type. In the agency records I review, North Dakota errors are geographic and categorical, a statewide reading of a two county gate, a fee imported from a national grid, a repealed certificate of need chapter treated as live. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 143,000 seniors in the smallest market in this series

Start with why owners keep looking at North Dakota. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 799,358 as of July 1, 2025, and 17.9 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 143,000 seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for North Dakota puts median household income at $76,657 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into. The market is small and concentrated: Fargo in Cass County and Bismarck in Burleigh County anchor demand, and those are, not coincidentally, the two counties named in the state enrollment gate covered below. A North Dakota plan is a county plan before it is a state plan.

The license question, answered directly: no non-medical license, a QSP enrollment for public pay, and licenses for skilled care and hospice

Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in North Dakota? No state license exists for that work. No statute or rule creates a license for an agency that provides only personal care, companion, and homemaker services. The credential for public pay work is enrollment as an Agency Qualified Service Provider, an Agency QSP, under N.D. Admin. Code chapter 75-03-23, through the HHS QSP Enrollment Portal, designated service by service. Two licensed doors sit beside it, both administered by the Department of Health and Human Services: the home health agency license under N.D.C.C. chapter 23-17.3 and N.D. Admin. Code chapter 33-03-10.1, and the hospice program license under N.D.C.C. chapter 23-17.4. No one door covers another door's services, and an unlicensed agency may not use the terms home health agency or home health services.

The state enrollment gate needs its full scope stated, because the first summaries of it were incomplete. Effective June 11, 2026, North Dakota Medicaid placed a temporary moratorium on new enrollments that covers Agency QSP enrollments in Burleigh and Cass counties, non-emergency medical transportation agencies statewide, and developmental disabilities agencies in Burleigh and Cass counties. The first term runs six months, to about December 11, 2026, and as of November 19, 2026 no extension has been published. Applications already in final approval on the effective date continued; applications short of final approval were denied. It is a state payer enrollment gate from the Department of Health and Human Services, not a licensure pause, not a bar on private pay work, and not the federal Medicare moratorium. Checked negatives complete the frame: North Dakota repealed its certificate of need chapter, former N.D.C.C. chapter 23-17.2, and the certificate of need sentence still printed at N.D. Admin. Code 33-03-15-01 points at the repealed chapter and is a stale citation, not a live gate.

The non-medical lane: private pay on the business record, public pay on the QSP door

Private pay non-medical work runs on business formation, insurance, and the hiring record. Personal care services are help with activities of daily living: mobility, transfers, dressing, eating, toileting, bathing, and hygiene, plus household tasks such as shopping, laundry, and cleaning, and no practitioner order is required on the private pay door. The public pay build adds two enrollments. Agency QSP enrollment under chapter 75-03-23 pays for personal care, homemaker, respite, and the other home and community based services the designation covers, and an Agency QSP is designated service by service, with competency documentation for every worker for the services that worker delivers. North Dakota Medicaid provider enrollment runs alongside it, through the state Medicaid enrollment process with Noridian Healthcare Solutions as the enrollment contractor. No Agency QSP enrollment fee was published on the HHS QSP pages checked, so the packet prints NO FEE PUBLISHED for the enrollment fee as well as for any license fee.

Sequence the county gate before any Fargo or Bismarck public pay opening: while the moratorium's first term runs, a new Agency QSP enrollment in Burleigh or Cass County is gated, and a developmental disabilities agency plan in those counties is gated with it, while non-emergency medical transportation agencies are gated statewide. Outside those categories and counties, enrollment runs on the normal frame, enrolled providers are not displaced, and private pay work is untouched everywhere. Electronic visit verification attaches to Medicaid personal care visits through the Therap state system, offered at no charge, or an alternate system feeding the Sandata aggregator; North Dakota runs an open EVV model, and Therap is the visit capture system while Sandata is the aggregator, a pairing that plans regularly swap by mistake.

The skilled and hospice lanes: licenses with clocks and no published fees

A home health agency under chapter 23-17.3 provides skilled nursing and at least one other therapeutic service, physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, medical social services, or home health aide services, on a regular basis, under a supervising physician or supervising registered nurse employed full time, with operating standards at chapter 33-03-10.1. There is no certificate of need gate in front of this license. The sequence is the license itself: the application, the fitness and ownership record the rule requires, the inspection, and the licensure survey. The clocks are the substance here: an initial license runs no more than one year, licenses expire December 31 each year under 33-03-10.1-03, and a provisional license runs no more than 90 days and may be renewed once. Build the renewal calendar on the December 31 expiry, not on the anniversary of the first license. The fee facts are a checked negative: 33-03-10.1-03 sets the application, issuance, and renewal process and prints no dollar fee, and no fee figure was located on the HHS licensing pages, so this packet prints NO FEE PUBLISHED for the home health agency license fee.

The hospice program license under chapter 23-17.4 is renewed annually under section 23-17.4-03, and its fee handling matches: section 23-17.4-04 requires a uniform license fee determined by the department and prints no figure, so the packet prints NO FEE PUBLISHED there too, and no consultant figure fills the gap. Medicare certification layers on either licensed door through Form CMS-855A in PECOS with the CY2026 federal enrollment fee of $750, to CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for North Dakota; the A/B MAC is Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction F, and Noridian Jurisdiction D is the DME MAC only. Initial Medicare enrollment on the certified tracks is gated by the federal moratoria extended effective November 12, 2026 under CMS-6101-N2 and CMS-6102-N2, with no end date printed in the notices (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). Beside the home-based doors, the residential credentials carry their own published figures, $75 per year for an assisted living facility under section 50-32-02 and $10 per bed for a basic care facility under section 23-09.3-05.1, and a developmental disabilities group home runs on the license at chapter 25-16 and chapter 75-04-01. None of those figures is a home care fee.

Background checks, competency files, and the two-vendor EVV rule

North Dakota readiness is a documentation test run service by service. On the QSP door, every worker carries the competency documentation for the services that worker delivers, and the designation itself is granted service by service, so a worker file that documents personal care does not carry homemaker or respite authority the agency never enrolled for. On the licensed doors, the personnel folder carries the ownership, competency, and background record the rule requires, home health aides work under nursing supervision, and the supervising clinician is employed full time on the chapter 23-17.3 frame. The deficiencies the packet names are the recurring ones in the records I review: registry, background, and exclusion checks run at hire and never again, plan of care reviews drifting past the cycle chapter 33-03-10.1 prints, and EVV exceptions left unworked in the Sandata aggregator.

The EVV pairing deserves its own line because it decides system setup. North Dakota runs an open model: Therap is the state contracted EVV system and it is free to providers and individuals, and Sandata Technologies is the data aggregator that alternate systems feed. A plan that names Sandata as the visit capture system has the two vendors swapped, and the correction is a configuration project, not a sentence edit, if it is found after visits start. EVV covers Medicaid personal care services and home health services that require an in-home visit, and it attaches from the first billed visit on either door.

What you still do today: form the business the North Dakota way

Form the entity with the North Dakota Secretary of State, get the EIN and the tax registrations, and put liability insurance in place before the first client. Then build the record for your door: the competency and screening systems and a service agreement that keeps the words home health agency and home health services off a non-medical agency's materials, because those terms belong to the chapter 23-17.3 license; or, on the licensed doors, the application file, the ownership and fitness record, and the survey preparation. Set the calendars the state actually prints: December 31 license expirations, the 90 day provisional limit, and, for a Burleigh or Cass County public pay plan, the moratorium's first term running to about December 11, 2026, with a recheck for any extension notice before an enrollment date is promised to anyone.

The practical warning

Four North Dakota traps recur. The first is the license that does not exist: North Dakota creates no non-medical home care license and publishes no home care license fee, and the $500 figure in a national quick reference grid matches no North Dakota primary source, so a budget carrying it is carrying another state's number. The second is the moratorium read at the wrong scope, in either direction: read as only a two county QSP pause, it misses the statewide non-emergency medical transportation coverage; read as a statewide bar on home care, it wrongly stops private pay work and enrollments the notice never named. The third is the repealed certificate of need chapter, sequenced as a live gate off the stale sentence at 33-03-15-01. The fourth is the uniform fee with no figure: section 23-17.4-04 requires a hospice fee the department determines, and until the department publishes one, the honest entry in the budget is NO FEE PUBLISHED, confirmed at filing, not a borrowed number.

How do I start a home care agency in North Dakota?

You start by separating private pay from public pay, because North Dakota licenses neither non-medical work nor its public pay credential. Non-medical home care holds no state license. Public pay work runs on Agency Qualified Service Provider enrollment under N.D. Admin. Code chapter 75-03-23. Skilled care files for the home health agency license under N.D.C.C. chapter 23-17.3, and hospice files for the hospice program license under chapter 23-17.4. Your next step

Work in door order, county first. If non-medical care is the plan, form the business and serve private pay clients on the business record, and for public pay, run Agency QSP enrollment under chapter 75-03-23 service by service, sequenced against the moratorium if the agency sits in Burleigh or Cass County, where the first term runs to about December 11, 2026 with no extension published as of November 19, 2026. If skilled care or hospice is the plan, file for the chapter 23-17.3 or chapter 23-17.4 license, budget it as NO FEE PUBLISHED and confirm any fee with the Department of Health and Human Services at filing, and build the renewal calendar on the December 31 expiry.
