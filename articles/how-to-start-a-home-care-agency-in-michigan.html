How to Start a Home Care Agency in Michigan (No State License for Non-Medical Care)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 27, 2026

The search usually starts with a license that does not exist. Someone in Detroit, Grand Rapids, or Ann Arbor decides to start a non-medical home care agency, goes looking for the Michigan application, and keeps landing on licensing pages for nursing homes, hospice programs, and homes for the aged. An hour later the doubt sets in: maybe the application is buried somewhere, maybe the state calls it something else. It does not. For a private pay agency that provides only non-medical care at home, Michigan has no home care agency license to apply for.

That is the moment this video was made for, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against a primary source. Michigan is not a small consolation market, either. It is the largest senior market I found among the states with no license for non-medical home care in my own 50 state analysis, with more than 2 million seniors and no licensure application standing between a prepared owner and the first client. The short version is this: no license does not mean no work, and it does not mean no standards. It means your preparation, not a state approval, is the gate.

The demand is real, and it is large

Start with why Michigan draws so many new owners. The Census Bureau puts Michigan population at 10,127,884 as of July 1, 2025, and 20.5 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 2.08 million seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Michigan puts median household income at $72,875 for 2020 to 2024, which matters because private pay home care is bought out of household income.

The price families face explains the business case. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Michigan, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $79,508, based on 44 hours a week for 52 weeks. That works out to about $35 an hour. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows that Michigan families are already paying for help at home at a level that can support a real agency, if the agency is built right.

Why there is no application to find

Michigan licenses health facilities and agencies through its Public Health Code, and the list of what gets licensed is specific. Under section 333.20106 of the Michigan Compiled Laws, a health facility or agency means an ambulance operation, a county medical care facility, a freestanding surgical outpatient facility, a health maintenance organization, a home for the aged, a hospital, a nursing home, a hospice, or a hospice residence. A non-medical home care agency is not on that list. The license requirement itself, in section 333.20141, attaches to a health facility or agency as defined there: a person shall not establish, maintain, or operate one without a license from the department.

When Michigan law does talk about home health, it ties the term to Medicare. In section 333.20173a, a home health agency is defined as a person certified by Medicare whose business is to provide nursing services, therapeutic services, social work services, homemaker services, home health aide services, or other related services in a person's home. Skilled home health runs through federal Medicare certification. That is a different lane from private pay non-medical care, and it is not a state application you file to sell companionship and personal care.

So the line to build on is the service list. Personal care, companionship, homemaking, meal preparation, transportation, medication reminders, and respite care, provided as non-medical support at home, sit in the no license lane. Skilled nursing, therapy, and other clinical work do not. And if the plan changes from care in the client's own home to room, board, and supervised personal care in a facility you run, you have left this lane entirely: a home for the aged is on the licensing list above, and adult foster care carries its own license. Do not market across those lines and operate on the other side of them.

What no license does not remove

No state home care license removes the application. It does not remove the business. You still form an entity, get an employer identification number, carry liability insurance and workers compensation where required for your staff, write policies down, hire and train caregivers, and keep records a family or a referral source can inspect.

On the entity step, Michigan files business formations with the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs, LARA, through its Corporations Division, not with a secretary of state style office. A limited liability company files Articles of Organization, Form CSCL/CD-700, and the filing fee on the LARA fee schedule is $50. An LLC also files an annual statement each year, $25, due by February 15, and it keeps a resident agent with a Michigan street address on file. Check whether your city or township requires a local business license before you open. Those are ordinary business steps, but they are the steps that make you an agency instead of a person with a flyer.

Policies are not back office decoration in a no license state. They are your proof. Write down, before the first client, how you screen and train caregivers, how you build and change a care plan, how you handle a missed visit, a fall, a medication reminder that stays within the non-medical line, a complaint, and an emergency. Run a background check on every caregiver before the first visit and keep the result in the file, as your own documented standard. Families vet you. Hospitals, discharge planners, and other referral sources ask for your policies and your insurance before they send anyone your way. When anyone can open, professionalism is what sets you apart, and professionalism has to be written down to be believed.

The Medicaid lane is a different lane

Private pay with no license is not the same as Medicaid work. Michigan pays for personal care through its Home Help program, run by the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services, MDHHS. On the MDHHS Home Help individual caregivers page, MDHHS states that an individual caregiver must have enrollment approved through the CHAMPS Provider Enrollment subsystem to be reimbursed for covered services to eligible Medicaid beneficiaries, and that a caregiver can enroll on their own or enroll and associate with an enrolled Home Help agency. The same page points caregivers to Electronic Visit Verification, EVV, and to the local MDHHS county office for authorization before services and payment.

Keep the lanes separate in your plan. Getting hired by private pay families does not enroll anyone with Medicaid, and Medicaid enrollment does not turn skilled work into non-medical work. Decide which payer you are building for first, then build the enrollment, training, and records that payer requires.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Michigan owner. The risk in a no license state is not that the state slows you down. The risk is that you slow yourself down by treating no license as no preparation, or that you drift across the skilled line in your marketing because a family asks for one more task. A caregiver who is hired for companionship and homemaking cannot quietly become the person managing wound care or insulin. Stop, write the service list down, and refer skilled needs to a Medicare certified home health agency. That boundary protects the client, the caregiver, and the business you are trying to build.

The second risk is invisibility. A large senior population does not fill your census for you. Families cannot hire an agency they have never heard of, and referral sources cannot refer to an agency that has not shown them insurance, policies, and a clear service list. In Michigan, speed to open only helps if visibility and proof move at the same speed.

Your next step

Write your service list down first, in plain language, and check every item against the skilled line above. If the list is clean, form the entity with LARA, get the employer identification number, bind insurance, and write the policies and caregiver training plan down before you take the first client. That order is the whole game in Michigan. Owners who follow it open as a business. Owners who reverse it open as a risk.
