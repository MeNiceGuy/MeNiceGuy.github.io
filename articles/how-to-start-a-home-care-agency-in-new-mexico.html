How to Start a Home Care Agency in New Mexico (No License for Non-Medical Care, $300 Licenses for Home Health and Hospice)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The New Mexico story starts with bad directions. A founder I worked with arrived holding a printout that described a licensing frame for this state with confidence and detail: a two scope license, a supportive care category, citations, the works. Almost none of it was New Mexico law. The frame had been transplanted from another state's rulebook, and it contradicted the state's own fee schedule printed a few pages later in the same packet. I flag that here because New Mexico is the state in this series where secondhand summaries have done the most damage, and this article is built the other way: every door, fee, and payer name below comes from the New Mexico Health Care Authority and Title 8, Chapter 370 NMAC, checked against the corrected November 2026 record. The real New Mexico picture is simpler than the transplant made it look. Non-medical care is not licensed. Skilled home health is licensed at $300. Hospice is licensed too, at $300, whatever a summary that says otherwise claims. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 448,000 seniors, more than one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at New Mexico. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 2,125,498 as of July 1, 2025, and 21.1 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 448,000 seniors, more than one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for New Mexico puts median household income at $64,059 for 2020 to 2024, the lowest income base in this batch of states, which is part of why the Medicaid doors, Turquoise Care and the waiver programs, carry so much of the practical planning here. Private pay exists in New Mexico, and the payer sequence matters more, not less, because of the income mix.

The license question, answered directly: the Health Care Authority licenses home health and hospice, and non-medical care has no license

Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in New Mexico? No state license exists for that work. Facility licensing in New Mexico sits with the Health Care Authority, in its Division of Health Improvement, and the health facility rules sit at Title 8, Chapter 370 NMAC, recodified effective January 28, 2025. No license category for a standalone non-medical agency appears in the health facility list at Section 24-1-2 NMSA 1978, in the fee schedule at 8.370.3.10 NMAC, or in the home health agency rule at 8.370.22 NMAC, whose scope is medically directed care. There is no supportive care license scope in New Mexico law, and any source that describes one is describing another state.

The two licensed doors are specific. Home health agencies are licensed under Section 24-1-5 NMSA 1978 and 8.370.22 NMAC, with operating standards at 8.370.22.7 through 8.370.22.33 NMAC. Hospice is licensed under 8.370.19 NMAC, the requirements for in-home and inpatient hospice care, and the fee schedule at 8.370.3.10 NMAC charges for that license, which settles the question some summaries get wrong: a New Mexico hospice license exists. No certificate of need requirement for home health agencies or hospices was located in the sources checked, and no Turquoise Care or DDSD payer enrollment moratorium was located either. One perishable belongs in this section because owners budget against rules: 8.370.22 NMAC is under a proposed repeal and replace. The Health Care Authority notice is dated November 3, 2026, the public hearing is December 4, 2026, and the proposed effective date is December 22, 2026. That change is proposed, not enacted. This article follows the current rule, and any filing made after December 22, 2026 should recheck the adopted rule first.

The non-medical lane: no license, a screening act, and the Turquoise Care door

The non-medical build has no licensure filing at all. What attaches is ordinary business formation with the New Mexico Secretary of State, the EIN and tax registrations, insurance, and the workforce record. The Caregivers Criminal History Screening Act, Sections 29-17-1 and following NMSA 1978, with the screening rule at 8.370.5 NMAC, covers every caregiver the agency sends into a home, licensed or not. Build the screening, competency, and supervision files before the first visit, because on a door with no license, that file is the credential a family, a hospital discharge planner, or a managed care credentialing reviewer can be shown.

The Medicaid door on this lane is Turquoise Care Community Benefit. Personal care under Community Benefit is agency based personal care for members who meet nursing facility level of care, delivered through enrollment and contracts with the Turquoise Care managed care organizations. Enrollment runs through the Provider and PED Enrollment System at YES.NM.GOV, with revalidation at least once every five years. Managed care runs through four Turquoise Care organizations: Blue Cross Blue Shield of New Mexico, Molina Healthcare of New Mexico, Presbyterian Health Plan, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan of New Mexico. Get the roster history right: Western Sky Community Care was a Centennial Care plan. It never held a Turquoise Care contract; it did not carry into Turquoise Care when the program launched on July 1, 2024, so a plan list that shows Western Sky exiting Turquoise Care in 2025 is describing a contract that never existed. Electronic visit verification runs through AuthentiCare, by Fiserv, the state funded system provided at no cost to providers, or an approved alternate feeding the state aggregator, and it attaches to Medicaid personal care visits from the first billed visit. A private pay only agency holds no enrollment door and needs none.

The skilled and hospice lanes: two $300 licenses under Chapter 370

The home health agency license covers skilled home health services under practitioner orders, plus HCBS attendant care where the agency serves waiver programs, and a clinical manager who is a registered nurse is required. There is no certificate of need gate in front of it. The sequence is the license itself: application, the fee, the policy file the Division of Health Improvement reviews, and the licensure survey, and no person may provide home health services before the license issues. The fee at 8.370.3.10 NMAC is $300 per license, charged on initial application and on renewal of the annual license; the first temporary license fee is included in the initial fee, and the same $300 is charged before a second temporary license issues. Fees are prepaid and nonrefundable under 8.370.3.13 NMAC. The $300 figure at 8.370.3.11 NMAC prices an amended license, a change to an existing license; it is a coincidence of the schedule that the two figures match, and the $500 figure in secondary 50 state grids has no New Mexico primary source behind it. Plan of treatment reviews run on the sixty day cycle at 8.370.22.33 NMAC.

Hospice runs the same fee on its own license. Under 8.370.19 NMAC and 8.370.3.10 NMAC, in-home and inpatient hospice care costs $300 per license, and a free-standing hospice costs $300, on the annual cycle. Medicare certification under 42 CFR Part 418 is the parallel federal door, and initial Medicare enrollment for hospices is gated by CMS-6102-N2, as home health enrollment is gated by CMS-6101-N2, extended effective November 12, 2026 with no end date printed in the notices (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). Medicare enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS with the CY2026 federal enrollment fee of $750. The Home Health and Hospice MAC for New Mexico is Palmetto GBA, Jurisdiction JM. The A/B MAC is Novitas Solutions, Jurisdiction H, and CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction C, is the DME MAC only. Beside the home-based doors, the residential licenses on the same fee schedule include adult day care facilities at $300 per license under 8.370.20 NMAC and assisted living facilities at $300; an assisted living license is a residential credential issued by the Health Care Authority, it authorizes no in-home agency work, and no in-home agency needs it.

Screening, waivers, and EVV: the requirements behind the doors

The screening record is the constant across every New Mexico door. The Caregivers Criminal History Screening Act assigns the criminal history checks for home health agency staff through the state systems the act names, the New Mexico Nurse Aide Registry is checked for aide staff, and the file is built before the first visit, not after a survey finding. One caution belongs here because a transplanted figure circulated on this point: no $50 fingerprint fee cap appears in 8.370.22 NMAC, and no New Mexico source prints one, so no figure for a fingerprint cap belongs in a New Mexico budget. Across doors, the deficiencies the packet names are operational, not clerical: EVV exceptions left unworked past the aggregator window, plan of treatment reviews drifting past the sixty day cycle, and registry and exclusion checks run at hire and never again.

The waiver map is New Mexico's own, and it is short enough to state completely. The Developmental Disabilities Waiver, the Mi Via Waiver, which has operated in New Mexico since 2006 as the self-directed model, and the Medically Fragile Waiver run through the Developmental Disabilities Supports Division frame, and Community Benefit is delivered under the Turquoise Care 1115 demonstration, carrying agency based personal care, respite, and homemaker lines for eligible members. Any waiver list that names Physical Disability, Brain Injury, Technology Assisted, Autism, or Serious Emotional Disturbance waivers for New Mexico is naming another state's programs. EVV covers Medicaid personal care, attendant care, respite, homemaker, home health, private duty nursing, and therapy services under the Turquoise Care EVV requirements, through AuthentiCare or an approved alternate feeding the state aggregator.

What you still do today: form the business the New Mexico way

Form the entity with the New Mexico Secretary of State, get the EIN and the tax registrations, and put liability insurance in place before the first client. Then stand up the record for your door: on the non-medical door, the screening, competency, and supervision files and a service agreement that keeps skilled tasks outside the menu; on the licensed doors, the policy file, the clinical manager appointment, and the survey record. Add the payer systems in the right order, enrollment through the Provider and PED Enrollment System, managed care contracts with the four Turquoise Care organizations for Medicaid work, and EVV configuration before the first billed visit. And put one date on the calendar in red: December 22, 2026, the proposed effective date of the 8.370.22 repeal and replace, as a recheck date for the adopted rule, not as a rule change that has already happened.

The practical warning

The New Mexico warning is about sources before it is about rules. The transplanted frame that opened this article invented a department, a bureau, a license scope, and a hospice position, and every one of those inventions would have misdirected a filing: the regulator is the Health Care Authority, Division of Health Improvement; hospice holds a $300 state license under 8.370.19 NMAC; non-medical care holds no license at all; and the Turquoise Care roster has four plans, including Molina, not three. The operational traps underneath are the usual ones, with New Mexico names: skilled services marketed or delivered on the unlicensed non-medical door, an assisted living license treated as an in-home credential, a home health filing sequenced before its survey record exists, and a Medicare filing sent to the wrong desk, CGS Jurisdiction C instead of Palmetto GBA Jurisdiction JM. Verify the door against Chapter 370 and the fee schedule at 8.370.3.10 NMAC, and the New Mexico build is one of the most straightforward in this series: two $300 licenses, one screening act, and one managed care program with four plans.

How do I start a home care agency in New Mexico?

You start by picking the door your services require, because New Mexico licenses skilled care and hospice and does not license non-medical care at all. Non-medical care holds no state license; its Medicaid door is Turquoise Care Community Benefit enrollment. Skilled care files for the home health agency license under 8.370.22 NMAC, and hospice files for its own license under 8.370.19 NMAC. The licensed sequence runs form the business, build the policy file and screening records, file the application with the $300 fee, and pass the licensure survey before serving anyone. How much does a home care license cost in New Mexico?

Hospice costs $300 per license for in-home and inpatient hospice care, and $300 for a free-standing hospice, on the same schedule.
