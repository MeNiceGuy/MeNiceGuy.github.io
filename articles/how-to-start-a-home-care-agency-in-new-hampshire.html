How to Start a Home Care Agency in New Hampshire (Three RSA 151 Licenses, and No Certificate of Need Anywhere)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The New Hampshire story starts with a founder who had the right instinct and the wrong form. She knew the state licensed home care, she found a department application, and she budgeted the fee it printed for her small agency, $100 for the under 10 clients tier. The statute says $25. That gap, a superseded form against a controlling statute, is the whole New Hampshire lesson in one line: this is a clean, readable licensing system, one chapter, one fee paragraph per door, no certificate of need anywhere in it, and it still punishes anyone who files from a secondary source instead of RSA 151 itself. The state is also one of the oldest in the country by share of seniors, which is why founders keep arriving here with forms already half filled in. In the agency records I review, New Hampshire errors are small, specific, and entirely avoidable: the wrong tier, the wrong hospice fee, an individual registration treated as an agency license. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 326,000 seniors, nearly one in four residents

Start with why owners keep looking at New Hampshire. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 1,415,342 as of July 1, 2025, and 23.0 percent of residents are age 65 or older, one of the highest shares in this series. Run the math and you get about 326,000 seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for New Hampshire puts median household income at $99,031 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into. A small state with a high senior share and a high household income is a compact market: the service area math is short drives and dense demand, and the licensing math, covered next, is shorter than in most states.

The license question, answered directly: three RSA 151 licenses, one per door

Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in New Hampshire? Yes, an agency license, and the chapter that holds it is RSA 151. The New Hampshire Department of Health and Human Services, Health Facilities Administration, licenses home care service provider agencies under He-P 822, home health care providers under He-P 809, and home hospice care providers under He-P 823. An agency that employs caregivers holds an agency license. An individual who works by direct agreement registers under He-P 820 instead, and that registration is not an agency license. No one door covers another door's services, and the parts are easy to merge by mistake, which is why the packet keeps them separate.

Two checked negatives shape every New Hampshire plan, and both were verified against the sources checked on November 17, 2026. First, there is no certificate of need: RSA 151-C was repealed effective June 30, 2016, so no certificate of need requirement gates home health or hospice in New Hampshire, and the 15 mile critical access hospital determination at RSA 151:4, III(a)(7) is a siting determination for listed facility types, not a certificate of need, and it does not list home health, home care, or home hospice providers. Second, no DHHS Medicaid provider enrollment moratorium or licensure pause was located for any New Hampshire door. The only enrollment gates in this state are the federal Medicare moratoria on the certified tracks. An owner budgeting around a certificate of need step or a state pause is budgeting around doors that do not exist here.

The non-medical lane: the home care service provider agency license under He-P 822

A home care service provider agency, under He-P 822 and RSA 151:9-a, XI, provides personal care services or homemaker services through personal care service providers or homemakers employed by the agency. Personal care services are help with activities of daily living: bathing, dressing, eating, toileting and continence care, transferring, mobility, and medication reminders. The license authorizes no nursing and no therapy. He-P 822.17 requires at least 8 hours of training for staff who help with transfers, feeding, bathing, or dressing, and the Home Care Clients Bill of Rights at RSA 151:21-b is given to every client at admission. He-P 822.02 also draws the boundary lines: entities licensed under He-P 809 are excluded because the skilled license already covers their personal care, meal-only providers are outside the part, and individuals registered under He-P 820 are a different door entirely.

The fee on this door is decided by client count, at RSA 151:5, XII: $25 for a personal care provider with fewer than 10 clients, and $250 for 10 or more clients. Exactly 10 clients sits in the $250 tier, because the statute reads ten (10) or more clients. The trap figure is the superseded DHHS application form figure of $100 for the under 10 tier; the statute prints $25 and controls. The license term is one year under RSA 151:5. The Medicaid door on this lane is the Choices for Independence Waiver, CFI, at He-E 801, which pays for personal care, homemaker, respite, and adult family care services for participants who meet nursing facility level of care. Enroll through the NH MMIS Health Enterprise Portal as a CFI provider, meet the He-E 801 provider standards, and configure electronic visit verification: New Hampshire runs an open vendor model with AuthentiCare, by Fiserv, as the state aggregator, and waiver services bill to DHHS fee for service, not to a managed care plan.

The skilled and hospice lanes: He-P 809 at $250 and He-P 823 at $250

A home health care provider under He-P 809 and RSA 151:2-b, I arranges or provides nursing services, home health aide services, or other therapeutic and related services, which may include physical and occupational therapy, speech pathology, nutritional services, medical social services, personal care services, and homemaker services, to persons in their places of residence. The filing sequence is the license itself: the application, the RSA 151:5 fee, the local health, building, zoning, and fire approvals the application requires, and the DHHS inspection before the license issues. The fee at RSA 151:5, XI is $250 for an agency and $25 for a registered individual, and the term is one year. RSA 151:2-b, II and III print limited license forms, for homemaker-only and personal-care-only providers; an agency that wants the limited form files on the He-P 822 track instead, because the limited license is not a second door to stack on this one.

The home hospice care provider license under He-P 823 costs $250 a year at RSA 151:5, X. Keep it separate from the hospice house license under He-P 824, which prices the residential building at $25 per licensed bed at RSA 151:5, VII; budgeting either figure for the other license underfunds or overpays the filing. Beside the home-based doors, the residential fee lines at RSA 151:5 include adult day care centers at $200 flat at RSA 151:5, XX, under rule part He-P 818, and residential and supported residential care at $15 per licensed bed. Medicare certification layers on the He-P 809 license through Form CMS-855A in PECOS with the CY2026 federal enrollment fee of $750, to National Government Services, Jurisdiction JK, which is both the A/B MAC and the Home Health and Hospice MAC for New Hampshire. Initial Medicare enrollment on the certified tracks is gated by the federal moratoria extended effective November 12, 2026 under CMS-6101-N2 and CMS-6102-N2, with no end date printed in the notices (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). Those federal gates do not touch the state licenses, and no state pause fills the space beside them.

Background checks, training records, and the fee-for-service waiver rule

New Hampshire readiness is a records test. The training record at He-P 822.17, at least 8 hours for staff helping with transfers, feeding, bathing, or dressing, belongs in the personnel file before the first visit, and the Home Care Clients Bill of Rights delivery at admission belongs in the client file from day one. Across the doors, the deficiencies the packet names are the ones that recur in the records I review: registry and exclusion checks run at hire and never again, plan of treatment reviews drifting past the cycle He-P 809 prints, and EVV exceptions left unworked past the aggregator window. None of those are filing problems. All of them are systems problems, and they are cheaper to build before the first client than after the first survey.

The payer map has one line that decides billing setups. Medicaid Care Management runs through three plans, AmeriHealth Caritas New Hampshire, NH Healthy Families, and WellSense Health Plan, and the plans pay plan benefits. Waiver services, Choices for Independence and the developmental services waivers, bill to DHHS fee for service through NH MMIS. A billing configuration that routes waiver claims to a managed care plan is built on the wrong door, and the packet names it as a trap for that reason. EVV is mandatory for the Medicaid personal care and home health services the New Hampshire requirements cover, through the AuthentiCare state system or an approved alternate feeding the aggregator, from the first billed visit.

What you still do today: form the business the New Hampshire way

Form the entity with the New Hampshire Secretary of State, get the EIN and the tax registrations, and put liability insurance in place before the first client. Then assemble the license file for your door: the application, the correct RSA 151:5 fee for your tier, the local health, building, zoning, and fire approvals the application requires, written policies and procedures for the services you will sell, the training and screening systems, and a service agreement that states what the agency does and does not do. On the non-medical door, keep skilled tasks on the does not do side of that agreement, because the He-P 822 license authorizes no nursing and no therapy, and the boundary is the license. Hold services until the license issues and the DHHS inspection is behind you.

The practical warning

Four New Hampshire traps recur, and all four are reading errors rather than rule changes. The first is the individual registration trap: He-P 820 covers a person providing care by direct agreement, personally, and a business that employs caregivers files under He-P 822. The second is the client count tier: the fee is decided at exactly 10 clients, $25 below it and $250 at it, whatever a superseded form or a secondary guide says. The third is the two hospice fees: home hospice care providers pay $250 a year under He-P 823, and a hospice house pays $25 per licensed bed under He-P 824, and the two figures price two different licenses. The fourth is the imported gate: a home health filing sequenced around a certificate of need step that has not existed since RSA 151-C was repealed on June 30, 2016, or around a state enrollment pause that no source prints, wastes the one advantage this state offers, which is a short, direct path from a complete file to a license.

Do I need a license for non-medical home care in New Hampshire?

Yes, if you run an agency. An entity licensed as a home health care provider under He-P 809 is exempt from He-P 822 and delivers personal care under the 809 license.
