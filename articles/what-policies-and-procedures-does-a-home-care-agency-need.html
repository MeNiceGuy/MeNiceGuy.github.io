What Policies and Procedures Does a Home Care Agency Need? (The 18 Policies Surveyors Actually Check)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 12, 2026

The call I get most often from a new owner sounds calm for about ten seconds. Then the real sentence comes out: my application is almost ready, but my manual is not. When I ask what is in the manual so far, the answer is usually a binder or a folder borrowed from three different places, a few policies copied from a friend in another state, a few bought online, and gaps where the hard ones should be. That owner is not lazy. She is running into the part of starting an agency that nobody warns her about early enough: a licensing reviewer does not grade your intentions. A reviewer grades your documents, and then checks whether your caregivers actually work the way those documents say. A missing policy is a finding. A policy your staff have never seen is a worse one. In my walkthrough video I count eighteen policies a non-medical home care agency manual needs, in five sections. This article walks the same eighteen in story order, shows you what a reviewer checks against each one, and then proves the count against two states that publish their own lists, Illinois and Tennessee, so you can see exactly where your state layer sits on top of the baseline.

The front door: scope, rights, and admission

The manual opens where your agency opens, at the front door. Policy one is scope of services and service exclusions: what your agency does, and what it never does. That line matters because home care is non-medical. Companionship, homemaking, meal preparation, and hands-on personal care are in. Nursing tasks are out, and your policy has to route skilled needs to a licensed home health agency instead of stretching your caregivers into work they are not licensed to do. Policy two is client rights and responsibilities, the rights statement you hand every client at admission. Reviewers check that it exists, that clients actually receive it, and that any wording your state requires is in it. Policy three is intake, assessment, and admission, the path from the first phone call to a signed service agreement, done the same way every time. In the records I review, agencies rarely get cited for having no front door at all. They get cited because three clients came through three different versions of it.

Care delivery: the plan is the work order

Section two is care delivery, and it starts with policy four, care planning and service plan reviews. The written plan is the caregiver's work order for every visit, and reviewers compare visit notes against it. Care that does not match the plan is one of the fastest ways to get cited, because the mismatch is visible on the face of the file. Policy five sets activities of daily living scope boundaries and hands-on care limits, the daily task boundary for caregivers. This is the policy caregivers break most often by accident, usually while trying to be helpful, so what I tell owners is to train it with real scenarios, not just have staff read it once. Policy six is supervision and quality assurance visits, your proof that somebody with authority is overseeing care, documented on the schedule your policy promises. Policy seven is discharge and termination of services, how a client leaves your agency, clean and documented, with the notice period your state requires. A discharge handled kindly but documented nowhere reads to a reviewer the same as a discharge handled badly.

Your people: screening, training, and proof of competency

Section three is your people. Policy eight is hiring, background checks, and caregiver eligibility. Screening happens before client contact, not after the first shift. The rule I give every owner I advise is simple: no caregiver works alone in a client home until the personnel file is complete, no exceptions. Policy nine is orientation, training, and competency. Reviewers pull personnel files and look for training records and proof of competency, and for hands-on skills they expect competency verified by observation, not just a signature on a form. Policy ten is caregiver conduct, boundaries, and professional standards, the code of conduct every caregiver is held to. Policy eleven covers scheduling, timekeeping, and payroll practices, the unglamorous policy that keeps hours, assignments, and pay consistent instead of improvised. When a family's complaint and a caregiver's time record tell two different stories, this section is where a reviewer decides which story your agency can prove.

Safety and compliance: the policies read first after something goes wrong

Section four is safety and compliance. Policy twelve is incident reporting and investigation: what counts as an incident, who reports it, how fast, and how the investigation gets documented. Policy thirteen is abuse, neglect, and exploitation reporting. This one carries a legal duty. Your staff are mandatory reporters, the policy names your state reporting hotline and deadline, and that hotline number belongs posted where staff can see it, not buried in a binder. Policy fourteen is infection control and bloodborne pathogens, standard precautions built for a client home, not a hospital. Policy fifteen is emergency preparedness and business continuity, covering client emergencies and the day your office cannot operate. Policy sixteen is privacy, confidentiality, and information security: how client information is handled, shared, and locked down. Nobody reads these five policies on a quiet Tuesday. They get read, closely and in order, on the worst day of your agency's year, which is exactly why they have to match how your team actually responds.

Complaints and records: the trail a reviewer follows

Section five is client relations and records. Policy seventeen is complaints and grievances: how a client or a family member raises a concern, how you respond, and a hard rule that nobody gets punished for complaining. Policy eighteen is documentation, record retention, and record security: what gets documented, where it lives, and how long you keep it. Here is how a reviewer uses these two policies, and it is the test I walk owners through before any survey. The reviewer picks one complaint or one incident and follows it through your files: the log, the investigation, the follow-up, and the fix. If that trail goes cold anywhere, the gap is the citation. Your records are either your defense or your confession, and you decide which one every time a caregiver writes a note.

The state layer, verified: Illinois names seven groups, Tennessee lists seventeen items

Now the layer that trips up agencies using generic templates. There is no single national policy list for non-medical home care, because licensure is a state function and every state writes its own floor. Two states show the shape of that floor clearly, and both lists were re-read for this article. Illinois requires an initial applicant to submit copies of policies and procedures for seven groups under 77 Ill. Adm. Code 245.90(a)(3)(I): complaint resolution, employee health and safety, infection control, health care worker background check compliance together with mandated reporting compliance, supervisory visits, client records management, retention and release, and employee training. If your Illinois application packet is missing any one of those seven, the packet is incomplete before anyone reads your intentions. Tennessee writes the longer list. Tennessee Rule 0940-05-38-.06 requires a Personal Support Services Agency to maintain written policies covering seventeen items, from a description of services with enrollment and termination criteria, an organizational chart, and a plan for when the assigned worker is absent, through confidentiality, emergency plans, reporting and investigation of abuse or neglect and other critical incidents, communicable disease procedures, medication assistance, transportation, and cooperation with department investigations. The same chapter adds eight personnel policy topics in Rule 0940-05-38-.07(2), including workers age 18 or older, background checks, and Abuse Registry and sexual offender registry checks, and it requires documented competency in seven areas before a worker begins serving clients under Rule 0940-05-38-.08, including observing and reporting changes, abuse and neglect detection and reporting, service recipient rights, infection control, personal hygiene assistance, safety, and emergency procedures. Same work, two different state shapes. That is the point. And if your agency is Medicare certified home health rather than non-medical home care, a federal layer sits on top instead: the Conditions of Participation in 42 CFR Part 484 govern clinical records and the rest of certified agency operations, with clinical records retained for five years under section 484.110. Never copy a state rule from memory, and never borrow one from another state's manual. Verify it with your licensing body, and put your state's requirement into the policy in plain language.

The mistakes that get agencies cited

The mistakes I see most in the manuals owners send me for review are not exotic. Adopting a manual without customizing it, with the template brackets still sitting in the text. Guessing at state rules. Skipping the attorney review. Training by signature only, collecting acknowledgements from staff nobody actually taught. Promising response times your office cannot meet. And letting the manual go stale, adopted once and never reviewed again while the rules keep moving. Every one of those is a practice problem before it is a document problem, because the reviewer is not really grading the binder. The reviewer is grading whether the binder and the practice are the same thing.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give owners who are tempted to buy the cheapest manual they can find the week before they apply. A generic manual does not fail because it is generic. It fails at the eighteen state insertion points: training hours, supervisory visit frequency, reporting deadlines and hotline numbers, record retention periods, termination notice periods, and medication reminder rules. Those numbers differ by state, and a wrong number copied confidently is worse than a blank you still have to fill, because the wrong number trains your whole team to do the wrong thing on schedule. Build the manual before you need it, while your application is pending, and build it from your state rule first and the general baseline second.

Your next step

This week, do the count. Print your state licensing rule's policy list, the way Illinois prints its seven groups and Tennessee prints its seventeen items, and check your manual against it line by line, then check one complaint or incident file against your complaints and records policies to see whether the trail holds.
