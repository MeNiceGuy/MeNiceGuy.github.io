How to Start a Home Care Agency in Missouri (No License for Private Pay Care, a Contract for Medicaid)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Missouri story starts with a founder shopping for a license the state does not sell. She had a personal care plan for families around St. Louis, a caregiver bench, and a budget line for the home care license application fee she had seen in a national guide. Missouri issues no license for private pay non-medical home care. The Department of Health and Senior Services licenses home health agencies only, and an agency below the skilled threshold has no license to apply for. When owners come to me with a Missouri plan, the first job is to split the doors cleanly: business formation for private pay, a state contract for Medicaid personal care, a license for skilled home health, and a certificate, not a license, for hospice.

Missouri home-based care runs on those separate doors, and one of them is not a license at all. The Medicaid door for non-medical care is the in-home services contract under 19 CSR 15-7.021, run through the state's provider enrollment system. Skilled home health is the home health agency license issued by the Department of Health and Senior Services, called DHSS, under sections 197.400 to 197.478, RSMo and 19 CSR 30-26.010. Hospice is the certificate issued under sections 197.250 to 197.280. No one door covers another door's services. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 1.23 million seniors, nearly one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Missouri. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 6,270,541 as of July 1, 2025, and 19.6 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.23 million seniors, nearly one in five people in the state. The same Census QuickFacts page for Missouri puts median household income at $70,702 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

Missouri also carries a large Medicaid home care footprint, because the aged, blind, and disabled populations that use most home-based Medicaid services are served fee-for-service through MO HealthNet rather than through managed care plans. That is why the contract door in the next sections matters as much as the private pay door: in Missouri, the second payer is not an afterthought, it is a separate filing with its own standards and, right now, its own pause.

The license question, answered directly: no Missouri license for private pay non-medical care

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Missouri? For private pay personal care, homemaker, and companion services, no Missouri license exists to get. The doors that exist are business formation for private pay, and the Medicaid in-home services contract for MO HealthNet clients. A provider of Medicaid personal care and chore services contracts with the state by written proposal, meets the in-home services standards in 19 CSR 15-7.021, a certified manager, registered nurse supervision, Family Care Safety Registry checks under section 192.2495, and staff training, and holds a participation agreement with the Department of Social Services under 13 CSR 70-91.010. Approval as a Title XX in-home services provider comes first; the Title XIX participation agreement follows it. DHSS authorizes each client's care through its Division of Senior and Disability Services. Effective September 16, 2026, provider enrollment administration sits with the MO HealthNet Division, and procedures continue unchanged.

Consumer Directed Services is a separate Medicaid model, not the agency model: under sections 208.900 to 208.918 and 19 CSR 15-8.400, the participant is the employer and the vendor runs fiscal and support functions. An agency in-home services provider employs its own workers under 19 CSR 15-7.021. The two models file, hire, and bill differently. The residential models in the generator, DMH group homes and supported living settings, residential care and assisted living facilities, nursing facilities, ICF/IID settings, and the other all-homes tracks, each carry their own Missouri credential, and none of them is a home care door.

The Medicaid lane: the in-home services contract, its standards, and the current pause

No fee figure for the in-home services contract or for MO HealthNet provider enrollment was located in the primary sources checked for the packet, so the fee line for this door reads NO FEE PUBLISHED, and any fee figure for this door in a secondary guide is unverified. The standards are the real cost of the door: a manager who completes the certification course the rule requires, registered nurse supervision of the personal care program, Family Care Safety Registry checks before assignment for every worker, and the staff training sections the rule sets, orientation and service-specific training. Build that record before the contract proposal, so the review is a submission exercise.

The pause that runs this track has to be read exactly. Effective August 1, 2026, a temporary moratorium pauses new provider enrollments for Durable Medical Equipment, Prosthetics, Orthotics, and Supplies and for Consumer Directed Services (CDS) / Personal Care, for an initial period of six months that may be extended in six-month increments up to one year, statewide, which puts the initial period running to about February 1, 2027. Applications received before August 1, 2026 are not affected, actively enrolled providers are not affected, and revalidations are not affected. The pause does not touch the DHSS licenses in this article, and private pay work is not an enrollment and is not paused. One uncertainty has to be carried plainly: the pause notice is scoped to the CDS / Personal Care provider type, and whether new agency-model in-home services enrollments are included is not stated in the notice. The packet's instruction is to confirm with the MO HealthNet Division Provider Enrollment before sequencing an agency-model filing date, and that confirmation, not a hope about scope, is what a launch date should rest on.

The skilled lane: the home health agency license at $600, renewed annually

The moment your plan reaches the skilled threshold, Missouri licenses it. No home health agency may operate without a license issued by DHSS under section 197.405. A licensed agency offers at least two skilled services on an intermittent basis, one of which must be skilled nursing; an agency offering one skilled service, or no skilled nursing, is below the licensure threshold and does not hold this license. The application is filed with the Bureau of Home Care and Rehabilitative Standards with the secretary of state registration, the State Disclosure of Ownership and Control Interest Statement, and evidence that the agency has established the policies and procedures the statute requires. The fee is $600 under section 197.410, nonrefundable, and the license renews annually on the application and a $600 nonrefundable renewal fee filed before the license expiration date, with compliance evidenced by a survey inspection under 19 CSR 30-26.010. The licensure rule incorporates the Medicare Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR Part 484, so licensure surveys run to Medicare standards and Part 484 is the operating standard for the licensed agency, not only for the certified one.

Medicare certification layers on this license through Form CMS-855A in PECOS to CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for Missouri, with the CY2026 institutional enrollment fee of $750. Initial Medicare enrollment is blocked under the federal moratorium extended and modified by CMS-6101-N2, effective November 12, 2026, with no calendar end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). The state license sequence runs on its own clock while the federal door is gated.

The hospice lane: a certificate under sections 197.250 to 197.280, never a license

Missouri issues a hospice certificate, not a hospice license, and the packet never calls the credential a license. No person may provide hospice care or hold out as a hospice without the certificate, and a hospice that exacts no charge is exempt. The fee is set by DHSS with input from the hospice advisory council on a sliding scale, minimum $250 and maximum $750, under section 197.254; the statute prints a scale, not one flat figure, and the $600 home health fee does not carry into hospice. On application and fee, DHSS surveys the program before the certificate issues, and the certificate is posted as the statute directs. Renewal runs at least once every twelve months, with an application not less than thirty days before any change in ownership or management, under section 197.256. The Medicare component runs on Form CMS-855A to CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15, with the CY2026 federal fee of $750 under 42 CFR Part 418, gated for initial enrollment by CMS-6102-N2 on the same federal terms as the home health gate.

Background checks: the Family Care Safety Registry, before assignment

Missouri's screening system for home care workers is the Family Care Safety Registry under section 192.2495, RSMo, and in Missouri that registry name is the correct one. The packet builds the personnel file around a registry result completed before assignment for every worker, alongside license or credential verification for each licensed class, orientation and training records by topic and date, competency evaluation for each assigned service, and the supervision and annual evaluation record. Workers assigned before registry screening and training records were complete and filed are a named deficiency in the packet, because the in-home services standards and the licensure frame both read the file that way at survey. Electronic visit verification is a condition of payment for the Medicaid personal care services the federal frame covers, and the visit record, the plan DHSS authorized, and the claim must agree.

What you still do today: form the business the Missouri way

Form the entity and complete the secretary of state registration, hold the EIN, and put insurance in place before the first client. Then build the operating record in the order the doors test it: Family Care Safety Registry screening for every worker, the worker training system, the service plans, and the policies the applicable frame requires, the in-home services standards for the contract door or sections 197.400 to 197.478 for the license door. Managed care in Missouri runs through Healthy Blue, Home State Health, and UnitedHealthcare Community Plan, plus Show Me Healthy Kids for children and youth in foster care and adoption or guardianship subsidy, but members who are aged, blind, or disabled are served fee-for-service, which is where most home-based Medicaid volume in this article sits. State enrollment establishes fee-for-service participation only; managed care takes a separate credentialing application and contract with each plan.

The practical warning

Three Missouri traps cost new owners money. The first is buying a license that does not exist: a guide that sells a Missouri home care license application is selling a door the state does not print, and the reverse error, skipping the in-home services standards because private pay needs no license, leaves the Medicaid door unbuilt. The second trap is reading the enrollment pause as a license moratorium, or as nothing. The pause gates new Medicaid personal care enrollments only; it does not pause DHSS licensure or certification, and a licensed agency gains no Medicaid personal care door from its license while the pause runs. It also carries the open scope question on agency-model in-home services, so confirm with the MO HealthNet Division before promising a start date. The third trap is the wrong MAC desk: Missouri home health and hospice enrollment goes to CGS Administrators, Jurisdiction 15. Wisconsin Physicians Service, Jurisdiction 5, is the A/B MAC, and Noridian Healthcare Solutions, Jurisdiction D, is the DME MAC and is never the enrollment desk for these tracks.
