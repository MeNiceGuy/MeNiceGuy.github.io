How to Start a Home Care Agency in New York (The $2,000 License and the Need Test You Have to Pass First)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 5, 2026

The New York story usually starts with a founder doing the math that works in almost every other state. She counts the seniors, checks what families pay for care, adds up the application fee, and starts building the business. Then she hits the step no other state in this series puts in front of her. New York does not just ask whether your paperwork is complete. It asks whether your county needs another agency at all, and if the county already has five or more agencies actively serving patients, the state presumes the answer is no. The $2,000 application fee in Public Health Law section 3605 gets your application reviewed. The need case is what gets it approved. An owner who spends the fee before checking her county has the order exactly backwards. This article walks the real path in the order the state checks it.

The demand: about 3.86 million seniors in one state

Start with why owners keep choosing New York despite the gate. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 20,002,427 as of July 1, 2025, and 19.3 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 3.86 million seniors, one of the largest senior markets in the country. The same Census QuickFacts page for New York puts median household income at $85,974 for 2020 to 2024. Millions of seniors, spread from New York City and Long Island through the Hudson Valley, the Capital District, and the upstate metros, in a state where a large share of families can carry private pay care.

The price families face explains the revenue side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for New York, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $80,080, up 2 percent, ranked 26th among states. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $35 an hour. That updates the figure in my video, which used the 2024 survey. The point stands either way: one full time client in New York represents a substantial annual contract, which is why the license work below is worth doing in the right order instead of fast.

The license: the LHCSA, and the second path most private pay founders do not take

New York licenses this business through the New York State Department of Health. The private pay path is the Licensed Home Care Services Agency, the LHCSA, under Public Health Law section 3605. No home care services agency providing nursing services, home health aide services, or personal care services may operate without that license, and the commissioner may not issue one without the written approval of the Public Health and Health Planning Council, usually shortened to the PHHPC. The application fee is set in the statute itself at $2,000, payable to the Department of Health.

The second path is the Certified Home Health Agency, the CHHA, under Public Health Law section 3606. A CHHA is built for part time, intermittent skilled care and is tied to Medicare and Medicaid certification, and its establishment application fee is also $2,000. Most founders reading this article want the LHCSA. The CHHA is a different business, with a different approval and certification load, and choosing between them is a business model decision, not a paperwork preference. Either way, approval runs through the PHHPC. There is no walk-in license in New York.

The gate: the public need test, county by county

Here is the step that makes New York New York. Applications are open. The processing moratorium that froze new LHCSA applications ended March 31, 2020, and in August 2022 the Department of Health released the current licensure application and guidance, with applications filed electronically through the NYSE-CON system. But every new application is reviewed on three grounds: public need, financial feasibility, and character and competence.

The need rule sits in 10 NYCRR section 765-1.16. Each county is its own planning area. If a planning area has five or more LHCSAs actively serving patients, there is a rebuttable presumption of no need for another agency in that county. Actively serving, in the Department survey behind the county report, means an agency serving at least 25 unduplicated patients in the county. The Department of Health County Presumption of No Need Report does the counting for you. On the most recent survey results published in that report, from 2022, 20 counties are listed as presumed no need, including all five New York City boroughs, Nassau, Suffolk, Westchester, Erie, Monroe, and Onondaga, and 42 counties are listed as presumed need, mostly rural and upstate. Two things follow. First, check the current report before you spend anything, because the Department can update the survey and the list. Second, presumed no need is not the same as no. The presumption is rebuttable, but you rebut it with data: demographics, waiting lists, the number and capacity of agencies already operating, and the quality of the services they provide, which are the factors section 765-1.16(d) names. Enthusiasm is not evidence.

Two routes sit outside the need test, and both matter to a serious applicant. Under section 765-1.16(c)(2), an application based on a change of ownership of an LHCSA actively serving at least 25 patients is not subject to public need review. It is evaluated on financial feasibility and the character and competence of the proposed operator, unless the new operator wants to serve patients outside the approved planning area. Buying a working agency is therefore a real path into a no need county, with its own diligence load. And under section 765-1.16(c)(3), the need determination does not include LHCSAs affiliated with an Assisted Living Program, a Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly, a Nurse Family Partnership, or a Continuing Care Retirement Community, the restricted agencies that serve only their own program populations.

The package: what the PHHPC review actually weighs

Once the county question is answered, the application itself is the work. Applications are submitted through NYSE-CON, and the Department LHCSA application requirements FAQ and the Department LHCSA page are the current sources to build from. Financial feasibility is a real test, not a formality: the Department reviews your sources of available working capital, with a minimum expectation of at least two months of estimated operating expenses, alongside your projected operating costs. Character and competence review reaches every owner through a detailed questionnaire. And note the sequence on the Department page itself: policy and procedure manuals are submitted after the LHCSA application has been approved by the PHHPC and the Department, not with the first filing. An applicant who pays for a full manual build before the need and feasibility review has spent money in the wrong order.

On timing, I will tell you what I tell every owner who asks: the Department publishes no guaranteed processing timeline for a new LHCSA, and no consultant can give you one honestly. The state controls that clock. Plan your capital so the business can wait, because an application built to survive a long review beats an application built on a promised date.

Life under the license: registration every year, an RN over patient care, and fingerprint based checks

The license has no set expiration term, but it is not a file and forget asset. Under Public Health Law section 3605-b, every LHCSA must register with the commissioner every year, and an agency that is not registered may not operate or be reimbursed. The teeth are specific. A LHCSA that misses the registration deadline owes a late fee of $500 for each month, or part of a month, it is in default, and unpaid late fees block the next registration. The Department shall institute proceedings to revoke the license of any LHCSA that fails to register for two annual registration periods, whether or not those periods are consecutive, and it may also pursue revocation over a pattern of late registration across multiple years. Put the registration date on the calendar the day the license arrives. It is the cheapest requirement in this article and the one that can cost you the license.

Clinical leadership is set in the minimum standards at 10 NYCRR Part 766. Under section 766.9(h), the agency must employ at least one licensed and currently registered professional nurse, with qualifications the agency deems appropriate for the duties, who is responsible for the direction and supervision of all patient care services and other health care activities of the agency. No RN in that role, no patient care. That hire shapes your operating budget from day one, which is another reason the feasibility numbers have to be real.

Hiring runs through the criminal history record check system. Article 36 agencies are subject providers under the Department CHRC program, governed by 10 NYCRR section 402.4: the checks are fingerprint based, the agency must keep written policies and procedures for running them, employees work under supervision while a check is pending, and a person with a negative determination is removed from direct care. Build that sequence into your hiring process before the first aide, the same as every other state in this series, because a caregiver in a home before the check clears is a compliance problem, not a staffing solution.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every New York founder, and it is the whole article in one line: do not spend the $2,000 until the county report says your county is worth applying in. The most expensive mistake in New York is not a bad application. It is a well built application filed into a presumed no need county with no rebuttal evidence behind it, by an owner who treated the need test as a rumor. The quieter errors cost owners here too: budgeting working capital below the two months of operating expenses the feasibility review expects, buying a finished policy manual before PHHPC approval when the Department takes manuals after approval, and, after licensure, treating annual registration as paperwork instead of the two strike revocation risk section 3605-b makes it. New York is a hard state because the gate is judgment, not just forms. It is a fair state for owners who read the county report first and build the case the rule actually asks for.

You start with the Licensed Home Care Services Agency license (LHCSA). The packet lays out 8 steps. What are the New York home care license requirements?

Home care is licensed by the New York State Department of Health (DOH) under Public Health Law Article 36. The Licensed Home Care Services Agency (LHCSA) license at PHL 3605 is the license for an agency that provides nursing, home health aide, personal care, or therapy services in client homes, whether the payer is Medicaid, a plan, or the client. Check the free New York preview checklist for the need review and filing requirements.

How much does a home care license cost in New York?

The LHCSA application fee is $2,000 under PHL 3605(13). It is paid by check payable to the New York State Department of Health with the NYSE-CON number on the check, within 30 days of filing, and it is non-refundable once the application is acknowledged. A $5,000 figure circulates in secondary guides, but the statute prints $2,000 and controls. The free New York preview prints the full fee table from the state's own schedule.

Do I need a license for non-medical home care in New York?

Yes. Non-medical home care provided by an agency needs the Licensed Home Care Services Agency license (LHCSA). New York has no separate non-medical home care license: PHL 3605(1) reaches private pay work too, and the LHCSA covers personal care services in client homes. Medicare billing uses the CHHA certificate instead, not this license. Review the free New York preview to confirm the track for your plan.

Your next step

Work in the order the state checks you. Open the Department county report and find your county: presumed need, or presumed no need. If it is presumed need, build the application package: the NYSE-CON filing, working capital covering at least two months of estimated operating expenses, projected operating costs, and the owner character and competence questionnaires, with the $2,000 fee. If it is presumed no need, decide honestly whether you have data that rebuts the presumption, or whether the change of ownership route into an agency already serving 25 or more patients is the better door. Line up the registered nurse who will direct and supervise patient care, and set up the fingerprint based CHRC process and the annual registration calendar before the first client, not after. An owner who files in that order spends the review proving a needed agency, not explaining an unneeded one.
