How to Start a Home Care Agency in Alabama (No Home Health License Exists, the Door Is a Certificate of Need)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Alabama story starts with a founder who did everything in the order a generic guide gave her. She formed the company, priced insurance, and then sat down to file for the home health license, because every checklist she had found put the license first. Weeks later she was still hunting for an application that Alabama never built. I see this pattern in the records I review: the delay is rarely effort, it is a map drawn for another state. Alabama does not license home health agencies, and it does not license private pay non-medical home care either. The doors that do exist are specific, and they sit in different agencies. Here is the full picture, door by door, in the order a founder actually meets it.

Hold the headline first, because it decides the budget. For personal care, homemaker, and companion services paid out of pocket, there is no Alabama application to file. For skilled care in the home, the state door is a certificate of need, not a license. For hospice, there is a real license, with a real fee and a December 31 expiry. An owner who sorts those three sentences before spending sorts Alabama correctly.

The demand: about 1.01 million seniors, close to one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Alabama. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 5,193,088 as of July 1, 2025, and 19.4 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.01 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Alabama puts median household income at $63,999 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into. That senior count is the population figure multiplied by the age 65 and over share, and I label it as that math because the Census page publishes the two inputs, not the product.

Demand alone does not open a door, and in Alabama the payer mix matters as much as the headcount. Alabama Medicaid is primarily fee-for-service, and its home and community-based waiver services run through named operating agencies, covered below. A founder who plans only for private pay and a founder who plans for waiver clients are building two different filings on the same business, and the second filing starts with the operating agency for the waiver served, not with a license application.

The license question, answered directly: no license for non-medical care, no license for home health, a license for hospice

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a home care agency in Alabama? For private pay non-medical care, no state license exists to get. For home health, the answer is also no license, and the rule says so in terms: Ala. Admin. Code r. 410-2-4-.07 states there is no licensure requirement for home health agencies in Alabama. Market entry for a home health agency runs on a certificate of need from the State Health Planning and Development Agency (SHPDA) under section 22-21-265 of the Code of Alabama, with the Alabama Department of Public Health (ADPH), Bureau of Health Provider Standards, as the Medicare and Medicaid certification survey agency.

Hospice is the exception that proves the structure. Hospices are licensed by the State Board of Health through ADPH under Ala. Admin. Code chapter 420-5-17, and a hospice also sits inside the certificate of need frame. Residential models are separate doors again: the Department of Mental Health certifies community programs, including group homes for developmental disabilities under rule 580-5-30, ADPH licenses assisted living facilities under chapter 420-5-4 at $240 plus $18 for each bed, and the Department of Human Resources licenses residential child care. No one door covers another door's services, and the packet for Alabama is built track by track for exactly that reason.

The non-medical lane: no license for private pay, waiver enrollment for Medicaid

The non-medical track covers personal care, homemaker, companion, and respite help delivered in the client home. Alabama issues no license for the private pay form of this work, so the startup sequence is business formation, insurance, screening and training systems, service plans, and the operating policies the waiver standards require, built before any enrollment filing. Private pay clients need no enrollment at all. Keep the private pay record and the Medicaid record separate, so a payer audit reads cleanly.

The Medicaid door runs through Chapter 107 of the Alabama Medicaid Provider Manual. Each waiver names its operating agency: the Elderly and Disabled and Alabama Community Transition waivers sit with the Department of Senior Services, the State of Alabama Independent Living (SAIL) Waiver with the Department of Rehabilitation Services, and the Intellectual Disabilities, Living at Home, and Community Waiver Program waivers with the Department of Mental Health, while Technology Assisted Waiver enrollment runs directly through Gainwell Technologies. The agency applies with the operating agency for the waiver it serves, then completes Alabama Medicaid provider enrollment processed by Gainwell, which approves, returns, or denies each application within ten business days of receipt. No fee figure for this door was located in the primary sources checked, so the packet lists it as NO FEE PUBLISHED. Electronic visit verification applies to the Medicaid personal care visits the federal frame covers, on a provider choice model, with visit data flowing to the state aggregator.

The skilled lane: home health enters on a certificate of need, not a license

A home health agency in Alabama is an organization primarily engaged in providing skilled nursing services and other therapeutic services. The moment your plan includes skilled nursing or therapy, the SHPDA door controls market entry. The sequence opens with the letter of intent the certificate of need rules require ahead of the application, on the SHPDA review cycle and batching calendar for home health. The application is filed under Ala. Admin. Code 410-1-7-.06 with a nonrefundable fee of one percent of the estimated cost of the proposed service, minimum $3,500, maximum $26,143 under the SHPDA memorandum effective October 1, 2025. Older maximum figures in secondary guides are superseded by that indexed memorandum.

While the review runs, build the governance and clinical record: governance, administrator, clinical supervision, personnel qualifications and licensure, background screening, clinical records, and the policies the federal Conditions of Participation require. ADPH then surveys for Medicare and Medicaid certification. Medicaid home health enrollment under Chapter 17 of the Provider Manual requires Medicare certification, ADPH certification, and an EVV system secured before enrollment, with the EVV Attestation Form filed. A home health provider that skips the attestation files an incomplete application, and an incomplete attestation means the application is denied.

The certified and hospice lanes: the federal layer and the one real license

Medicare certification builds on the certificate of need position for the service area; it is a federal layer on that position, not a substitute for it. Enrollment runs on Form CMS-855A through PECOS with the federal application fee of $750 for calendar year 2026, routed to Palmetto GBA, Jurisdiction M for the Home Health and Hospice desk, with Jurisdiction J as the A/B desk. Read the federal gate before timing the filing: CMS-6101-N2 extends and modifies the moratorium on initial home health enrollment, effective November 12, 2026, with no end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). That federal gate reaches initial Medicare enrollment only. It does not govern Alabama Medicaid, and it does not close the state certificate of need door.

Hospice holds the one home-based license Alabama issues. File the initial license application with ADPH under chapter 420-5-17, confirm the SHPDA position for the service area first, and build the interdisciplinary group structure, plan of care systems, bereavement and volunteer programs, and inpatient arrangements where offered. The corrected fee line matters here: the fee is $240 for in-home hospice, and $240 plus $6 per bed over the first ten beds for inpatient hospice. Renewal is also $240, bed increases for an inpatient unit price at $240 plus $6 for each net gain bed, and the renewal late penalty is $300 or 100 percent of the renewal fee, whichever is greater. Licenses run for the calendar year and expire December 31. Medicare hospice certification runs separately under 42 CFR Part 418, gated for initial enrollment by CMS-6102-N2 on the same federal terms.

Background checks and the worker file: screening before the first assignment

Alabama survey findings in the records I review cluster in the personnel file, and the packet builds the file in a fixed order: application and role description for the position held, license or certification verification where the role requires one, background screening results filed before the first assignment, orientation and training records with dates, competency evaluation for the services assigned, annual performance review, health screening records the program requires, and signed confidentiality and conduct acknowledgments. The common deficiencies repeat across tracks: visit notes that do not match the authorized plan, personnel files missing screening or training records at survey, plans of care unsigned or past their review date, EVV records missing for visits that were billed, and incident reports filed without documented follow-up.

Treat that list as the operating standard for every lane, including private pay. No license does not mean no standard. In a state with no licensure gate for the non-medical door, your screening file, your training records, your insurance certificate, and your policies are the proof of seriousness that a license provides automatically in other states, and referral sources ask for that proof before they send a family to an agency they cannot check against a license roster.

What you still do today: form the business the Alabama way

Form the entity, get the EIN from the IRS, and put liability insurance in place before the first client. Then stand up the ordinary employer load in the right order: written policies and procedures covering the services you will actually sell, caregiver training and background screening set up as a system rather than a scramble, and a service agreement that says clearly what your agency does and does not do, with skilled and medical tasks on the does not do side of the line for a non-medical agency. Pull every form from the regulator on the day of filing; a form printed from a third-party site is not evidence of the current version. I print no Alabama formation fee here because no formation fee figure sits in the primary sources this article is built from, and an invented figure helps no one.

The practical warning

Three Alabama traps cost new owners money. The first is the license that does not exist: guides that list an Alabama home health license, or an Alabama home care license, describe doors Alabama never built, and weeks spent hunting for those applications are weeks lost. The second is the statute fee trap: the $200 plus $15 per bed text at section 22-21-24 is the assisted living and specialty care fee text, not a home health or hospice fee, and the ADPH schedule figure of $240 is the operative hospice figure. The third is record merging: ADPH certification survey work and Medicaid enrollment are separate records, and holding one while billing on the other is the classic Alabama file error. Certification is not enrollment, in this state or any other.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a home care agency in Alabama?

You start by naming the door, because Alabama has no single home care license. Private pay non-medical care (personal care, homemaker, and companion services) has no state license; you form the business and build the operating record. Medicaid waiver work adds enrollment through the operating agency for the waiver served plus Alabama Medicaid enrollment through Gainwell. Skilled home health enters on the SHPDA certificate of need, and hospice files the ADPH license under chapter 420-5-17. What are the Alabama home care license requirements?

Alabama home-based care runs on separate doors. There is no license for private pay non-medical care and no home health agency license at all (Ala. Admin. Code r. 410-2-4-.07). Home health market entry is the SHPDA certificate of need under section 22-21-265, with ADPH as the certification survey agency. Hospice is licensed by the State Board of Health through ADPH under chapter 420-5-17. Medicaid waiver services enroll through the operating agency for the waiver served, plus Gainwell enrollment. No one door covers another door's services.

How much does a home care license cost in Alabama?

The certificate of need filing fee is 1 percent of estimated project cost, minimum $3,500, maximum $26,143 effective October 1, 2025. The hospice license fee is $240 for in-home hospice, and $240 plus $6 per bed over the first ten beds for inpatient hospice. The federal CMS-855A fee is $750 for 2026 on the certified tracks. Do I need a license for non-medical home care in Alabama?

No. Alabama issues no license for private pay personal care, homemaker, and companion services, and no application exists to file for that model.
