How to Price Private-Pay Home Care (The Math, Step by Step)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 14, 2026

She was busy, and she was broke, and both facts had the same cause. When I reviewed her numbers, the schedule was not the problem. She had clients, her caregivers were working, and the phone was ringing. The problem was sitting in the first decision she had made as an owner, months before the first client signed. She had set her hourly rate by looking at what two agencies across town charged and picking a number a little below theirs, because she wanted to be competitive while she was new. What she never did was run her own costs through that rate. Her caregivers cost more per hour than she thought once payroll taxes and insurance were added. Her office costs sat on top of every billable hour whether she counted them or not. And the margin she believed she had built in was a markup, not a margin, which is a smaller number wearing a bigger number's clothes. By the time the math caught up with her, she was funding the gap out of her own pocket, one payroll at a time. In the video below I walk the pricing method step by step, the same method I give owners who are setting a rate before they launch or fixing one that is quietly losing money: add your fully loaded caregiver cost to your overhead spread per hour, then divide by one minus your target margin. This article walks that math in story order, with the wage, tax, and market figures re-verified against primary sources, so you can run your own numbers before a family ever asks what you charge.

Watch this walkthrough on YouTube

Step one: price off the loaded cost, never the wage

Step one is the caregiver cost, and it has to be fully loaded. The wage is only the first layer. On top of it sit the employer payroll taxes, workers compensation insurance, unemployment taxes, and any benefits or paid time off you offer. The federal floor of that burden is not a guess: the Internal Revenue Service, in Topic No. 751, puts the employer share of Social Security at 6.2 percent of wages and the employer share of Medicare at 1.45 percent, 7.65 percent together before workers compensation, unemployment taxes, or a single hour of paid time off enters the picture. Ground the wage layer in a real figure too: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook puts the May 2025 median pay for home health and personal care aides at $35,800 a year, $17.21 an hour, and plenty of local markets sit above that median. The worked example in the video uses round illustrative numbers so the method is easy to follow: a caregiver paid $15 an hour, with payroll taxes, workers compensation, and a small paid time off allowance added, lands at a fully loaded cost of about $20 an hour. That $20, not the $15 wage, is the number that goes into the pricing math. If you price off the wage alone, every hour you bill quietly loses the difference, and no amount of new clients fixes a rate that loses money by the hour. It only makes the loss bigger, faster.

Step two: turn weekly hours into monthly billable hours

Step two converts your weekly billable hours into monthly billable hours, because overhead arrives monthly and the two figures have to match before they can be combined. Take your target billable hours per week, multiply by 52 weeks, and divide by 12 months. At 40 billable hours a week, that comes out to about 173 billable hours a month. Use billable hours here, not scheduled hours. An hour a caregiver is scheduled but no client pays for carries no overhead at all, and counting it flatters the math while the bills stay real. This is also the step where new owners see the launch problem clearly: at low billable hours, every fixed cost in the business gets spread across very few paid hours, which is why a rate that works at 200 billable hours a week can be impossible at 40, with the exact same caregiver wage.

Step three: spread your overhead across those hours

Step three spreads your overhead across those billable hours. Overhead is everything that does not change when one more hour of care is delivered: rent, software, insurance, office help, and marketing. In the video's worked example, monthly overhead is $5,000. Divide $5,000 by about 173 monthly billable hours and you get about $28.85 of overhead sitting on top of every billable hour. Read that number carefully, because it is the lesson of the whole article in one figure. At a small census, overhead per hour can be larger than the loaded caregiver cost itself. The owner in the opening story had never seen her version of this number. Once she did, the conversation changed from how to charge less than the agency across town to how to get more billable hours under the overhead she already had, and which overhead lines she could cut without cutting the service.

Step four: add them together for your true cost per hour

Step four adds the two layers together to get your true cost per hour. The loaded labor cost for the month is $20 an hour times about 173 billable hours, about $3,467. Add the $5,000 of overhead and total monthly cost is about $8,467. Divide that by the same 173 billable hours and your true cost is about $48.85 for every hour you sell. Notice what this step does to the competitor comparison she started with. A rate copied from across town, in the low to mid $30s in many markets, does not come close to covering a $48.85 true cost at this volume. That does not automatically mean the right answer is to charge far above the market. It means the inputs have to move: more billable hours under the same overhead, lower overhead, a different service mix, or some honest combination of the three. The math does not set your price by itself. It tells you which levers are real.

Step five: divide for margin, do not add for markup

Step five is the margin math, and this is where most owners get it wrong. A 25 percent margin does not mean adding 25 percent to your cost. Margin is a percent of revenue, not a percent of cost. So you divide your true cost per hour by one minus your target margin: $48.85 divided by 0.75 gives about $65.13 an hour. That is the minimum rate at which a 25 percent margin is mathematically possible on these illustrative costs. Below it, the margin you want cannot happen, no matter how hard the month goes. Rounding up is fine and usually smart: rounding $65.13 up to $66 an hour gives a real margin of about 26 percent, and the revenue picture becomes visible. At $66 an hour times about 173 billable hours, the agency bills about $11,440 a month, about $137,280 a year. As a matter of professional judgment, most private pay agencies I work with aim for a gross margin in the 20 to 30 percent range. Below that range, one bad month can eat the year. Above it, you may be pricing yourself out of your local market. Pick the number your costs and your market both allow, and then defend it instead of apologizing for it.

The market check: a sanity check, not your price

Every rate needs one sanity check against the market, and the verified benchmark is this: the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, released March 2, 2026 by Genworth and CareScout from more than 25,000 provider rates gathered between July and November 2025, puts the national median for non-medical caregiver services at $35 an hour, up 3 percent from the year before, which comes to $80,080 a year at the survey's 44 hours a week basis. Use that $35 only as a sanity check, not as your price. State medians inside that survey run far above and far below the national figure, and your costs are your costs. If your math says $66 and the national median says $35, that gap is a signal to examine your billable hours, your overhead, and your service mix, exactly as step four showed. It is not a signal to throw the math away and copy the median. The agencies I see get hurt are not the ones whose rate looked high next to a survey. They are the ones who charged the survey number with costs the survey never saw.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every owner setting a first rate. Do not discount your way out of a slow month. When census drops, cutting the rate shrinks revenue while overhead stays exactly where it was, which is the fastest way to make a slow month expensive. The fix for a slow month is more billable hours, not a cheaper hour. And revisit this math at least twice a year. Wages move, insurance renews, and your billable hours shift with census, so a rate that was right in January can be wrong by July, and you usually do not feel it until the bank account tells you. Run the five steps once with your hoped-for numbers, then run them again with your honest numbers. In the records I review, owners who do the second run find one of three things: the loaded labor cost was higher than they thought, overhead per hour was eating the margin, or the rate was set by copying a competitor instead of by their own costs. All three are fixable, but only before the rate is printed, quoted, and defended to families for a year.

Your next step

This week, before you quote another rate, run your own five steps. Write down your real caregiver wage, load it with the employer payroll taxes, workers compensation, unemployment taxes, and paid time off you actually carry, convert your real weekly billable hours to monthly hours, spread your real monthly overhead across them, and divide by one minus the margin you intend to keep. Compare the result to the $35 an hour CareScout national median only as a check, then decide which input you control: hours, overhead, or service mix.
