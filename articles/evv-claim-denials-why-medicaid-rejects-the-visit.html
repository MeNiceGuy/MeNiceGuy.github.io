EVV Claim Denials: Why Medicaid Rejects the Visit, and the Fix Sequence That Stops It

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 17, 2026

The claim looked clean. The service was authorized, the caregiver showed up, the work got done, and Medicaid still denied it. In the denials I review for agency owners, the care is almost never the problem. The visit record is. The claim said one thing, the electronic visit verification record said another, and the state paid neither. Owners call this a billing problem and start by arguing with the claim. That is the wrong end of the chain. The denial was decided earlier, at the moment the visit was captured, and the fix has to run in the order the records actually travel. This article walks that chain in story order: the federal rule underneath every EVV record, how your state aggregator matches a visit to a claim, the six field fights that cause most denials, and the five step sequence that stops them. If you are still deciding whether EVV applies to your agency at all, that answer is here too, because a private pay only agency sits outside the federal mandate and a Medicaid agency does not get to opt out.

The rule underneath the record: six data points, no exceptions

Section 12006 of the 21st Century Cures Act added section 1903(l) to the Social Security Act and required states to electronically verify Medicaid personal care visits and home health visits that require an in-home visit. Federal law names six data points that every verified visit has to carry. One, the type of service performed. Two, the individual receiving the service. Three, the date of the service. Four, the location of service delivery. Five, the individual providing the service. Six, the time the service begins and the time it ends. CMS repeats those six points word for word on its MES Certification Repository EVV outcomes page, and it defines a complete EVV record as one carrying all of them. Those six points are not suggestions. Your state built its whole claim edit around them, and a record missing any one of them is an incomplete record for that visit.

Two scope points matter before anything else. First, the mandate is a Medicaid rule. It reaches Medicaid personal care services and home health care services. A private pay only agency, with no Medicaid visits, sits outside the federal EVV mandate, though a state contract or a payer agreement can still impose its own verification terms. Second, the federal law does not pick your system. Each state designates its own EVV system or aggregator and its own visit maintenance process, so the screens, the rounding rule, and the correction path in your state are the ones that govern your claims, not a national standard and not another state's workflow.

Follow the visit: where the match happens

Now follow the chain your visit travels. Your caregiver clocks in and out on a phone app, a landline system, or another device your state approves. That record lands in your EVV system. From there it moves to your state aggregator, the clearinghouse platform your state or its contractor runs. Names like Sandata, HHAeXchange, Netsmart, and AuthentiCare sit in that seat in a lot of states. The aggregator checks the visit record. Then your claim goes in, and the claim gets matched against the verified visit. If the two do not agree, the claim pends, reduces, or denies.

CMS describes that match in its own outcome language. On the same EVV outcomes page, the state is expected to use EVV data to check, for each claim, that the provider in the visit record is approved for the beneficiary and matches the claim, that the visit procedure codes match the record and were approved for the beneficiary, and that the units charged do not exceed the beneficiary's approved units. CMS tracks three measures off that logic: the association of an EVV record to each paid claim, the match against approved services, providers, and units, and the share of EVV records without manual edits. Read what that means for your office. Nobody at the state is reading your notes and making a judgment call. Software is comparing fields. So when I open a denied claim with an owner, I am looking for a field fight, and the fights repeat in a short list of causes.

The six fights behind most denials

First, the units fight. The claim bills eight units and the visit record shows ninety minutes, and the state rounding rule does not forgive the gap. Second, the service code fight. The visit was captured under one service and billed under another. Third, the caregiver fight. The person who delivered the care is not the person the system expected, because the caregiver was never fully set up in the EVV system, or the visit got recorded under a supervisor, an office login, or the wrong staff profile. Fourth, the manual entry fight. A missed clock in or clock out got fixed by hand days later, and manual edits are the records aggregators flag hardest, which is why CMS counts records without manual edits as its own measure. Fifth, the authorization fight. The visit happened, but it ran past the authorized hours, and no verified visit can rescue units the plan never approved. Sixth, the timing fight. The claim went out before the visit record was verified and transmitted, so the match found nothing to match against.

The fight that causes the most of them: units

The units fight deserves its own explanation, because it produces more denials than the other five combined in the batches I review. Most state Medicaid programs bill personal care in fifteen minute units, which means a ninety minute visit is six units, not eight and not four. When the schedule is built in hours, the caregiver clocks real time, and the claim is keyed by hand or by habit, the three numbers drift apart silently until the aggregator compares them. The same drift happens with rounding. Your state sets a rounding rule for partial units, and a visit that rounds up in your scheduling software may round down in the state system. Nobody made an error of character. Three systems each did their own math, and the claim paid for the disagreement. Before you bill, your units, your visit minutes, and your state rounding rule have to be the same math, done the same way, every time.

The fix sequence, in order

Every one of these fights is fixable, and the order you fix them in matters, because fixing them in the wrong order just creates a second denial. Here is the sequence I run with owners. Step one, pull the denial by reason code and group your denials before you touch a single claim. Ten denials with the same cause are one problem, not ten. Step two, for each group, put the claim and the visit record side by side and find the exact field that disagrees: units, code, caregiver, date, location, or time. Step three, fix the record at the visit level first, inside the EVV system, using your state process for visit maintenance, and document why the edit was made. Never fix only the claim. A claim that disagrees with the visit record today will disagree again on the next audit. Step four, resubmit or adjust the claim only after the corrected visit shows as verified in the aggregator. Step five, build the pre-bill check that stops the next round: before any claim batch goes out, run the exception report, clear every unverified visit, every unit mismatch, and every visit with no matching authorization, and only then release the batch. Weekly is the right cadence for most small agencies. Daily is better when volume climbs.

Two more disciplines belong in that sequence. Work your denial backlog against the clock. Every state sets a timely filing window for corrected claims and appeals, and a fixable denial ages into an unfixable one while it sits in a pile. Sort by the deadline, not by the dollar amount, and touch the oldest first. And keep an edit log for manual visit changes: who made the edit, when, and the reason in plain words. Aggregators and auditors both treat manual edits as the soft spot in the system, and a documented reason is the difference between a corrected visit and a questioned one.

The people problem under the software problem

There is a people problem under the software problem, and software settings will not fix it. EVV lives or dies on whether your caregivers clock in and out correctly when they are tired, rushed, and standing in a client driveway with a weak signal. In my doctoral research, I surveyed 68 managers of privately owned home health agencies in the United States about what drives adoption of new systems, and the strongest predictor was learning culture, the extent to which an organization treats learning as part of the job. It explained more of the adoption picture than leadership enthusiasm did. My read of that, from the research and from watching agencies fight EVV: the agencies that win this are not the ones with the strictest memo. They are the ones that train the visit workflow until it is boring, explain to caregivers why the record matters, and treat a missed clock in as a training gap before they treat it as a discipline problem.

Two habits separate the agencies that drown in EVV denials from the ones that rarely see one. The first is same day review. A visit problem found the same evening is a two minute correction. The same problem found at billing time is a research project, and found at audit time it is a repayment conversation. The second habit is one owner of the exception list. Somebody in your office, by name, clears exceptions every day and can tell you right now how many unverified visits are sitting open. If that number lives in nobody's job, it lives in your denial rate.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every owner staring at a denial pile. Never fix only the claim and call it done. The claim is the second record, not the first. If the visit record underneath it still carries the wrong units, the wrong code, or the wrong caregiver, the corrected claim may pay once and the same mismatch is still sitting in your EVV data waiting for an audit, a revalidation review, or the next batch built the same way. Fix the visit first, let it verify, then move the claim. The order is the whole lesson.

Your next step

This week, pull your last thirty days of Medicaid denials and group them by reason code before you resubmit anything. Put one claim and its visit record side by side for the largest group and name the exact field that disagrees. That single exercise tells you whether your problem is units, codes, caregiver setup, manual edits, authorizations, or timing, and it points the fix at the pattern instead of the pile. If you want a workbook that does that side by side check across a whole batch, flagging participant, date, provider, code, units, and authorization mismatches with the fix next to each flag, that is the EVV to Claim Match Checker. Run the sequence once on your current denials and you will learn more about your operation than the denial total tells you. The pattern will point at one caregiver who needs training, one client whose authorization never matched the schedule, or one office habit that keeps producing manual edits. Fix the pattern, not just the claims, and the denials stop being weather and start being a number you control.

EVV systems, aggregators, rounding rules, visit maintenance processes, and timely filing windows vary by state and by Medicaid program. Confirm your state's current EVV rules and your aggregator's correction process before you rely on a general description.
