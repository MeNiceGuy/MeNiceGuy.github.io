How to Start a Home Care Agency in Colorado (The $2,720 License and the Manager Who Has to Qualify First)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 9, 2026

The Colorado story starts with a founder who had everything except the one thing Colorado actually sells first. She had families in the Denver suburbs asking when she could start, two experienced caregivers ready to work, and a rate sheet built on some of the strongest home care numbers in the West. What she did not have was a license, and in Colorado personal care is a licensed activity from the first client, not a paperwork step you catch up on later. When I recorded my Colorado walkthrough, I called the Class B license the barrier in this state, and I meant it as good news. The license stops casual starters cold. It also means every owner who clears it competes in a thinner field, at rates most states never see. Here is the full picture, in the order a founder actually meets it: the demand, the license itself, the fee, the manager gate most applicants trip on, and the checks and training the rule builds around every worker.

The demand: about 1.03 million seniors and the seventh highest care cost in the country

Start with why owners keep looking at Colorado. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 6,012,561 as of July 1, 2025, and 17.1 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.03 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Colorado puts median household income at $95,470 for 2020 to 2024, one of the strongest income bases in the region and the base a private pay agency sells into. That income figure replaces the $97,113 number in my video, which came from an older survey year.

The price families face explains the revenue side, and it moved the other way. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Colorado, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $94,952, down 1 percent, ranked 7th highest among the states, against a national median of $80,080. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to $41.50 an hour. My video carried the 2024 survey figures, $91,520 a year at about $44 an hour and a 10th place ranking. The current survey supersedes all three: the annual median rose, the hourly math settled at $41.50, and Colorado now ranks 7th. The competition side is the other half of the story. In my own 50 state analysis, Colorado runs about 230 Medicare certified home health agencies statewide, roughly 0.22 agencies for every 1,000 seniors. That density figure is my analysis, not a state published number, and I label it that way. Moderate competition, high rates, and a license gate in front of all of it is the Colorado shape.

The license question, answered directly: personal care takes a Class B license from CDPHE

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Colorado? Yes. To provide personal care services, you need a home care agency license from the Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment, and the non-medical license is Class B. The classes are set out in 6 CCR 1011-1 Chapter 26, Part 4.1. A Class A agency provides any skilled healthcare service and may also provide personal care. A Class B agency provides only personal care services, and a Class B licensee shall not provide any skilled healthcare service. That one sentence is the whole scope of the license: bathing, dressing, grooming, transfers, meal help, homemaker work, companionship, and protective oversight are in. Nursing, therapy, wound care, and every other skilled task are out, and taking them on anyway is not a gray area, it is practicing in the Class A lane without a Class A license.

Two neighbors of this license cause real confusion, so name them now. Services regulated by the Department of Health Care Policy and Financing, the state Medicaid agency, still sit under a Class B license unless the agency also delivers skilled care, per Part 4.1(B). Licensure and Medicaid enrollment are two separate doors held by two separate departments, and the last section of this article walks that sequence. And an operation that only refers caregivers to families for a fee, without employing or contracting the people who provide the care, is not a home care agency at all. It is a home care placement agency, which registers with the department under Part 3 of the same chapter instead of licensing, at a non-refundable annual registration fee of $939.69. Founders who plan to employ their caregivers, which is the model this article covers, are in the Class B lane.

The process: letter of intent first, application at least 90 days before you open

Colorado runs its licensing through a sequence, and the sequence starts before the application exists. Under 6 CCR 1011-1 Chapter 2, Part 2.3.1, anyone seeking a license first notifies the department by submitting a letter of intent on the department form. The department page for beginning the application process is blunt about the order: submit the letter of intent only when you have reviewed the requirements and are prepared to begin, because the department responds with access to the application in its COHFI portal. Part 2.3.2 then sets the clock founders underestimate. The complete application, with every attachment the form specifies and the non-refundable fee, shall be submitted at least ninety (90) calendar days before the anticipated startup date. Ninety days is a floor the rule sets, not a processing promise, and the same part carries the deadline in the other direction: an application may be considered abandoned if the applicant fails to complete it within twelve (12) months and fails to respond to the department.

After the application comes the survey. The department reviews the packet, inspects the agency against Chapter 26, and licenses agencies that show compliance. All licenses expire one year from the date of issuance under Part 2.1.3, so the license you earn is an annual license, renewed every year. Where an applicant has not fully conformed but is making a substantial good faith attempt, Chapter 2, Part 2.4 allows a provisional license valid for ninety (90) days, and Chapter 26, Part 4.3 adds a home care condition that matters: no provisional license shall be granted before completion of the criminal background check and finding. A provisional license is a bridge for an agency that is close, not a way to start billing while the real work waits.

The fee: $2,720.30 to file, $1,638.36 a year to renew, and a new schedule every July 1

Budget from the schedule the department actually charges, not from the numbers printed in the rule text. Chapter 26, Part 4.4 states the structure: all license fees are non-refundable, the fee total accompanies the application, and a provisional license costs fifteen percent (15 percent) of the applicable initial license fee for each provisional term. The dollar amounts live in the fee schedule the department publishes each year. The CDPHE facility fees page explains the rhythm: a new schedule is published by March 1 and takes effect on July 1 of the same year. On the current schedule, covering July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027, the initial license fee for a Class B agency is $2,720.30, and the renewal base fee is $1,638.36. Renewal carries add-ons a growing agency should know before it grows: an additional volume fee of $123.65 at 50 to 99 annual admissions and $247.30 at 100 or more, a branch fee of $247.30 per branch office, and a workstation fee of $61.83 per workstation. The practical point is timing. An application filed under one schedule is priced under that schedule, and the schedule changes every July 1, so confirm the current sheet on the facility fees page in the week you file.

The manager gate: the qualification that decides whether your application is real

Every Colorado founder I talk to asks about the fee first. The rule cares about the manager first. Under Chapter 26, Part 7.2, the agency governing body or designee appoints an HCA manager to supervise the provision of services, and that manager shall meet a set qualification. The standard route is at least twenty-one (21) years of age, a high school diploma or GED, and at least one (1) year of documented supervisory experience in the provision of personal care services. If the manager does not have that supervisory year, the rule gives exactly two substitutes: a college degree in healthcare services plus at least one (1) year of work experience in health care during the previous ten (10) year period, or a college degree in any field plus two (2) years of health care work experience in that same ten year window. There is no third route and no waiver language in the part. On top of experience, the manager must have successfully completed an eight (8) hour agency manager training course, with additional related training of twelve (12) hours required in the first year and annually thereafter, and the certificate of completion stays in the manager personnel file where a surveyor can ask for it.

The manager does not stand alone in the rule. Part 7.1 requires the governing body or designee to develop and adopt policies and procedures for the operation and administration of the agency, reviewed annually, to review operations at least annually, to run a comprehensive annual evaluation of the whole operation, and, the item home based founders miss, to provide and maintain a fixed office location that provides for consumer confidentiality and a safe working environment. Colorado expects an office, not a kitchen table with a post office box. Part 4.2(C) adds the geographic commitment: with the application, the agency submits the list of contiguous counties it plans to serve and assures adequate staffing, supervision, and consumer care across that declared area. Claim a service area you cannot actually staff and supervise, and you have built your first survey finding into your own application.

The people rules: fingerprints at the top, CAPS checks before anyone gives care

Colorado checks the owners before it checks the workers, and it checks both. Under Part 4.2(D), with the license application, or within ten (10) calendar days after a change in the owner, manager, or administrator, each owner and each manager or administrator submits a complete set of fingerprints to the Colorado Bureau of Investigation for a state and national fingerprint based criminal history record check, at that person own expense. The consequence clause is direct: no license shall be issued or renewed if the owner, applicant, or licensee has been convicted of a felony or a misdemeanor that the department determines could pose a risk to the health, safety, or welfare of home care consumers, and every owner carries an ongoing duty to report a later conviction of that kind. Run your own record before you pay a non-refundable fee on an application the department cannot grant.

For the workforce, Chapter 26, Part 5 sets two gates before employment. Before employing any individual to provide direct consumer care or services, the agency must show compliance with the Colorado Adult Protective Services Data System check, the CAPS check, under section 26-3.1-111, C.R.S., and it shall contact the Department of Regulatory Agencies to verify whether any license, registration, or certification the person holds exists and is in good standing, keeping a copy of that inquiry in the personnel file. Training then splits by role, and the split surprises founders who expect an hour count. For homemakers under Part 7.3 and personal care workers under Part 7.4, the rule sets no fixed number of initial training hours. It sets topics and proof instead: training on the listed subjects, from consumer rights and confidentiality to infection control, home safety, and the hands on activities of daily living, plus a competency evaluation that includes visual observation and a skills validation, all before the worker provides care independently. A personal care worker must also pass a proof of competency evaluation on the actual tasks, run by the manager or supervisor before assignment. Ongoing training runs on topics, at least four (4) topics every twelve (12) months for homemakers and at least six (6) topics for personal care workers, and supervision under Part 7.8 happens at a minimum of every three (3) months, in person or by telehealth, with an in person evaluation of every worker once a year. The rule trades an hour count for documented competence, which means your training files carry the weight an hour log carries in other states.

Insurance and the Medicaid door: two separate proofs, two separate departments

The application carries one financial proof founders should price early. Under Part 4.2(B), an initial or renewal application includes evidence of either liability insurance coverage or a surety bond in lieu of that coverage, maintained for the whole license period. For Class B the minimum coverage is $100,000 per occurrence and $300,000 aggregate. Get the quote before you file, because the coverage has to exist at application, not at first client.

Medicaid is the second door, and it opens after the first, not beside it. The license comes from CDPHE. Enrollment to bill Health First Colorado, the state Medicaid program, runs through the Department of Health Care Policy and Financing, and for waiver services such as In Home Support Services the departments work in sequence: the CDPHE guidance for Medicaid certification states that an IHSS applicant needs a Class A or Class B home care agency license, passes an initial CDPHE survey, and receives a certification and transmittal document that goes to HCPF as an attachment to the separate Medicaid provider enrollment application, with a separate HCPF application for each physical location. A founder who plans a private pay launch does not need HCPF on day one. A founder who plans a Medicaid launch needs both departments, in that order, and should build the timeline that way instead of discovering the sequence mid application.

What you still do today: form the business the Colorado way

The entity step is the cheapest line in this state. If you choose an LLC, Articles of Organization file online only with the Colorado Secretary of State, at a $50 filing fee on the Secretary of State fee schedule, and the entity stays current with a periodic report each year at $25. File the entity, get the EIN, then work the license order: name or hire a manager who meets Part 7.2 and finish the 8 hour course, adopt the policies Part 7.1 requires, secure the fixed office, bind the liability coverage at the Part 4.2(B) minimums, declare a service area of contiguous counties you can truly staff, and only then send the letter of intent that starts the department clock.

The practical warning

Two Colorado traps cost new owners money, and both are timing traps. The first is the one my video named plainly: do not take paying clients before you are licensed. Personal care in Colorado is licensed activity, the license takes at least the 90 day application runway the rule builds in plus survey time, and revenue that starts before the license issues is revenue earned outside the license the state requires. Budget the license period as a cost of opening, with the fee, the manager course, the insurance premium, and the office running before the first dollar comes back. The second trap is the manager. Founders recruit caregivers first and go looking for a qualifying manager last, then discover the Part 7.2 standard, one documented supervisory year or one of the two degree routes, is a small labor pool that takes time to hire. An application cannot be stronger than the manager named in it. Hire the manager first, or qualify yourself before you file, and let the caregiver recruiting run during the application window when it belongs.

Your next step

Work in license order. This week, settle the one decision everything else hangs on: who is your HCA manager, and which Part 7.2 route proves the qualification, the supervisory year, the healthcare degree route, or the any degree route. Get that person through the 8 hour agency manager training course and keep the certificate for the personnel file. Then send the letter of intent to CDPHE, file the complete application at least 90 calendar days before your planned startup date with the $2,720.30 initial fee on the current schedule, and use the window to stand up the fixed office, the insurance, the CAPS and fingerprint checks, and training files that prove competence worker by worker. Put the $41.50 an hour CareScout median next to your own cost math, not in place of it, and price from your numbers. An owner who runs that order opens into a state with about 1.03 million seniors and the seventh highest care costs in the country, behind a license barrier that keeps the casual competition out of her market.
