How to Start a Home Care Agency in Georgia (The $800 Fee and the Three Month Clock)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 30, 2026

The Georgia story usually starts with a fee that sounds too small to worry about. Someone in Atlanta, Savannah, or Augusta adds it up: $300 to process the application, plus $250 for each service the agency will offer. A standard agency offering personal care and companion or sitter services lands at $800 total. That feels manageable, and that feeling is the trap. Every dollar of it is non-refundable. Approved or denied, the state keeps the money. And the denial Georgia applicants actually get is rarely about the business idea. It is about a packet that went in incomplete, a nurse who was never lined up, or a three month clock that ran out after the license arrived.

That sequence is the moment this video was made for, and this article walks the same ground in the same order, with every number checked against a Georgia primary source today. One number has moved since the video was recorded, and it moved in the applicant's favor: the CareScout median cost of care in Georgia jumped 11 percent in the 2025 survey, which strengthens the demand case while the license gate stays exactly where it was. The short version is this: Georgia charges a modest fee, demands a complete packet, requires a registered nurse on every application, and then gives a new agency three months to prove it is real.

The demand: about 1.89 million seniors in a growing state

Start with why Georgia draws new owners. The Census Bureau puts Georgia population at 11,302,748 as of July 1, 2025, and 16.7 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.89 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Georgia puts median household income at $77,353 for 2020 to 2024, and the state population grew 5.5 percent from the 2020 estimates base to July 1, 2025. Private pay home care is bought out of household income by families who need help at home, and Georgia keeps adding both households and seniors.

The price families face explains the business case. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Georgia, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $73,216, up 11 percent from 2024. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $32 an hour, against a national median of $80,080 a year. That is a median for care, not a rate I am telling you to charge. It shows that Georgia families are already paying for help at home at a level that can carry a properly run agency, if the agency clears the license and staffs to the standard the work requires.

The license is not optional, and each office needs its own

Georgia licenses this business as a Private Home Care Provider, through the Healthcare Facility Regulation Division of the Department of Community Health. The rule is direct. Under Rule 111-8-65-.05(1), no private home care provider shall operate without a license or provisional license issued by the department, and the department restates that mandate on its Private Home Care Program page. The exemptions are narrow: services provided directly by an individual with no employees, contractors, or referral arrangements, licensed home health agencies, and personal care home staff working inside that home are among the listed exemptions in Rule 111-8-65-.07. Everyone else sending workers into homes under a company name needs this license.

Two structural rules shape the plan from day one. Each office location must be separately licensed, and no license is assignable or transferable, under Rule 111-8-65-.05(2). Budget per site, and understand that buying an existing agency does not transfer its license to you. A change of ownership runs through its own application, and all past due fees must be paid in full before a permit issues on that application.

Pick your services carefully: three categories, each one priced

A Georgia provider chooses from three service categories in Rule 111-8-65-.10(1), and the choice is a money decision because each category carries its own fee. The categories are nursing services, provided by a Georgia licensed registered nurse or licensed practical nurse; personal care tasks, meaning help with bathing, toileting, grooming, dressing, and eating, plus related support such as transfers and home management; and companion or sitter tasks, meaning transport and escort, meal preparation, and household tasks essential to cleanliness and safety. The definitions sit in Rule 111-8-65-.03.

The boundary matters as much as the list. Under Rule 111-8-65-.03(p)4, private home care services do not include physical, speech, or occupational therapy, medical nutrition therapy, medical social services, home health aide services provided by a home health agency, or proxy caregiver services. Medically related activities, such as observing and reporting changes, reminding clients to take medication, and assisting with self-administration, stop where professional services regulated under a professional licensing statute begin. Pick only the categories you can actually staff, because each one you add costs money at filing and again at renewal.

The money: $300 to process, $250 a service, nothing back

The fee schedule lives in Rule 111-8-25-.03(8). The application processing fee is $300, due upon submission. The license fee for a Private Home Care Provider is $250 for each service offered, companion or sitter, personal care, and nursing, capped at $750 a year. Companion plus personal care is $500 in license fees, so $300 processing plus $500 equals the $800 total this video is built around. All three services comes to $1,050. Fees collected are not refundable, except in extraordinary circumstances as determined by the department, and that decision is final. The application checklist says the same thing in plain terms: application fees are non-refundable.

There is no surety bond in the Private Home Care Provider rules or in that fee schedule. The only bonding rule in this program is a narrow one: under Rule 111-8-65-.03(g), an employee authorized unlimited access to a client's personal funds for home management must be bonded through the provider. Owners who budget for a large bond premium in Georgia are budgeting for a requirement this program does not impose. Renewal runs on the state fiscal calendar: for continuing licenses, ongoing licensure activity fees are due October 31 of each state fiscal year under Rule 111-8-25-.03(6), and a $150 late fee applies per instance when fees are sixty days past due. Calendar October 31 every year, and treat the sixty day mark, around December 30, as the expensive date.

The rule that surprises everyone: a registered nurse on every application

Here is the requirement that stops Georgia applicants who planned a companion only agency. The Department of Community Health states it outright in its Private Home Care Providers Application Checklist: the administrator and a registered nurse are required to open a Private Home Care Provider, and regardless of the service level provided, an RN is required for the initial application. The checklist also notes that one RN may hold the positions of administrator, owner, and certified nursing assistant at once, which is often the most practical structure for a small startup. If you do offer nursing services, Rule 111-8-65-.10(2) adds a supervision duty: the provider must employ a fully licensed Georgia registered professional nurse to supervise the nursing services and the staff providing them.

The administrator bar itself is narrower than most people expect. Under Rule 111-8-65-.09(3), the administrator needs a satisfactory fingerprint records check determination, the orientation and training the rules require, and no material false statements about qualifications. The rule sets no degree requirement and no years of experience bar for the administrator. The hard part of the Georgia application is not the administrator resume. It is having the nurse identified, licensed in Georgia, and documented before you file.

The packet: fifteen items, filed complete through GAHLES, or not at all

Applications run online through the GAHLES portal, and the checklist carries fifteen items for an initial application. The packet includes two notarized affidavits, the affidavit of personal identification and the affidavit of compliance for Private Home Care Providers; a current city or county business license in the facility name and address; evidence of Georgia Secretary of State entity registration; the electronic owner form listing every individual owner; satisfactory determination letters from the Georgia Criminal History Check System, dated within twelve months of submission, for the administrator and every individual owner with ten percent or more ownership, based on fingerprint checks; the administrator qualifications, job description, signed no misconduct statement, job duty statement, and orientation training evidence; the registered nurse current Georgia license and signed no misconduct statement; and the licensure fee payment.

The department instruction on timing is unusually blunt, and it is the sentence this whole article turns on. The checklist tells applicants not to submit unless all required documents are complete and ready for upload in the GAHLES portal, and warns that failure to provide the information at submission may result in delays and rejection of the application and forfeiture of application fees. Failure to upload requested documents results in denial. Applications are reviewed in the order received, and the department states an initial review timeframe of thirty business days from the submission date. Policies and procedures are not uploaded with the packet: the checklist requires them to be established and readily available upon request, and the affidavit of compliance attests that they exist and will be implemented if the application is approved, with unannounced inspections ahead where they will be reviewed. Build the policies before you file anyway, because the survey after approval is where the state reads them.

After approval: the three month clock

Approval brings a provisional license, and Georgia puts a clock on it that the checklist states in full. The provisional license stays active for three months, and inside that window the provider must become operational and admit at least three clients. Before the end of the three month period, the provider must email the Private Home Care Unit to request the initial survey. After the survey is successfully completed, the agency receives the permanent permit. Nobody from the state chases you for that email. It is yours to send.

Miss the window and the consequence is automatic. Failure to notify the department that the agency is ready for the initial survey within three months of the provisional license effective date results in the license being automatically voided for expiration, and reentry means a new application and a full review, with the fees paid again. Extensions are not granted without approval obtained before the three month deadline, supported by documented justification submitted ahead of that deadline. The practical sequence follows from the rule: line up referral sources, caregivers, and your first clients while the application is under review, not after the provisional license starts, because the three months are for operating and surveying, not for starting to market.

The caregivers: fingerprint checks first, forty hours of training on a split clock

Staffing carries its own Georgia requirements. Under Rule 111-8-65-.09(5)(a)1, every direct access employee must obtain a satisfactory fingerprint records check determination before serving clients, and all employees are screened for tuberculosis before starting work under the rule health screening provision. Personal care assistants can qualify through the forty hour training program route in Rule 111-8-65-.09(5)(c): a program provided by the private home care provider, run under the direction of a licensed registered professional nurse or a health care professional with commensurate education and experience, covering ambulation and transfer, bathing, toileting, grooming, dressing and eating assistance, basic first aid and CPR, special conditions care, home safety and sanitation, infection control, and medically related activities. Twenty hours must be completed before the employee serves any client, and the remaining twenty within six months of when training began. No one is assigned a task before training and a competency determination for that task.

Companions and sitters complete their own track, demonstrating competency in client needs, meal preparation, transportation and escort, housekeeping and sanitation, home safety, medical emergencies, and infection control. Every client facing employee completes orientation before client contact, covering policies and scope of services, client rights and complaint procedures, assigned duties, and how to report problems and handle medical emergencies. After the first year, each employee receives at least eight hours of job related training annually. In a market where the median family cost of care just rose 11 percent in one survey year, that training record is part of what a family is paying for, and it is the first thing a surveyor can ask to see.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give every Georgia owner. The $800 is not the real risk, because the fee is visible and modest. The real risk is sequence. An application filed without the registered nurse the checklist requires is dead on arrival. A packet filed one document short can be denied with the $300 processing fee gone. And a provisional license treated like a finish line expires in three months, voids automatically, and sends you back to a new application, a full review, and the full fees again. Every one of those failures happens before the agency does anything wrong in a client's home. File complete, with the nurse named, and have three clients ready to admit before the provisional clock starts.

Your next step

Start with the nurse and the calendar, not the portal. Identify the Georgia licensed registered nurse for the application, decide exactly which of the three service categories you will staff and offer, and build all fifteen checklist items to completion before you open GAHLES. Run the fingerprint checks so the determination letters land inside their twelve month window, hold the submission until the packet is whole, and use the thirty business day review window to line up the caregivers and first clients the provisional period will demand. An owner who files in that order pays the $800 once, on a license that survives its first three months.
