How to Start a Home Care Agency in Maine (Non-Medical Care Has Been a License Since July 1, 2024)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The Maine story starts with a founder working from an old playbook. She had cared for neighbors around Portland for years, she had a name picked out and a phone number ready, and the guide she trusted told her Maine personal care was a simple registration with a small fee. That was true once. It stopped being true on July 1, 2024, when registration became licensure under Public Law 2023, chapter 309, and Maine started licensing non-medical home care as a personal care agency. When owners come to me with a Maine plan built on the registration frame, the first job is to retire the old frame completely, because an agency that opens on it is providing services before the license issues, and the statute treats that as a civil violation.

Maine runs three separate licenses for home-based care through the Division of Licensing and Certification, called DLC, inside the Department of Health and Human Services. Non-medical home care is the personal care agency license under 22 M.R.S. section 1717 and 10-144 C.M.R. chapter 129. Skilled care in the home is the home health care services license under 22 M.R.S. chapter 419 and 10-144 C.M.R. chapter 119. Hospice is the hospice program license under 22 M.R.S. chapter 1681. The licenses are easy to merge by mistake, and no one license covers another license's services. Here is the full picture, lane by lane.

The demand: about 348,000 seniors, nearly one in four residents

Start with why owners keep looking at Maine. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 1,414,874 as of July 1, 2025, and 24.6 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 348,000 seniors, nearly one in four people in the state, one of the oldest population shares in the country. The same Census QuickFacts page for Maine puts median household income at $74,733 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into.

An older population spread across a largely rural state is the operating fact underneath that count. Care is delivered over distance, caregiver supply is thin outside the southern counties, and families start looking for help earlier because the alternative is often a long drive to a facility. The demand is not the hard part in Maine. The license queue is, and the sections below say why.

The license question, answered directly: non-medical care needs the personal care agency license

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in Maine? Yes. Under 22 M.R.S. section 1717(2-A), beginning July 1, 2024, an entity may not provide home care services for a fee without a personal care agency license issued by the Department of Health and Human Services, and 10-144 C.M.R. chapter 129 is the licensing rule. Personal care services are help with activities of daily living: mobility, transfers, dressing, eating, toileting, bathing, and hygiene, plus household tasks such as shopping, laundry, and cleaning. The license authorizes no nursing and no therapy, and a personal care agency may not advertise itself as a home health agency or advertise health services that need a licensed medical professional.

The doors beside it are specific. Skilled care in the home is the home health care services license under chapter 419. Hospice is its own program license under chapter 1681, and under section 8622(5) any hospice program except a volunteer hospice program must be Medicare certified to be eligible for licensure, so the state and federal doors open together on that track. The residential models in the generator, residential care facilities, assisted living facilities, adult family care homes, HCSS agency group homes, nursing facilities, ICF/IID settings, and the other all-homes tracks, are facility credentials under their own chapters. An assisted housing license under 22 M.R.S. chapter 1664 authorizes no in-home agency work, and no in-home agency needs it. Checked negative, printed because owners budget against it: the Maine certificate of need act defines the facilities it reaches at 22 M.R.S. section 328, and home health agencies, hospice programs, personal care agencies, and assisted housing programs are not in that definition, so no certificate of need gate sits in front of any base track in this article.

The non-medical lane: the personal care agency license, its fees, and the backlog

The fee facts for this door sit at 10-144 C.M.R. chapter 129, section 2(E). The initial application fee is $500, and no fee is refunded. Renewal is priced by the number of employees at the time of renewal: $200 for 1 to 5 employees, $500 for 6 to 10, $1,000 for 11 to 20, $1,500 for 21 to 50, and $2,000 for 51 or more. A change of agency name, site, or location carries a $10 processing fee. The first license is provisional, for at least 3 months and not more than 12, a full license runs up to 2 years, and renewal is filed at least 60 days before expiration on the frame chapter 129 prints. The application names the payment sources for services, the administrator, and the owner background check results the rule requires, and chapter 129 requires evidence of adequate general and professional liability insurance with the application.

Processing, stated honestly, is the Maine fact founders miss. Chapter 129 frames a department decision within 90 working days after receipt of the complete documentation, and the DLC personal care agencies page states that DLC is running 6 to 9 months behind typical processing and inspection timelines because of rapid growth in the number of personal care agencies. Plan the opening against the backlog statement, not the rule target. No personal care agency may provide services or accept payment for services until DLC licenses it, so the queue is not a detail, it is the launch date.

The Medicaid doors on this track are MaineCare Section 19, Home and Community Benefits for the Elderly and Adults with Disabilities, and Section 96, Private Duty Nursing and Personal Care Services, after the functional assessment the assessing services agency runs. A license does not itself enroll the agency in MaineCare: enrollment runs through the MaineCare Partner Portal, section by section. MaineCare is fee-for-service, so there are no MaineCare managed care organizations to contract with.

The skilled lane: the home health care services license under chapter 119

The moment your plan includes professional nursing or another therapeutic service in the home, you need the home health care services license under 22 M.R.S. sections 2141 to 2150 and 10-144 C.M.R. chapter 119. DLC distinguishes the state-licensed-only form, Home Health Care Services, from the state licensed and federally certified form, which DLC calls a Home Health Agency; both hold the same state license. The sequence is the license itself: a preliminary conference with DLC that chapter 119 requires at least 45 days before the initial survey, the application and fee, the policy file, and the licensure survey. The full license term may not exceed 24 months under section 2144.

The fee facts for this door, from the DLC application form revised June 2024 and the DLC memorandum of June 13, 2024: the initial application fee is $300, and the renewal application fee is $600 for the two-year renewal term that took effect July 1, 2024. Fees are nonrefundable. Hold the trap figure next to it: chapter 119 prints an annual fee of $300, which was the one-year form of this fee, and a renewal budgeted at $300 a year underfunds the two-year filing the current form prices at $600. Medicare certification layers on this license through Form CMS-855A in PECOS to National Government Services, Jurisdiction K, the Home Health and Hospice MAC for Maine, with the CY2026 institutional enrollment fee of $750, under 42 CFR Part 484. Initial Medicare enrollment is blocked under the federal moratorium extended and modified by CMS-6101-N2, effective November 12, 2026, with no calendar end date printed in the notice (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027). The hospice track carries the same federal gate under CMS-6102-N2, a $200 initial application fee for a one-year license, and a $400 renewal for a two-year license on the DLC hospice form.

Background checks: the Maine Background Check Center and the CNA registry

Every Maine personnel file in the packet carries the same screening spine: the Maine Background Check Center record for every staff member with direct access to clients, the Maine Registry of Certified Nursing Assistants and Direct Care Workers check before hire and on the cycle the applicable standards set, and OIG and SAM exclusion checks before hire and monthly. Hands-on personal care staff must be certified nursing assistants, or have completed, or be enrolled in, the department-approved 50-hour Personal Support Specialist training, with enrollment within 60 days of hire on the frame chapter 129 prints. Build the screening and training file before the first visit, not after the survey finding, because on the personal care door the file is part of what the provisional license period tests.

Electronic visit verification runs on an open model in Maine. The state offers the Sandata EVV system through Gainwell at no integration charge, and an agency may use an alternate EVV system that is certified for the Maine program and feeds the state aggregator. EVV covers MaineCare personal care services and home health care services, and in-home hospice services under MaineCare Section 43.06-7.

What you still do today: form the business the Maine way

Form the business with the Maine Secretary of State, hold the EIN and the tax registrations, and put the operating accounts and the liability insurance chapter 129 requires in place before the application goes in. Then build the systems the license will test during the provisional term: the screening file, the Personal Support Specialist training file, the service plans, and the policies for the services you will actually sell, with the skilled and medical tasks on the does not do side of the line for a personal care agency. One payer caution belongs in the formation plan: the Office of Aging and Disability Services, OADS, has temporarily paused acceptance and review of initial provider applications under MaineCare Sections 18, 20, 21, and 29, effective April 13, 2026, with no end date printed. That pause gates the disability waiver doors in the all-homes module only. It is a payer approval pause, not a licensure pause: the DLC license sequence for the base tracks is not gated by it, and no MaineCare enrollment moratorium for home health or hospice was located in the sources checked for the packet.

The practical warning

Two Maine traps cost new owners money. The first is opening on the old registration frame. Registration ended July 1, 2024, and guides that still price a $25 registration are describing a scheme that no longer exists; services delivered before the license issues are a civil violation on the statute frame, and the 6 to 9 month DLC backlog means a founder who files late opens late, with rent and payroll math that assumed otherwise. The second trap is scope drift in the other direction: a personal care agency that lets a caregiver perform a skilled task, or that markets itself with language suggesting nursing or therapy, has stepped outside chapter 129, which bars a personal care agency from advertising health services that need a licensed medical professional. In Maine the service menu is the license. Keep the menu, the marketing, and the caregiver instructions on the same side of the line.
