How to Start a Home Care Agency in South Carolina (Non-Medical Care Needs the In-Home Care Provider License)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 19, 2026

The South Carolina story starts with a founder working from a guide that was two reorganizations out of date. Her checklist named a department that no longer exists as her licensing authority, cited the non-medical regulation by its old chapter number, and treated a drug test paragraph as a general hiring rule when the regulation assigns that test to a specific person. Every one of those errors was avoidable, and every one sat in print she trusted. South Carolina abolished the old health department effective July 1, 2024, renumbered its facility regulations in 2025, and replaced the in-home care regulation in full effective May 22, 2026, so any pre-2026 summary of this license is stale by definition. The good news is that the current frame is clean once it is read from the current sources: a named license for non-medical care with published fees, a skilled license gated by a certificate of need, and a hospice license that no longer carries one. Here is the full picture, lane by lane, in the order a founder actually meets it.

The demand: about 1.18 million seniors, more than one in five residents

Start with why owners keep looking at South Carolina. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 5,570,274 as of July 1, 2025, and 21.1 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.18 million seniors, more than one in five people in the state, in one of the faster growing states in this series. The same Census QuickFacts page for South Carolina puts median household income at $69,324 for 2020 to 2024, which is the income base a private pay agency sells into, beside a large Medicaid waiver system.

Growth is the demand story and the trap at the same time. New residents arrive with no referral relationships, and the agencies that win them are the ones whose license, screening, and visit records were built before the first inquiry. In a state that licenses the non-medical door and requires the license before advertising, readiness is not a branding claim; it is the legal order of operations.

The license question, answered directly: non-medical care takes the In-Home Care Provider license

Here is the searched question this article exists to answer. Do you need a license to start a non-medical home care agency in South Carolina? Yes. Non-medical in-home care is licensed by the Department of Public Health, Bureau of Health Facilities Licensing, under the In-Home Care Providers Act, S.C. Code Title 44, Chapter 70, and Regulation 60-122. Section 44-70-20 is plain in both directions: no person may provide in-home care services without a license, and no person may advertise as an in-home care provider without one. Regulation 60-122 was replaced in full effective May 22, 2026, by State Register Document No. 5407, and the licensing authority is the Department of Public Health: the Department of Health and Environmental Control was abolished effective July 1, 2024 by 2023 Act No. 60, and any document naming it as the current authority is built on a department that has not existed for over two years.

The neighboring doors are separate. Skilled home health is licensed under Title 44, Chapter 69, behind a certificate of need. Hospice is licensed under Title 44, Chapter 71 and Regulation 60-78, in two forms inside one regulation, the Hospice Program and the Hospice Facility. The residential doors sit in their own regulations: community residential care facilities under Regulation 60-84, intermediate care facilities under Regulation 60-13, residential habilitation settings licensed under Chapter 88 of the Code of Regulations, and day care facilities for adults under Regulation 60-75. The exclusions in the Act are narrow and named: a person providing care only to family without compensation, and an individual hired directly by the person receiving care or that person's family. Neither exclusion covers a business. No one license covers another license's services.

The non-medical lane: Regulation 60-122, $1,000 to file, license before advertising

The sequence runs in filing order. First, confirm the license line: the services are non-skilled care delivered in client homes, activities of daily living, personal hygiene and personal care, meal preparation, transportation, or housekeeping, by caregivers the business employs and sends into homes. If the plan is skilled nursing or therapy, it is the Chapter 69 home health track; if the plan is hospice, it is the hospice track. Second, file the license application with the Bureau of Health Facilities Licensing before providing or advertising services, with the initial license fee of $1,000 under Regulation 60-122, Section 103. Pull the current regulation text and application form from the department on the day of filing, because the regulation was replaced in full in May 2026. The fee frame around the license is published: annual renewal is $800, the late fee is $200, an amended license is $50 per amendment, and suspended license reinstatement is $400.

Third, complete the applicant signer's criminal background check through the South Carolina Law Enforcement Division, dated within 90 days of the application, under Section 103.G. A check that ages past 90 days before filing must be redone, so sequence it late in the preparation, not early. Fourth, bind the insurance or bond coverage at the $100,000 and $300,000 levels the regulation states, confirming the current coverage wording against the post-May 2026 regulation text before binding, and keep the certificate current for renewal. Fifth, adopt the policies and train before the first client: the license issues to a business that can show the operating system, hiring, screening, drug testing, caregiver training and supervision, service agreements, client rights, and incident handling, not to a filing alone.

Background checks and drug testing: whose check, whose test

South Carolina separates two steps that summaries merge, and the separation matters. The background check at Section 103.G belongs to the application: the applicant signer completes a criminal background check through the South Carolina Law Enforcement Division, dated within 90 days of the application. The five-panel drug test at Section 103.H is also the applicant signer's test, taken within 30 days on that section's framework. Caregiver drug testing sits at a different section, Section 402, which carries the pre-employment and random testing framework for the people the agency sends into homes. Build both systems before the first hire, the signer's application record on the Section 103 frame and the caregiver testing vendor, consent forms, and personnel file slot on the Section 402 frame, and keep the two records in their own places. A file that mixes them reads to a surveyor like a file built from a summary.

The skilled lane: home health behind a certificate of need, priced by county

Skilled home health is gated before it is licensed. Under Section 44-69-75(A), a certificate of need is required before licensure, and the current State Health Plan standards require a new agency to serve the entire county and meet the projected patient volume standard, 50 or more patients in a non-rural county and 25 or more in a rural county. A continuing care retirement community serving only its own residents is exempt under Section 44-69-75(B). Without the need approval there is no license application to file. After the need gate, the license application goes to the Department of Public Health at $100 plus $50 per county served, governmental agencies exempt, and the license term is 12 months from issue under Section 44-69-30. The late fee is 25 percent of the license fee, minimum $75, and an amended license adding a county runs $50 per additional county. This article prints no chapter number for the home health licensing regulation: the department copy located for the underlying build carries pre-2025 numbering, and the current chapter cite must be confirmed against the current department publication before a filing relies on it. A guessed number is worse than an honest gap.

The hospice lane, the Medicare layer, and the Medicaid doors

Hospice runs in two forms inside Regulation 60-78: the Hospice Program, outpatient services in patients' homes, and the Hospice Facility, the inpatient form, each on its own application. The fee is $100 plus $50 per county served, and the facility form adds $10 per bed or $75, whichever is greater. No certificate of need step exists on this track: 2023 Act No. 20 removed the hospice facility need requirement, and the need text still printed at Regulation 60-78, Sections 201.C and 203, is stale text that the department's Act No. 20 guidance supersedes. Medicare certification for home health and hospice layers on the state license, on Form CMS-855A through PECOS to Palmetto GBA, Jurisdiction M, which is both the A/B MAC and the Home Health and Hospice MAC for South Carolina, with the CY2026 institutional enrollment fee of $750. Send nothing to CGS for this enrollment. The federal Medicare enrollment moratorium gates initial enrollment on the certified tracks only (the extension is a six-month period, running to about May 12, 2027), and it blocks no state license.

Medicaid runs through Healthy Connections, administered by the South Carolina Department of Health and Human Services. Enrollment is online only, the license comes first, and state enrollment places a provider in no managed care network: each of the five Healthy Connections Medicaid MCOs, Absolute Total Care, BlueChoice / Healthy Blue, Humana Healthy Horizons, Molina Healthcare of South Carolina, and Select Health / First Choice, credentials and contracts separately, and waiver services are paid fee-for-service. The current approved HCBS waiver list is seven, run through Community Long Term Care and the Office of Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities projects. Electronic visit verification is a payment condition on the Medicaid in-home doors, through AuthentiCare, with the Care Call telephonic option, linked to the Phoenix case management system. No state Medicaid enrollment moratorium and no licensure moratorium was located in the sources checked for this article.

What you still do today: form the business the South Carolina way

The license sits on top of an ordinary business build, done in the right order. Form the entity and keep the state corporate record current, get an EIN from the IRS, and stand up the operating accounts before the application goes in. Put the insurance or bond coverage the regulation requires in place at the $100,000 and $300,000 levels, adopt the Regulation 60-122 policy set, set up the signer background check inside its 90 day window, the signer drug test inside its 30 day window, and the caregiver screening and testing systems, and draft the service agreement that states what the agency does and does not do, with skilled services on the does not do side of the line. No South Carolina formation fee is printed in the sources checked for this article, so I print none: confirm the current filing fee on the day of filing.

The practical warning

South Carolina's traps are stale-source traps. Name the abolished department as the licensing authority on a filing or a policy manual and the document announces its own age. Cite a Chapter 61 regulation number in a current filing and the citation is two renumberings out of date. File a hospice facility certificate of need application because the regulation text still prints one, years after Act No. 20 removed it, and the quarter is spent waiting on a repealed clock. Sequence a home health filing as if licensure were open filing, with no need approval behind it, and there is no application to accept. Send the Medicare enrollment to the wrong MAC desk and it goes nowhere at all. Every one of these errors comes from reading a summary instead of the current text, in the one state in this series where the text changed three times in three years.
