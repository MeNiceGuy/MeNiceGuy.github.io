How to Start a Home Care Agency in Massachusetts (The $500 LLC and the License Law That Just Took Effect)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 6, 2026

The Massachusetts story starts with a founder who did everything in the wrong order without knowing it. She watched the market first, and the market was real: more than 1.4 million seniors, the highest household incomes in New England, and families paying premium rates for care at home. Then she checked the license, and there was not one. Massachusetts did not license non-medical home care agencies, so she formed her company, hired carefully, and started serving clients. When I recorded my Massachusetts walkthrough, that was still the picture, with one warning attached: the state House had passed a bill in November 2025 to build a licensing system, and the no license era had an expiration date on it even if nobody knew the day. That day is now on the calendar. The bill became law on August 6, 2026, as Chapter 180 of the Acts of 2026, and it took effect November 4, 2026. Here is the part that matters for anyone starting today: the law is real, the license is coming, and there is still no license application to file, because the licensing system itself has not been built yet. This article walks the new timeline in the order the state will run it.

The demand: about 1.42 million seniors and the richest households in the region

Start with why owners keep choosing Massachusetts. The Census Bureau puts the state population at 7,154,084 as of July 1, 2025, and 19.9 percent of residents are age 65 or older. Run the math and you get about 1.42 million seniors. The same Census QuickFacts page for Massachusetts puts median household income at $103,960 for 2020 to 2024, the figure my video used and still the highest in New England. That income base is the business case: a large senior population in a state where a meaningful share of families can carry private pay care without a public program in the middle.

The price families face explains the revenue side. In the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey release for Massachusetts, the annual median cost for a non-medical caregiver is $91,520, up 5 percent, ranked 10th among states. CareScout builds that annual figure on 44 hours of care a week for 52 weeks, which works out to about $40 an hour, the same hourly figure my video carried. One full time client in Massachusetts is a substantial annual contract, which is why the licensing work below is worth tracking month by month instead of discovering late.

What changed: the bill in my video is now the law

When the video was recorded, the honest status was this: the House had passed its version in November 2025 and the measure was still moving. The Massachusetts Senate press release from July 16, 2026 tells the next step plainly. The Senate passed its version that day, and the release states the fact the whole law rests on: Massachusetts is currently among a handful of states that do not regulate home care agencies. A conference version, House Bill 5627, passed both branches, and the Governor signed it on August 6, 2026. It is Chapter 180 of the Acts of 2026, and it took effect November 4, 2026.

So the video warning was right and the video status is out of date, and I am not going to leave the old status standing in this article. The article follows the enacted law, not the recording. What the law does is insert four new sections, 16HH through 16KK, into chapter 6A of the General Laws. Section 16II(a) carries the prohibition the whole system hangs on: no person or entity shall establish, maintain, operate or hold itself out as a home care agency, provide home care services, or use the words home care to describe its services without a home care agency license issued by the Executive Office of Health and Human Services, shortened in the act to EOHHS. What it does not do is hand you that license process today. The act orders EOHHS to build it, on clocks the act itself sets. Those clocks are the next section, and they are the most useful dates in this article.

The timeline: three clocks, and no application exists yet

Clock one is temporary licensure. Under section 5 of the act, EOHHS shall promulgate regulations for the temporary licensure of home care agencies and shall issue temporary licenses within 180 days of the effective date of the act. Count 180 days from November 4, 2026 and you land on May 3, 2027. One group goes first inside that window: EOHHS shall issue a temporary license to an applicant upon verification that the applicant is currently contracted with an aging services access point or MassHealth. If your agency already works inside the state system, your licensing moment comes early. If you are private pay only, your agency sits in the same temporary system once it opens, with no earlier door.

Clock two is the permanent system. Under section 6, EOHHS, working with the Executive Office of Aging and Independence, the Department of Public Health, and a new Home Care Oversight Advisory Council, shall adopt the rules for the licensing and conduct of home care agencies within one year of the effective date, which is November 4, 2027. The council itself has an early start date: under section 9 its members shall be named and its work shall begin within 60 days of the effective date. Clock three is the compliance date. Under section 7, EOHHS shall establish a date, not later than one year after the permanent regulations are adopted, by which entities providing home care services must be licensed. Put the three clocks together and the honest summary is this: no license is required to open today, no application form exists today, and no license fee has been published, because section 16II(b)(3) leaves application and renewal fees to be established under section 3B of chapter 7. Anyone who quotes you a Massachusetts home care license fee today is quoting a number the state has not set.

Who the license reaches: private pay included, and the five carve outs

The definition in section 16HH is wide, and founders should read it before they assume they sit outside it. A home care agency is any business, including a sole proprietorship, nonprofit organization or other entity, that does any one of three things: provides home care services to consumers in their residences for compensation; represents itself as a home care agency or uses the terms home care agency or home care in its name; or procures, offers, refers, provides or attempts to provide home care services by matching people seeking care to entities that provide it. That third prong reaches referral and matching models, not just employers of caregivers. Home care services are supportive services in the residence that let a person remain there safely and comfortably, including help with eating, toileting, dressing, bathing, transferring, ambulation and other activities of daily living, plus housekeeping, personal laundry and companionship. There is no private pay exemption in the definition and no small agency size threshold. A solo operator is named in the first three words of the definition.

Five carve outs sit inside the same definition. The act does not reach an entity operated by the federal government or the Commonwealth, an entity that limits its business to house cleaning services, an aging services access point designated under section 4B of chapter 19A, a hospice program licensed under section 57D of chapter 111, or a home health agency as defined in section 51K of chapter 111. The personal care attendant program and the MassHealth adult foster care program are also excluded from the home care services definition. If your model is skilled home health, you were never in this article: that path has always run under its own rules. If your model is non-medical care for compensation, plan on being in this one.

What the finished license will look like

The act does settle the shape of the license itself. Under section 16II(b)(1), EOHHS issues the license for a term of 3 years, renewable for like terms, to an applicant it deems responsible and suitable, and the license is not transferable or assignable. EOHHS may suspend, revoke or refuse to renew a license for cause, with an opportunity to appeal. It may also, with the Executive Office of Aging and Independence and the Department of Public Health, conduct surveys and investigations, and it must publicly post a list of licensed home care agencies on its website. For noncompliance once the system is live, section 16II(c) gives EOHHS three tools: deny an application, modify, suspend or revoke a license, or issue a fine of not more than $500 for each day of the failure or refusal to comply.

The suitability review is spelled out in section 16II(f), and it reaches owners personally. EOHHS will screen applicants against the state debarment list and the federal Office of Inspector General List of Excluded Individuals and Entities. Every individual with an ownership stake of not less than 5 percent must submit their name, contact information, the companies where they hold a stake of not less than 5 percent, any civil or criminal findings against them, and a completed background check. EOHHS also reviews whether the applicant has the financial capacity to provide ongoing services while staying compliant with labor and employment laws. A change of ownership gets its own process, with advance notice to consumers and workers and a fresh suitability review of the new owner. An owner with an undisclosed finding, or a second business they would rather not list, has a problem that surfaces at application, not at closing.

The preparation list: the eleven subjects the regulations must cover

Here is the most practical part of the act for a founder starting now. Section 16II(e)(3) lists what the permanent regulations shall include, at a minimum. That list is your build sheet, because an agency that already runs these systems will meet the regulations when they land, and an agency that waits will build them under deadline. The eleven subjects are background checks for all home care workers; consumer specific service plans and contracts; minimum coverage for workers compensation and liability insurance; a payroll process that follows state and federal labor and wage laws; annual and ongoing training and competency requirements; safe working conditions for workers; an emergency preparedness plan; quality metrics and standards with reporting mechanisms; annual reporting on quality and significant financial changes; a timely process for consumer complaints; and adequate equipment and supplies for workers.

Two of those subjects carry detail worth building to now. The background check regulation must include Massachusetts criminal background checks, state or county criminal history checks for each place outside the Commonwealth where the worker lived or worked during the previous 5 years, a review of the federal exclusion list, a review of the nurse aide registry, a review of professional certifications where applicable, and, for any worker who will transport consumers, verification of licensure, auto insurance and driving records covering not less than the previous 5 years. The service plan and contract regulation must require a detailed description of services, including anything subcontracted, a written unit rate and total cost of services inclusive of any additional fees or deposits, and agency contact information for consumer questions. The training regulation must cover, at a minimum, confidentiality and privacy rights, infection control, handling emergencies including safety and falls prevention, observing and reporting changes in a consumer, identifying and reporting suspected abuse, neglect or misappropriation of property, understanding Alzheimer disease and dementia, and employee rights under the wage and hour laws. None of that requires a regulation to start. It requires a file, a routine, and records, started while there is still time to build them calmly.

What you still do today: form the business the normal way

Until temporary licensure opens, opening in Massachusetts is a business formation exercise, not a licensing one, and it is not free. Most founders in this series form a limited liability company. In Massachusetts you file a Certificate of Organization with the Secretary of the Commonwealth, and the Mass.gov guide to starting an LLC puts the filing fee at $500. The same guide carries the recurring cost new founders miss: an LLC must file an annual report by the anniversary of its original filing, and that filing fee is also $500. Budget the anniversary fee from year one, because it arrives every year whether clients do or not. Beyond the entity, the ordinary employer load applies from the first hire, and the act makes two pieces of it permanent licensing subjects, workers compensation and liability insurance coverage and a payroll process that follows wage and hour law, so buying and running them properly now is licensing preparation, not overhead.

One existing state system deserves a straight note, because founders hear about it and misread its reach. Massachusetts already keeps a home care worker registry under section 4D of chapter 19A, but that registry covers workers employed by agencies under contract with an aging services access point under the state home care program. A private pay agency with no state contract sits outside it today. The new advisory council is charged with aligning the coming license with that registry and the nurse aide registry so agencies are not reporting the same worker three ways, which is worth watching if your agency later takes state program work.

The practical warning

The expensive mistake in Massachusetts this year is not opening without a license. Today, there is no license to get. The expensive mistakes are the two built on that fact. The first is treating no license yet as no preparation needed: the temporary system opens first for agencies contracted with an aging services access point or MassHealth, the permanent regulations must exist by November 4, 2027, and the compliance date follows within a year of them, so an agency that starts its background check files, training records, contracts, insurance, and complaint process when the application opens will be building eleven systems at once, under a deadline, while competitors who built early file clean. The second mistake is paying for certainty that does not exist: there is no application form, no published license fee, and no approved vendor list for a license the state has not started issuing, so any offer that sells you one today is selling paper, not a license. And hold the branding point for the day the requirement lands: once licensure is in force, using the words home care to describe your services without the license is itself a violation under section 16II(a), and each day of noncompliance can carry its own fine.

Your next step

Work in the order the state will check you. Form the entity first, the $500 Certificate of Organization if you choose an LLC, and calendar the $500 annual report on the filing anniversary. Then build the preparation file against the eleven subjects in section 16II(e)(3): worker background checks reaching back 5 years for out of state history and driving records, service contracts that state the unit rate and the total cost with every fee and deposit inside it, workers compensation and liability coverage, a clean payroll routine, annual training across the topics the act names, an emergency preparedness plan, and a complaint log with a written process. Put the two regulation dates on the same calendar, May 3, 2027 for temporary licensure and November 4, 2027 for the permanent regulations, and check the EOHHS website as those dates approach, because that is where the application and the licensed agency list will appear. An owner who builds in that order opens legally today and converts to licensed without a scramble.
