How to Start a Home Care Agency in 2026 (The Six Steps, in Order)

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · November 13, 2026

She had already spent the money in the wrong order. That is the version of the story I hear most often from owners who find me early, and it is also the version that is easiest to fix. The business cards were printed. A scheduling app was already billing her every month. She had told two friends they would have caregiver work soon. What she did not have was an answer to the first question in the whole business: which services was her state actually going to let her sell, and under what license, registration, or no license at all? Everything she had bought assumed an answer she had never checked. In the walkthrough video below I lay out the full path for starting a non-medical home care agency in 2026 as six steps, in order: choose your service lane and confirm what your state requires, form the business and run the money math, hire caregivers the right way, write your policies and train your team, build a referral pipeline for your first clients, and set up client operations, insurance, and a 30, 60, 90 day launch plan. This article walks the same six steps in story order, with the numbers and rules re-verified against primary sources, so you can see why the order itself is the strategy.

Watch this walkthrough on YouTube

Step one: choose your lane before you spend a dollar

There are three lanes, and your state draws the lines between them. Lane one is companion only: conversation and companionship, safety supervision, meal preparation, light housekeeping, errands, and transportation, with no hands-on personal care. Lane two is hands-on personal care: everything in lane one, plus help with bathing, dressing, grooming, and mobility. Lane three is skilled care, nursing and therapy, and that is not non-medical home care at all. You refer that work out. Most founders I advise start in lane one or lane two and stay there, and that choice decides everything downstream, because the state treats the lanes differently. The spread is wide enough to prove the point. Florida registers non-medical homemaker and companion services instead of licensing them, California licenses a home care organization for a $5,603 fee under Health and Safety Code section 1796.49, and Pennsylvania licenses a home care agency for $100 per location under 28 Pa. Code Chapter 611. Same industry, three completely different front doors, and those three figures come from the state sources I verified for the state walkthrough articles on this site. Do not advertise a single service until you know which lane your state puts you in, and get that answer from your state licensing office or its published rules, not from a forum post or a comment thread.

Step two: form the business, then run the money math

Once the lane is settled, form the business in the order the government expects. Pick your entity, usually an LLC or a corporation, and register it with your state first. The Internal Revenue Service says the same thing on its employer identification number page: if you are creating a legal entity, register it with your state before you apply for an EIN. The EIN itself is free directly from the IRS, the online application returns it immediately, and anyone charging you a fee for one is selling you a free form. Then open a business bank account that never mixes with personal money, and build two numbers before you spend anything else: a startup budget with one-time costs and monthly costs kept separate, and a six month cash flow view, so you can see the month your account would go negative before that month arrives.

Then the pricing math, because home care is an hourly business with a margin hiding inside it. The client pays an hourly rate. You pay the caregiver an hourly wage, plus payroll burden, plus small direct costs. The difference is your gross margin per hour, and everything else in the business gets paid out of that margin. Ground the wage side in a real figure: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook puts the May 2025 median pay for home health and personal care aides at $35,800 a year, $17.21 an hour, and your local market can sit well above that median. Set a minimum shift length, typically three or four hours, because scattered one hour visits look like revenue and behave like losses once drive time and scheduling gaps come out of the margin. Then divide your monthly overhead by your margin per hour. That answer is your break-even billable hours, and it is the number I tell owners to know cold before the first client calls, because it tells you exactly how much work the business has to sell every month just to stand still.

Step three: hire through a pipeline, not a hunch

Step three is hiring, and the rule here is absolute. Run every applicant through a pipeline, from applied to hired, with gates that stop the process: application, background check, references, and a red flag review. Nobody sees a client before the background check clears. No exceptions, no they seem fine, no first shift while it is pending. Then onboarding runs off a checklist, and a caregiver is client-ready only when that checklist is complete at one hundred percent: orientation done, competency checked, handbook signed and filed. In the records I review, every rushed hire becomes one of three things, a client complaint, a no-show, or a retraining project that costs more than the wait would have. The pipeline exists so you can say no early, while saying no is still cheap.

Step four: policies first, training right behind them

Step four is the paperwork that protects the work. The video counts eleven universal policies: client rights, confidentiality, abuse reporting, incident reporting, infection control, emergency preparedness, complaints, quality assurance, service plans, documentation, and caregiver conduct. Each one marks the exact spot where your state overrides the universal rule, so you always know what to verify against your own state requirements before you adopt it. Then comes training: an orientation baseline, competency checklists, and a training log that tracks every hour per caregiver. Here is the truth about policies that I give every owner I work with. A policy your staff has never been trained on is a document, not a protection. Train it, sign it, file it, in that order.

Step five: first clients come from relationships

Step five is the referral pipeline, because your first clients come from relationships, not from ads. Map the people who see seniors before you do: hospitals, senior centers, elder law attorneys, physicians, facilities, and community organizations. Then work that map on a schedule, touching every source regularly, because a referral source you forget is a referral source you lose. Two honesty rules govern this step, and I do not bend on either one. Do not promise Medicare billing you do not have, because private pay non-medical home care does not unlock Medicare billing. And do not promise Medicaid approval or insurance coverage you cannot guarantee. Implying payer approval you do not have is the fastest way to lose trust and invite scrutiny, and it is usually the first thing a family repeats to the next person who asks about your agency.

Step six: operations, insurance, and the 30, 60, 90 day launch

Step six sets up client operations from inquiry to discharge: every inquiry answered fast, a real assessment, a signed service agreement your attorney has reviewed, and documentation live from day one. On the protection side, work the insurance and bonding checklist until coverage is bound, not just quoted, with general and professional liability at a minimum. One technology question belongs in this step too, and it needs a precise answer. Federal electronic visit verification, or EVV, is a Medicaid rule: section 12006 of the 21st Century Cures Act, carried in section 1903(l) of the Social Security Act, requires states to verify Medicaid personal care and home health visits electronically, capturing the type of service, the person receiving it, the date, the location, the person providing it, and the time the service begins and ends, as the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services EVV outcomes page lays out. A private pay only agency is not under that federal mandate, but a state can still set its own visit verification expectations, and the moment you enroll with Medicaid the federal rule applies to those visits. Confirm which side of that line your agency sits on before you buy software to solve a problem you may not have yet.

Then launch on the 30, 60, 90 day plan. Days one to thirty are foundation: entity, EIN, banking, your service lane decision, your budget and pricing with real numbers, insurance quotes, policies drafted, and hiring documents ready, with nothing client-facing yet. Days thirty-one to sixty are people and readiness: the hiring pipeline live, first caregivers hired with clear background checks and full onboarding, your website live, and referral visits scheduled. Days sixty-one to ninety are first clients and rhythm: first inquiry, first assessment, first signed agreement, and first staffed shifts with backup confirmed. Stop and verify at the gate between every phase, so you never skip a compliance step to keep a date. And hold the distinction the video closes on: launch is a date, operations is a rhythm. The agencies that survive run the same weekly cadence whether the week feels busy or quiet: scheduling, payroll, quality review, referral touches, and training, every week.

The practical warning

Here is the warning I give owners who want to rearrange these steps to fit their excitement. The order is not a suggestion about style. Each step is the input for the next one. Pricing built before the lane decision prices services you may not be allowed to sell. Caregivers hired before policies exist get trained on habits you then have to untrain. Marketing done before operations exist creates inquiries your agency answers slowly, and a slow answer in this business is a lost client and a story told at the senior center. Validate before you spend: read the actual regulations, build the budget line by line, and do the math first.

Your next step

This week, do step one and nothing else. Write down the three lanes, circle the one you intend to operate in, and confirm against your state licensing office or its published rules what that lane requires: a license, a registration, or no state credential at all, plus the fee and the application clock if there is one. That single verified answer unlocks every step after it.
