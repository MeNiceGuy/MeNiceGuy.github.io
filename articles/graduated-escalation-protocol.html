The Graduated Escalation Protocol: How to Get a Stalled Case Unstuck

By Dr. Devon Boswell, DBA · October 23, 2026

When a payer, an agency, or a state office stops moving your file, this is the order of operations that gets it corrected.

Every owner in this business eventually meets the wall. A claim sits. A license renewal stalls. A care case you are responsible for has errors everyone can see and nobody will fix. The file sits with a chain of people who agree it is broken and do nothing, week after week.

Late last year I carried a case like that from the other side of the desk, as the legal advocate for a consumer in a consumer-directed care program. Her payroll did not match her timesheets. Her budget showed a negative balance nobody would explain, while her family paid caregivers out of pocket. The day before payday, a notice arrived saying her caregivers would not be paid that period, blamed on a spending plan that, by its own dates, had not started yet. Then a policy change cut her spouse, her primary caregiver, out of discussions about her own case, even after every document proving his standing had been provided and its receipt confirmed.

She has dementia. Every week the case stayed broken, her care stayed unstable.

I started in writing on December 1. The file reached the president and CEO of the administering agency in March, and she answered me personally four days later. A review of every area I raised was ordered. A new counselor was assigned. A quality assurance specialist took over the case. Every error was corrected, the corrected paperwork was executed through the agency's own signing process, and the case went back to good standing.

What follows is the sequence that did it. I call it the graduated escalation protocol, and it works the same whether the stalled file is a client's care case, your agency's enrollment, or a payer dispute sitting on your own desk.

Step 1: Establish your standing, in writing, first

My first letter did two things at once. It placed the agency on formal notice of the power of attorney, who the primary agent was, and who I was as secondary agent. And it demanded a comprehensive audit of the case: care plans, authorizations, budget worksheets, timesheets, utilization reports, internal notes. It cited the statutes that required them to honor the power of attorney and hand over the records.

That letter changed what every later conversation was. I was not a frustrated family member calling a case worker. I was a legal representative with a records demand on file. If your standing in a stalled case comes from a contract, a license, or a regulation instead, the move is the same: put it in writing, cite it, and date it, before you argue about anything else.

Step 2: Diagnose before you escalate

The audit demand was built on work I had already done: the portal records, the payroll against the timesheets, the spending plan dates. I knew the exact failure in each area before I asked anyone to fix anything.

This is the step most people skip, and skipping it is why their escalations die. A complaint tells leadership there is a problem they now have to investigate. Findings tell them what is broken, where, and since when. Leadership can act on findings in one meeting. Complaints get assigned to someone's inbox.

Step 3: When a written request goes unanswered, renew it in writing

The December audit request drew no response at all. Ten weeks of silence. So in February I sent a second request that said, in plain terms, that the first one had gone unanswered, and renewed it with the specifics attached: the payroll mismatches by period, the spending plan inaccuracies, the negative balance the supervisor had confirmed seeing but could not explain the math on.

The next day, the case worker replied that everything was being forwarded to her lead and to the program director. Silence is not a dead end if you document it. An unanswered written request, renewed once, becomes part of the record that justifies every step above it.

Step 4: Escalate in steps, with time between them

From there the ladder climbed at a deliberate pace. A written request for leadership review, asking first for the single most basic number the family had never been given: how many hours of care per week the consumer was officially authorized. Then the formal escalation to the president and CEO, with the leadership team copied.

This patience is not politeness. It is strategy. By the time the file reached the top, the record itself proved that every level below had seen it and had their chance. Nobody could say they were ambushed, and nobody at the top had to wonder whether the problem had been exaggerated, because the paper trail answered that question by itself.

Step 5: Open the oversight channel in parallel, early

The Long-Term Care Ombudsman office was copied on the leadership review in February, a month before the file reached the CEO. When she replied in March, I forwarded her the complete escalation the same day as a formal referral, asking the ombudsman to review the communication policy against participant and family advocacy rights.

People get this move wrong in both directions. They either never involve oversight at all, or they threaten it like a weapon after everything else fails. File it as a record instead, early, while the internal process is still running. Nothing was threatened in my messages. Everything was documented. A parallel record changes the permanence of the conversation without changing its tone.

Step 6: Verify the fix against the original findings

A promise to look into it is not a result. I tracked the response against the original findings until each one closed, and the closing happened on paper: the power of attorney form formally released at the end of March, the corrected case paperwork executed through the agency's signing process days later. Escalations fail at this step more than any other, because the person who escalated declares victory at the first reassuring email.

Why this belongs in your operations, not just your emergencies

This is the same method I run in my consulting work at Boswell Consulting Agency, where clients send me the symptoms in writing and I diagnose from the record and deliver written findings. The setting changes. The order of operations does not: establish standing, diagnose from the record, document the findings, escalate in writing with specific asks, keep the oversight record open, and verify the fix.

If you are an owner, you will use this on payers, on licensing offices, on referral partners, and on your own staff's stalled work. If you advocate for a family member, you will use it on the agencies holding their case. Either way, the principle is the one this case proved again: findings travel further than frustration, and a fair, documented, graduated record is very hard for any organization to wave off.
